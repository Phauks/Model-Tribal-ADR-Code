## 1. How each quotation was checked

Each source was read in full text, either the publisher's or a library's scan, a nation's own publication, or an authorized reprint. A script compared every quotation with the source's extracted text after removing punctuation and line-break hyphens, and reported the PDF page it sits on. That PDF page was then matched to the printed page number. Where the scan's text layer was poor, the page image itself was read: Cohen p. 122, Chief Joseph p. 433, Standing Bear p. 131, the Great Law pp. 30, 37 and 38-39, Porter p. 238 and Yazzie p. 175.

The "Check" column in the tables uses four levels.

| Level | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Page | Matched against a copy of the printed page, with the printed page number. Ready to cite. |
| Reprint | Matched against an authorized reprint that drops the original page numbers. Confirm the pinpoint in the original before print. |
| Quoted in | Seen only as another author quoted it. Read the original before using it (section 4). |

## 2. Before using a quotation: rights, length, voice and sensitivity

### Copyright and length

Works published in the United States before 1931 are in the public domain, as are works of the United States government (17 U.S.C. § 105). That covers Chief Joseph (1879), Standing Bear as reported by Tibbles (1879), Sarah Winnemucca Hopkins (1883), Charles Eastman (1911), Arthur C. Parker's edition of the Great Law (1916) and the 1942 *Handbook of Federal Indian Law*, which the Department of the Interior published. These may be quoted at any length.

The law review articles, the Navajo Nation's brochure and the Hopi opinion are not in the public domain, or are a nation's own law. Copyright law gives no fixed word count for a fair quotation. Courts weigh the four factors in 17 U.S.C. § 107, and even a short passage can weigh against fair use if it is the heart of the work. *Harper & Row, Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises*, 471 U.S. 539, 564-66 (1985).

Project analysis, not legal advice: a quotation of one or two sentences, credited and cited, inside commentary or scholarship is the kind of use section 107 favors. An epigraph is less clearly commentary than a quotation inside an argument, and the printed book will be sold. So the plan below is cautious.

1. Keep every quotation from a copyrighted work to 50 words or fewer, the same limit the project already uses for story excerpts. The word count is in each table. Every quotation from a copyrighted work below meets the limit (Q25 is exactly 50 words); the three longer entries (Q28, Q29 and Q41) are in the public domain.
2. Quote from the edition cited here, not from a quotation website, so that the words and the pinpoint match.
3. For epigraphs in the printed book, ask each living author (or the author's journal) for permission and for how the author wants to be identified. This is courtesy as much as law. Outreach waits for Trevor's go-ahead, so the requests belong in part 6 of the hand-pull list.
4. For the Navajo Nation's brochure and the Hopi Appellate Court's opinion, follow the project's rules for a nation's own publications and law (credit the nation and keep the removal link on the website).

### Voice and attribution

Trevor is not Native, and an epigraph can make it look as though the person quoted endorses the book. Project analysis: each quotation should run with the speaker's name, nation and role as of the date of the words (section 7), and with a full citation, never as decoration on its own. None of these speakers has reviewed this project, and the website and book should not suggest otherwise.

Two of the checked quotations speak directly to outsiders writing tribal law. Christine Zuni Cruz writes that she does not "anticipate that such work will be done by outsiders to indigenous communities ... without the necessary input of the traditional authorities of law" (Q21), and Matthew L.M. Fletcher calls it "a very sensitive subject" whether judges who are not members of a community should announce its customary law (Q25). Project analysis: these belong in the methods and manners essay as statements of the standard the project is held to, not as epigraphs that flatter it.

### Words recorded by others

Several historic leaders spoke through interpreters, and their words were written down and published by non-Native editors. Chief Joseph's 1879 article was spoken through an interpreter and published with an introduction by Bishop William H. Hare, who calls the narrative "ex parte." 128 N. Am. Rev. at 412. Standing Bear's words to John L. Webster were printed by the journalist Thomas H. Tibbles. Sarah Winnemucca Hopkins wrote her own book, which Mrs. Horace Mann (Mary Peabody Mann) edited. Credit these as the words "as published in" the cited source.

### Cultural sensitivity

The Great Law of Peace is the Haudenosaunee Confederacy's founding law and is recited in ceremony. Parker's 1916 text is one written version, made from a manuscript by Seth Newhouse (Mohawk) that Albert Cusick (Onondaga-Tuscarora) corrected, and from a second text compiled by the chiefs of the Six Nations council. Arthur C. Parker, *The Constitution of the Five Nations, or the Iroquois Book of the Great Law* 12-13 (N.Y. State Museum Bull. No. 184, 1916). The quotations below come from the Newhouse text. The Peacemaker's name is treated as sacred and "could only be spoken at certain times." Cheryl Petten, *Dekanawidah: The Peacemaker Inspired Democracy*, Windspeaker, <https://windspeaker.com/news/footprints/dekanawidah-the-peacemaker-inspired-democracy>. Project analysis: say "the Peacemaker" in the project's own text, and do not use section 1 of Parker's text (which opens with the name) as an epigraph.

The Navajo Nation's brochure prints a prayer in Navajo. It is left out here. Robert Yazzie's article refers to the Holy People and to ceremonies; the quotations below use only what he chose to publish, and none describes a ceremony.

Charles Eastman's account of Crow Dog (pp. 110-13) says Crow Dog was tried, convicted and later "acquitted." The federal record shows a different path: the Supreme Court held that the territorial court "was without jurisdiction to find or try the indictment against the prisoner" and that "the conviction and sentence are void." *Ex parte Crow Dog*, 109 U.S. 556, 572 (1883). Use Eastman's description of clan justice (Q41) as his account, and never his Crow Dog paragraph as history.

Some historic quotations use "men" and "the white man" for people in general or for settlers. Quote them as written and do not modernize them.
