# Should we search for a picture of each nation?

Date: November 23, 2026. Status: recommendation for the lead author; no search has been run.

## The question

Trevor asked whether, before any swarm of research assistants is started, a search for an individual picture of each Native nation (a government building, a courthouse, the land) would be warranted. This note weighs that search and recommends a narrower one. It is project analysis built on the [media source survey](2026-11-23-media-source-survey.md) and the project's own records, cited below.

## Recommendation

Do not run a per-nation picture search. Instead:

1. Keep each nation's own seal, emblem, flag or logo as its picture on the website. 389 of the 947 nation records already have one ([official-images.md](../../docs/official-images.md); second collection, PR #171).
2. Let nations supply their own pictures. The contribute and upload paths already accept pictures (uploads PR #198), and outreach to nations can invite one.
3. If a swarm is wanted now, point it at media about dispute resolution rather than pictures of nations: the nations whose courts or peacemaking programs publish their own videos, photos or recordings. The [peacemaking survey](../analyses/2026-11-21-peacemaking-programs.md) records peacemaking forums on 37 nations' profiles, and the [individual courts review](../reviews/2026-11-22-individual-courts.md) lists the courts found. A search of those nations' own sites and channels, with Haiku assistants and Sonnet for unclear rights, fits under ten assistants, writes a search log per nation as the project requires ([decision record 0025](../../docs/decisions/0025-search-logs.md)), and returns candidates for review only.

## Why not a picture of every nation

**Little value for what the pages are for.** A nation page exists to show how disputes are resolved there and where to go. A photo of an office building or a landscape adds decoration, not information (project analysis). The nation's own seal already identifies it, and it is the nation's own choice of how to be shown.

**Thin coverage.** Wikimedia Commons is the largest openly licensed source. It has subcategories for about 98 federally recognized nations, against 575 recognized by the Bureau of Indian Affairs, and its category for Native American government buildings holds about ten files (survey, section 4.5). The National Archives and the Library of Congress hold historical photographs filed by tribe, but these are agency photographs of councils, police and agents, not current pictures of a nation (survey, sections 4.1 and 4.2). Most nations would get no picture, or a casino or highway photo, and the style guide warns against conflating a nation with its businesses ([style guide, section 3](../../docs/style-guide.md)).

**A low yield in the closest earlier search.** The second collection of seals and logos searched for the nations that had none and wrote 589 search logs, and it added 24 images (PR #171; [session note](../sessions/2026/2026-11-22-official-images-second-pass.md)). Photos of a specific nation's buildings or land are rarer and harder to confirm than a seal on the nation's own website, so a per-nation photo search should be expected to yield less (project analysis).

**Wrong pictures are likely and hard to catch.** Nineteenth-century photographs of Native people often name the wrong person or nation. The Clements Library notes that many "contain misidentified persons," and a widely sold "Cochise" portrait shows an Isleta Pueblo farmer (survey, "Which sources already have openly licensed pictures"). Commons files carry metadata traps, such as an upload date shown as the date of a photograph taken decades earlier (survey, section 4.5). A reviewer can check a fact against its source, but often cannot tell from a photo alone that it shows the wrong building or the wrong people (project analysis). A wrong picture on a nation's page is an error about that nation.

**Sacred and restricted places.** Some nations bar or limit photography. The Hopi Cultural Preservation Office's visitor etiquette forbids "picture-taking, video recording, audio recording, sketching" in villages, and Navajo Nation parks ask visitors to "Obtain permission before taking pictures of the Navajo people" (survey, section 1.2 and "Which sources"). A landscape photo can show a sacred site without saying so. The charter keeps ceremonial and restricted material out without a community's written approval ([charter, commitment 5](../../docs/charter.md#5-cultural-knowledge-and-restricted-material)).

**Consent.** Under the CARE principles' "Authority to Control," how a nation's land, people and seat of government are shown is the nation's choice, even where a photo is lawful to reuse ([decision record 0001](../../docs/decisions/0001-adopt-care-principles.md)). Photos of council sessions and offices show identifiable people, and a Creative Commons license does not cover their privacy or publicity rights (survey, "How to read the license classes"). A seal the nation published, or a picture the nation sends, avoids these problems.

## When a per-nation picture would make sense

When a nation sends a picture or approves one, add it with the nation as the source, a credit and the usual removal link. If the project later wants pictures more widely, ask nations through outreach rather than searching for photos of them.

## Policies the survey suggests (for the lead author)

The survey raises three choices that apply whichever way this question is decided (survey, part c, item 12). The first is whether to register a Local Contexts Traditional Knowledge Notice for the project and show any Labels a community applies, which the charter already allows. The second is whether to add a site-wide notice and a per-item flag for media that "may contain images or voices of people who have passed on." The third is whether to make it a rule that no picture of a nation, other than its own seal or logo, goes up without the nation's approval.
