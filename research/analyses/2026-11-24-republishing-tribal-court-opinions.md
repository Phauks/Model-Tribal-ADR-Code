# Republishing Tribal court opinions: what the law and practice say

**Status:** research spike, written Nov. 24, 2026, in answer to Trevor's question whether any case law discusses showing Tribal court opinions on the website. No code was changed. Nothing here is legal advice. Numbers in brackets point to the Sources list in section 7, which marks what I read directly and what I took from another source. Statements marked "project analysis" are my reading of the sources, not a source's words.

## 1. Short answer

No court has decided whether Tribal court opinions or Tribal codes can be copyrighted, and no court has applied the government edicts doctrine to a Native nation's law. I found no such decision in web and law review searches, and none of the 2,368 Tribal court opinions the project holds from the Caselaw Access Project uses the word "copyright" (section 2).

The question is still open, but the arguments lean toward republishing being lawful. The doctrine rests on who the author is: a judge "vested with the authority to make and interpret the law" cannot be the author of an opinion [1]. Nothing in that reasoning depends on which government the judge serves, and the Copyright Office applies it to foreign governments as well as to federal, state and local ones [2]. It names no Tribal governments either way [2] (project analysis).

Two things cut the other way, and both matter more in practice than in court. First, at least one nation claims copyright in its opinions by statute: Penobscot law requires every publishing agreement to "[e]nsure that the Penobscot Nation retains all copyright to the tribal laws, opinions and memoranda of decision" [5]. Second, there is serious scholarly doubt that the federal Copyright Act governs creative work on Tribal lands at all, and a nation's own law may set different rules for its own materials [9][10]. A nation that objects is more likely to raise sovereignty and consent than to sue, and the libraries that publish Tribal law already work on a permission basis [6][7] (project analysis).

So the real limits on hosting full opinions are not copyright. They are:

1. **Privacy.** Many nations' codes and court rules keep juvenile and child welfare matters confidential and require initials or pseudonyms in any opinion that becomes public [5][13][14][15]. Federal law bars Native nations, states and territories from posting protection order information online when it would reveal the protected person [16].
2. **Publisher material.** Headnotes, summaries and editorial work added by a reporter can be copyrighted even when the opinion cannot [1][2]. Subscription services also bind users by contract.
3. **Each nation's consent.** The National Indian Law Library says a nation's permission to it "does not serve as implied consent to use the materials in any other way" [6].

The project's current approach (decision record 0047) fits this. It hosts only the Caselaw Access Project's release, whose headnotes were redacted and which was released without restriction, and it keeps a removal path for nations [3]. Section 6 lists what to add before hosting opinions from any other source.

## 2. Case law

### 2a. The government edicts doctrine

The Supreme Court restated the doctrine in *Georgia v. Public.Resource.Org, Inc.*, 590 U.S. 255 (2020) [1]. It traced it to *Wheaton v. Peters*, 33 U.S. (8 Pet.) 591 (1834), and *Banks v. Manchester*, 128 U.S. 244 (1888), and held that "[b]ecause judges are vested with the authority to make and interpret the law, they cannot be the 'author' of the works they prepare 'in the discharge of their judicial duties.'" [1] (slip op. at 7, quoting *Banks*, 128 U.S. at 253). The rule covers non-binding judicial works too, such as headnotes and syllabi that judges write, but not works by "court reporters" or others who "lack the authority to make or interpret the law," following *Callaghan v. Myers*, 128 U.S. 617, 647 (1888) [1] (slip op. at 7). The "animating principle behind this rule is that no one can own the law" [1] (slip op. at 7).

The Court's cases involved federal and state courts and a state legislature. The opinion never mentions Native nations or Tribal courts (I searched the slip opinion's text) [1].

The Copyright Office's Compendium refuses to register a government edict issued by "any federal, state, local, or territorial government," and "[l]ikewise ... any foreign government" [2] (§ 313.6(C)(2)). It says the doctrine "applies to binding opinions issued by any federal, state, local, or territorial court" [2]. Tribal governments are not named. The Compendium is the Office's practice manual, not law, and the Court gave an earlier version little weight [1] (slip op. at 14).

**Project analysis.** The doctrine is a reading of the word "author" in the federal Copyright Act. A Tribal judge is vested with the authority to make and interpret the law of the nation, and the opinion binds members and others subject to the court. On the Court's reasoning that judge is no more an "author" than a state judge, and the Office already extends the rule to foreign governments, which stand further from the federal system than Native nations do. That is the stronger reading, but no court has adopted it.

### 2b. Copyright and Native nations generally

The only federal decisions on the Copyright Act and Native nations that I found concern infringement suits *against* a nation or its entities, not the status of Tribal law. Reed reports that one district court held the Act's silence meant a Tribal agency could not be sued under it, *Multimedia Games, Inc. v. WLGC Acquisition Corp.*, 214 F. Supp. 2d 1131, 1135–37 (N.D. Okla. 2001), while other courts have dismissed on sovereign immunity and suggested the Act would otherwise apply, *Bassett v. Mashantucket Pequot Tribe*, 204 F.3d 343, 357–58 (2d Cir. 2000); *J.L. Ward Assocs., Inc. v. Great Plains Tribal Chairmen's Health Bd.*, 842 F. Supp. 2d 1163, 1178 (D.S.D. 2012) [9]. I took these from Reed's article and have not checked them against the reporters.

None of these cases asks whether a nation owns its own laws or opinions.

### 2c. Tribal court opinions the project holds

I searched the full text of all 2,368 Tribal court opinions from the American Tribal Law Reporter and the Navajo Reporter in the project's source library (decision record 0051). "Copyright" appears in none of them. "Public domain" appears seven times, always about land or about records already public in a discovery dispute, never about the court's own opinions. No opinion discusses the publication of opinions. Several courts publish child cases under pseudonyms, for example *In re D.A.*, 12 Am. Tribal Law 362 (Confederated Tribes of the Grand Ronde Cmty. Ct. App. 2007), which is captioned "D.A, (Pseudonym)" with the docket number given as "Confidential" (read directly).

## 3. Tribal codes and court rules

Nations that address publication do so as a matter of their own law, and they pair openness with confidentiality.

- **Penobscot Nation.** The Tribal Law and Court Opinions Publication Law makes all Penobscot laws "accessible to all tribal members and to the general public" (§ 4(A)) [5]. It lets the Tribal Council sign publishing agreements with "non-profit and commercial publishers" only if the Nation "retains all copyright to the tribal laws, opinions and memoranda of decision" (§ 4(B)(2)), and it requires free access for the Nation's officials when a publisher charges for access (§ 4(B)(4)) [5]. All appellate opinions are to be published in the reporters the Nation has agreements with, but trial court decisions are published only if the Chief Judge finds precedential value (§ 5(A)–(B)) [5]. In cases "involving children, adults in need of protection, or other confidential proceedings," no name or identifying information may appear in any opinion (§ 5(D)(1)), and judges may withhold a victim's name in other sensitive cases (§ 5(D)(2)) [5]. Read directly; the copy on the National Indian Law Library site is marked "v.060416" and has no chapter number.
- **Pawnee Nation.** In appeals from the Juvenile Division, "all opinions or other papers of the Court which may become public information shall contain only initials or other similar designations and not the names of the parties" (Law and Order Code tit. 2, § 112(a)) [13]. Read directly.
- **Poarch Band of Creek Indians.** Court files are open "except that the files and records of juvenile, Drug Court, and ethics matters shall not be open to public inspection" (Tribal Code § 3-1-9) [14]. Taken from the Reporters Committee's summary; I did not read the code itself.
- **Karuk Tribe, Mashantucket Pequot, Mohegan.** The Reporters Committee reports that their court rules limit access to records or set a presumption of open proceedings with exceptions [14]. Not read directly.

Federal law adds one hard line for governments: "A State, Indian tribe, or territory shall not make available publicly on the Internet any information regarding the registration, filing of a petition for, or issuance of a protection order ... if such publication would be likely to publicly reveal the identity or location of the party protected under such order." 18 U.S.C. § 2265(d)(3) [16]. It binds governments, not private publishers, but it is a sound benchmark for what the site should not show (project analysis).

## 4. How the publishers handle rights

- **National Indian Law Library (NARF).** Its collection policy lists "permission granted to share content with public researchers" as a test for adding a nation's laws [6]. Its Tribal Law Gateway states that a nation's permission to NILL "does not serve as implied consent to use the materials in any other way," and that reuse in another collection needs "separate permission from the Tribal Nation itself, as NILL was not granted the authority to consent on their behalf" [7]. It refuses "frequent or systematic" requests [7]. For opinions, it publishes selected ones in its Tribal Court Bulletin and relies on fee-based services such as VersusLaw, Casemaker and Westlaw for the rest [6]. The project's Nov. 23 note records that some bulletin text is vendor material posted "under an agreement with the vendor" [4]. Read directly, except the vendor point, which comes from the earlier note because that page now returns "not found."
- **Westlaw (Thomson Reuters).** In 2009 NARF and West announced an alliance under which nations submit codes, ordinances, constitutions and agreements; West annotates "[s]elect tribal law content" for Westlaw, and "[u]nannotated tribal law will be freely available through the NILL website" [8]. West gives participating nations free Westlaw access [8]. Read directly. I found no public statement of West's terms for Tribal court opinions; its subscriber agreement, not copyright, is what bars copying from Westlaw (project analysis, not checked against the agreement).
- **Indian Law Reporter.** Published by the American Indian Lawyer Training Program, it ceased with volume 40 (2013), and its full text is now available only through a HeinOnline subscription [11]. Pommersheim wrote in 1996 that Tribal opinions were "normally ... found in the Indian Law Reporter" and that efforts to get West to reprint them had failed [12] (21 Vt. L. Rev. at 36 n.93). I found no statement of how the Reporter obtained or licensed opinions.
- **Northwest Intertribal Court System.** Its appellate reporters reproduce each opinion "exactly as it was entered in each tribal court," and "[e]ach tribe is the ultimate custodian of its own records," with the court's copy controlling over the reporter's [17]. Read directly.
- **VersusLaw.** I found no public statement of its rights or permissions for Tribal opinions.
- **Caselaw Access Project (Harvard).** Released its data in 2024 "without restriction on access or use," with publisher headnotes redacted; this is the basis of decision record 0047 [3].

## 5. Commentary

No article I found applies the government edicts doctrine to Tribal law.

- Trevor Reed argues that the Copyright Act's reach onto Tribal lands is unsettled and that Tribal law should govern Tribal creativity by default where a nation has acted [9]. He notes that the Act makes federal works public domain under 17 U.S.C. § 105 while "State works may not be," but does not discuss Tribal laws or opinions [9] (67 J. Copyright Soc'y at 347 n.148). Read directly.
- Rebecca Chapman and Rebecca Plevel survey Tribal copyright laws (Ho-Chunk, Yurok) as acts of governance over cultural materials, describing it as "not a question of ownership, but of governance over the materials created within a jurisdiction" [10] (116 Law Libr. J. at 349). They do not discuss court opinions. Read directly.
- Jacob Franchek's note describes how hard Tribal law is to find, for example that the Oglala Sioux Tribe's database of laws can be used only "with permission from the Oglala Sioux Tribe," and argues that digitizing Tribal law strengthens sovereignty [18] (94 Wash. U. L. Rev. at 1027). Read directly.
- Dalindyebo Shabalala found that nine of 100 surveyed Tribal codes had provisions on intellectual property: *Intellectual Property and Native American Tribal Codes*, 51 Akron L. Rev. 1125, 1140 (2017), as cited in [9]. Not read.

## 6. What this means for the website (project analysis)

The current approach under decision record 0047 holds up. Before the site hosts opinions from any other source, or adds Tribal court opinions after 2017, these steps would close the gaps this spike found:

1. **Privacy pass on the hosted opinions.** Some reporters used pseudonyms in child cases (section 2c), but others did not. A rough keyword pass found child-related opinions captioned with full names, for example *Babby ex rel. O'Connor v. Babby*, 1 Am. Tribal Law 115 (Fort Peck Ct. App. 1997), which names a child suing through his mother. A careful review would find juvenile, child welfare, adoption and protection order opinions and show them with initials, or hold them back, as the nation's own law would require.
2. **Follow each nation's publication law.** Where a nation's law limits what may be published (Penobscot publishes trial decisions only when designated), the site should show only what the nation itself would publish.
3. **Opinion text only.** Never copy headnotes, synopses or editorial matter from Westlaw, VersusLaw, the Indian Law Reporter, NICS or NILL, and never copy from a subscription service at all.
4. **Ask for permission when a nation's opinions come from anywhere but a free release.** The Penobscot law shows what a nation expects from a publisher: retained copyright, free access for its officials and confidentiality. A short permission letter offering the same would fit.
5. **Model code.** A provision on publishing opinions, built on the Penobscot law and the Pawnee initials rule, may belong in the model code's court and records sections at Gate 2.
