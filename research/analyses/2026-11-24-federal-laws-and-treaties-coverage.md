# Federal Indian laws, treaties and executive orders: how many there are, how many we cover, and whether we can cover them all

**Status:** research spike, written Nov. 24, 2026, in answer to Trevor's question (asked beside the Supreme Court count) how many federal Indian laws and treaties exist, how many the project covers, and whether covering all of them is feasible; his follow-up added executive orders and asked what other federal sources of the relationship between the United States and Native nations we should look at. No site or content was changed. Numbers in brackets point to the Sources list in section 7, which marks what I read or counted directly and what I took from memory or inferred. Statements marked "project analysis" are my reading or estimate, not a source's words. The sister spike on Supreme Court cases covers that court; this note counts federal cases only in section 5.

## 1. Short answer

| Category | How many exist | What the project has now | Full coverage feasible? |

The short version: treaties, executive orders and both Title 25 codes are bounded, public domain and available as structured data, so the project can cover all of them. The open-ended category is session laws, because no one has counted every Indian Act Congress ever passed. The workable target there is every Act that Title 25 or the treaty and land records cite, which is about 1,000 (project analysis).

## 2. What each category means

**Ratified treaty.** An agreement between the United States and one or more Native nations, negotiated under the treaty power and ratified by the Senate between 1778 and 1868. Congress ended treaty-making in 1871 (Act of Mar. 3, 1871, ch. 120, § 1, codified at 25 U.S.C. § 71), and the Bureau of Indian Affairs counts 370 ratified treaties [1]. The National Archives' series "Indian Treaties, 1722-1869" holds 374 ratified treaties [2], because it begins with colonial-era treaties the United States later ratified or kept. The National Library of Medicine's timeline gives 367 [10]. Our research data uses the 370 whose Kappler header gives a Statutes at Large citation and no "unratified" marking [3], which matches the Bureau's count.

**Unratified treaty.** A treaty negotiated and signed but never ratified, such as the 18 California treaties of 1851-52 that the Senate rejected in secret session. The Bureau says "at least 45" [1]. The Oklahoma State University database holds 570 treaty-type documents, 200 more than the ratified 370 [3], but those include drafts, secret articles and accompanying documents, so the true number of unratified treaties is between 45 and 200 (project analysis). Deloria and DeMallie's *Documents of American Indian Diplomacy* (1999), already in our sources, is the standard collection of them.

**Agreement.** After 1871 the United States kept negotiating with Native nations, but the results were "agreements" that Congress approved by statute instead of Senate-ratified treaties (for example the 1888 Fort Peck, Fort Belknap and Blackfeet agreement, which we have as a statute record). The Oklahoma State University database has 149 agreement records [3].

**Federal Indian statute.** For this count, an Act of Congress whose subject is Native nations or Native people. It appears in two places. The United States Code, Title 25 ("Indians"), collects the general and permanent ones: the 2023 edition has 57 chapter headings and 2,396 section slots, of which 1,215 are marked repealed, transferred, omitted or renumbered (many 2018 transfers moved old sections such as § 450 to § 5301) [4], leaving about 1,180 live sections (counted directly). Indian provisions also sit outside Title 25, such as 18 U.S.C. §§ 1151-1170 (Indian country crimes), 28 U.S.C. §§ 1360 and 1362, the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act in 43 U.S.C. ch. 33, and Native Hawaiian provisions in Title 20 and 42 (project analysis; not counted). Many Indian Acts are never codified at all: settlement Acts, restoration and recognition Acts, land transfers and private Acts. Title 25's text and notes cite 601 distinct public law numbers (1957 and later) and 395 distinct earlier Acts by date and chapter, about 1,000 Acts in all, and its live sections' source credits draw on 438 of them [4] (counted directly).

**Federal Indian regulation.** Agency rules in the Code of Federal Regulations, Title 25 ("Indians"): Bureau of Indian Affairs (chapter I, 111 parts and 3,446 sections), Indian Arts and Crafts Board, National Indian Gaming Commission, Office of Navajo and Hopi Indian Relocation, the joint Bureau of Indian Affairs and Indian Health Service rules (the Indian Self-Determination contracts in part 900), the Office of the Assistant Secretary for Indian Affairs and the Office of the Special Trustee for American Indians. In all: 154 parts and 4,701 sections, current to Nov. 20, 2026 [5] (counted directly). Some Indian rules sit in other titles, such as 43 C.F.R. part 50 (Native Hawaiian relationship) and 42 C.F.R. part 136 (Indian Health Service).

**Executive order.** A presidential order. For Indian affairs there are two different groups. From 1855 to 1919 presidents set apart reservations by executive order, a practice Congress ended for new reservations in 1919 (memory; Act of June 30, 1919, 41 Stat. 3, 34); the Oklahoma State University database has 562 such records [3]. Since then, executive orders set policy, such as Executive Order 13175 (consultation, 2000) and Executive Order 14112 (Tribal funding, 2023). The Federal Register's database, which starts in 1994, finds 18 with an Indian term in the title [6] (counted directly; list in section 7).

## 3. What the project covers now

All counts were made directly from the repository on Nov. 24, 2026 (main at commit 27ed6ac1).

**Treaties.** The Nov. 23 treaty session (`research/raw/2026-11-23/treaties.json`, built into nation pages by `scripts/jurisdiction/build_treaties.py`) identified all 370 ratified treaties with their date, Statutes at Large citation and Kappler page, and drew 1,074 links from historic signatories to 150 modern nations. Today 357 distinct treaties appear on nation pages. The links are `verification: inferred`: an AI assistant matched signatory to successor from the treaty text and the nation's name, because the Oklahoma State University database removed its successor tags [3]. Separately, 27 treaties have their own source record (`kind: intergovernmental-agreement`), so they can be cited in essays and the book. The research data also lists 169 unratified or non-Kappler records it did not link.

**Statutes.** 122 records have `kind: federal-statute`. About 17 are general dispute resolution or court statutes (the Federal Arbitration Act, the Administrative Dispute Resolution Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1738 and the like) and 3 are territorial, which leaves about 100 on Indian affairs, covering roughly 85 distinct Acts once duplicates are merged (for example the Menominee Restoration Act and the Lumbee Fairness Act each have two records). Only 10 are Title 25 section records (§§ 232, 233, 1301, 1322, 1911, 2710, 5130, 5304, 5329(c) and 5331). Coverage is strongest for restoration, recognition and land claims settlement Acts, because the recognition-basis work needed them.

**Regulations.** 10 records, touching 25 C.F.R. parts 11, 83, 291, 293 and 900 (5 of 154 parts), plus 43 C.F.R. part 50 and two others.

**Executive orders.** 54 records: 51 reservation orders from 1855-1919 (built from the land-establishment research) and three modern orders that are not about Indian affairs (Executive Order 12988, a 1996 dispute resolution memorandum and Executive Order 14238). None of the 18 modern Indian-affairs orders is a record.

**Related federal material already held.** 779 Federal Register notices, almost all Indian gaming compact approvals, extensions and ordinance notices; 101 federal case records (61 Supreme Court, about 40 lower federal courts); and 1,102 Tribal-state compacts and agreements.

## 4. Can we cover them all?

Yes for treaties, agreements, executive orders, Title 25 of the Code and Title 25 of the regulations. Each is a closed list, the texts are United States government works the project may host on its own R2 copies under the standing hosting rule, and each has a structured source. A partial yes for session laws. The estimates below are project analysis, based on how long the Nov. 23 treaty session and the land-establishment work took.

**Treaties (370 ratified, 45-200 unratified, 149 agreements).** The research data already holds every ratified treaty's metadata, so creating a source record for each is a script run, not research. Texts come from three public domain sources: the Statutes at Large volumes 7 and 9-16 (Library of Congress PDFs, which answered our requests [3]), Kappler volume 2 (1904, Government Printing Office) and the National Archives' digitized originals [2]. Automation can build records, attach the Statutes at Large page range and pull page images to R2. The human work is the signatory-to-successor links, which are AI-inferred today and are the part a nation or a reviewer should check. Estimate: one thread-day for records and texts, then review time proportional to the 1,074 links. Unratified treaties need a judgment call on what counts (a draft is not a treaty), so use the Bureau's "at least 45" list and the California 18 as the core.

**United States Code, Title 25.** About 1,180 live sections. The Office of the Law Revision Counsel publishes the whole title in USLM XML and GovInfo publishes each annual edition in HTML (which I downloaded and counted [4]). A script can create one record per Act-level chapter (57) or per section (1,180), with source credits linking each section to the Acts that made it. Section-level is easy to generate but adds 1,180 records that nobody reads one by one; I recommend chapter-level records plus section anchors (project analysis). Estimate: a day, plus a yearly refresh.

**Session laws.** The roughly 1,000 Acts Title 25 cites are a closed list we can extract from the code itself, as I did for the count. Older and uncodified Acts are where the open-endedness lies: Kappler's seven volumes of laws run to 1971 [9], and the Statutes at Large are on the Library of Congress site as scanned PDFs, but there is no index of "every Indian Act". Congress.gov's API lists every law since 1973 with its title; a sample of five Congresses found 10 to 37 public laws a Congress with an Indian term in the title, some of them false positives such as post offices in Sioux Falls [8]. That suggests roughly 500-700 Indian-titled public laws since 1973 (project analysis), plus provisions inside omnibus Acts that no title search finds. The API's shared demonstration key ran out after a few calls, so a full pass needs a free api.data.gov key (kept as a Cloudflare secret, per the standing rule).

**Code of Federal Regulations, Title 25.** The eCFR API returns the whole structure and text as JSON [5]. Like Title 25 of the Code, this is a one-day import at part level (154 records) with a scheduled refresh, since regulations change often.

**Executive orders.** The 562 reservation orders are in Kappler volume 1 and the government's 1912 and 1922 compilations, all public domain, and in the Oklahoma State University database [3]. Records can be generated from the database's listing the same way the treaties were. The 18 modern orders come straight from the Federal Register API with their text [6]. Estimate: less than a day for both.

**Effort per item.** Once the scripts exist, a generated record costs seconds; the cost that does not shrink is review. If a reviewer spends about 5 minutes per treaty or order checking the text and the nation link, the 370 treaties and 562 orders together are about 75 hours of review. Title 25 code and regulations need no per-item review if imported verbatim from the official source (project analysis).

## 5. Other federal sources of the relationship

Trevor asked what else in federal legal history we should look at beyond laws, executive orders and the Supreme Court. Rough counts and a recommendation for each follow. Counts marked "inferred" are from memory or estimate and need checking before they are used anywhere public.

| Source | Rough size | Recommendation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Federal Register lists of federally recognized nations (published since 1979) | About 45 annual lists; 581 nations in our roster today | **Do now.** Cheap and central: one source record per list lets each nation page show when it first appeared. Ties to the recognition-basis parked research |
| Solicitor's opinions (Interior's legal opinions, "M-Opinions") | 1917-1974: two volumes with about 509 numbered opinions on Indian affairs [7]; 1993-present: about 120 opinions on Interior's index, roughly 60% about Indian affairs [7] | **Do next.** Public domain, high legal weight (trust land, the Indian Reorganization Act, recognition, gaming), and a closed list. The middle years (1975-1992) are not collected online and would need a library pull |
| Indian Claims Commission (1946-1978) | 370 petitions split into 617 dockets; 546 completed by the 1979 final report, with about $818 million awarded [11][12]; the decisions fill 43 published volumes (inferred) | **Do later.** One record per docket, linked to the nations, would show each nation's land claim history. The final report's docket index makes this scriptable |
| Court of Claims, Court of Federal Claims and Federal Circuit | Hundreds to low thousands of Indian cases (inferred) | Coordinate with the Supreme Court spike's thread; cover cited cases and the cases each nation was a party to, not every case |
| Other lower federal courts | Thousands of opinions (inferred); CourtListener holds them but its search needs a sign-in for automated counts | Not full coverage. Cite-driven, with reading links through CourtListener as the project already does |
| Interior Board of Indian Appeals (since 1970) and earlier Interior Decisions | Thousands of decisions (inferred) | Not full coverage. Index only the decisions on a covered regulation or a nation's own appeals |
| National Indian Gaming Commission decisions, Indian lands opinions and ordinance approvals | Hundreds (inferred); we already hold 779 gaming notices | Add Indian lands opinions and ordinance approvals to nation pages; skip enforcement minutiae |
| Federal acknowledgment decisions (Office of Federal Acknowledgment) | Several dozen final determinations (inferred) | **Do with the recognition list.** They are the source of many nations' status |
| Indian water rights settlements | About 35 enacted by Congress (inferred) | Cover; they are statutes and land-and-water history for specific nations |
| Attorney General and Office of Legal Counsel opinions | Probably a few hundred on Indian affairs across the official volumes (inferred) | Later and low priority; cover the ones courts or Solicitor's opinions rely on |
| Congressional reports and hearings (Serial Set, Senate Committee on Indian Affairs) | Many thousands (inferred) | Not as a category. Attach the committee report to each Act we cover, as its legislative history |
| Annual Reports of the Commissioner of Indian Affairs (1824-1932) and American State Papers: Indian Affairs (1789-1827) | About 110 annual reports; 2 volumes (inferred) | Library documents for historians, not item-level records |
| Presidential proclamations | 47 Indian-titled since 1994, mostly commemorative [6]; earlier reservation proclamations are mostly inside the executive order count | Cover only proclamations that change legal status |
| Self-determination contracts, self-governance compacts and funding agreements | Hundreds of agreements (inferred) | Nation-page facts, not documents, unless a nation shares its compact |

## 6. States

States can wait for a separate site, and I found no state work that has to happen now. The state-related items the project already holds should stay, because federal law creates them or they describe the nations themselves: Tribal-state gaming compacts (Indian Gaming Regulatory Act), Public Law 280 jurisdiction, and state recognition on nation pages (project analysis). One caution for later: state recognition is the only recognition some nations have, so a federal-only site will look empty for them; their pages should keep the state facts they already show.
