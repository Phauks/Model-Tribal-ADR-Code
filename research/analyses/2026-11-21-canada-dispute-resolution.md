# Dispute resolution among First Nations, Inuit and Métis in Canada: notes for a sidebar

**Status:** research notes, written Nov. 21, 2026, for a short comparative
sidebar in the book and essays. They are not a finding about any nation's law
or practice. They report what statutes, courts, governments and nations have
published, and they say where the evidence is thin. Interpretations are
labeled project analysis. The search method and its limits are in the
session log,
[2026-11-21-canada-dispute-resolution](../sessions/2026/2026-11-21-canada-dispute-resolution.md).
A sibling thread covers Native nations and Indigenous peoples in Mexico.

**Why this note exists.** The first edition covers Native nations in the
United States.[^charter-11] Many of those nations were there before the
international border, and some still live on both sides of it. Their legal
status in each country comes from that country's law, not from anything the
nations agreed among themselves. A reader who sees only the United States
model could mistake the trust relationship for the only way a settler state
has organized its dealings with Indigenous governments. Canada is the
closest contrast: same continent, many of the same peoples, a different
legal footing. (project analysis)

**Terms.** This note uses the terms Canadian sources use for peoples in
Canada: First Nations, Inuit and Métis, and "Indigenous peoples" for all
three.[^cirnac-terms] "Indian," "band" and "reserve" appear only where a
statute or case uses them as legal terms. The project's own glossary keeps
"First Nations" for nations in Canada.[^glossary-first-nations]

**What this note leaves out on purpose.** Some program pages describe
ceremonies, teachings or spiritual elements. None of that is recorded here,
even where it is public, following the charter's rule on community-held
knowledge.[^charter-5] If a later edition covers nations in Canada, the
charter also commits the project to the First Nations principles of OCAP®
(ownership, control, access and possession).[^charter-11]

## 1. The border came later: nations on both sides

**The Jay Treaty.** Article III of the 1794 treaty between Great Britain and
the United States provided for "Indians dwelling on either side of the said
Boundary Line freely to pass and repass by Land, or Inland Navigation, into
the respective Territories and Countries of the Two Parties."[^jay] Each
country now gives that promise effect, or not, through its own law:

- **United States.** The immigration chapter of the United States Code
  provides that nothing in it affects "the right of American Indians born in
  Canada to pass the borders of the United States," but limits that right to
  "persons who
  possess at least 50 per centum of blood of the American Indian
  race."[^usc-1359] A person who qualifies "shall be regarded as having been
  lawfully admitted for permanent residence."[^cfr-289-2] The treaty text has
  no blood test; the statute adds one (project analysis).
- **Canada.** The right turns on Canadian registration, not on the treaty:
  "every person registered as an Indian under the Indian Act has the right
  to enter and remain in Canada."[^irpa-19] The Supreme Court of Canada
  distinguished *Mitchell v. M.N.R.*, 2001 SCC 33, as a case in which the
  claimant asserted an Aboriginal right to cross the border.[^desautel]
  *Mitchell* itself (read Nov. 22, 2026) held that right not proven on the
  evidence, so duty was payable, and its only mention of the Jay Treaty is a
  quotation in the concurrence (source record `mitchell-v-mnr-2001`).

**Nations on both sides.** These are examples the project could source from
the nations' own pages, not a list:

- **Akwesasne (Kanien'kehá:ka, Mohawk).** The Mohawk Council of Akwesasne
  writes that "the colonial imposition of borders" now divides Akwesasne
  "between Canada and the United States — and further between the provinces
  of Ontario and Quebec and the state of New York."[^mca-community] On the
  United States side the Saint Regis Mohawk Tribe is a federally recognized
  nation in the project's dataset. In 2026 the two governments worked on the
  same case from opposite sides of the line: the Saint Regis Mohawk Tribe
  brought a challenge for Mohawk students born in Canada against a federal
  student-aid rule, and the Mohawk Council of Akwesasne filed a brief
  supporting them. The Council's notice says the federal district court for
  the Northern District of New York struck down the rule on Sept. 1, 2026,
  and that "Akwesasne and our people existed long before the international
  border divided our territory."[^mca-jay-students] The decision itself was
  not read (see section 9).
- **Haudenosaunee Confederacy.** The Onondaga Nation writes that "our right
  to pass over the US/Canadian border freely is protected by Article III of
  the 1794 Jay Treaty."[^onondaga-passports]
- **Blackfoot Confederacy (Siksikaitsitapi).** The Blackfoot Confederacy
  Tribal Council, "operational since the year 2000," serves four nations:
  Siksika, Kainaiwa (Blood Tribe) and Apatosi Piikani in Alberta, and the
  Aamskapi Pikuni, or Blackfeet Nation, "located in modern-day northwest
  Montana."[^bfc-home][^bfc-governance] The Blackfeet Nation is in the
  project's dataset.
- **Sinixt (Lakes Tribe of the Colville Confederated Tribes).** In *R. v.
  Desautel*, a citizen of the Lakes Tribe who lived in Washington State was
  charged with hunting in British Columbia. The Supreme Court of Canada held
  that "aboriginal peoples of Canada" in s. 35(1) "must mean the modern-day
  successors of Aboriginal societies that occupied Canadian territory at the
  time of European contact," so "groups whose members are neither citizens
  nor residents of Canada can be Aboriginal peoples of Canada."[^desautel]
  The Confederated Tribes of the Colville Reservation are in the project's
  dataset.
- **Tlingit.** The Teslin Tlingit Council in Yukon runs the Peacemaker Court
  described in section 4. Tlingit nations in Alaska, such as the Central
  Council of the Tlingit and Haida Indian Tribes, are in the project's
  dataset. No source was found for a cross-border Tlingit dispute
  arrangement.

Other peoples often described as divided by the border (Coast Salish,
Ktunaxa and Kootenai, Syilx and Okanagan, Gwich'in, Inuit, Passamaquoddy and
Peskotomuhkati, Mi'kmaq, Dakota and Lakota, Anishinaabe) were searched, but
no official source on cross-border arrangements was found in this session.

**Why it matters for a dispute code (project analysis).** A family dispute
at Akwesasne can involve people whose status, citizenship and courts differ
on each side of a line their nation did not draw. A model code written only
for the United States side should at least say so, and should not assume
that the parties, witnesses or elders all fall under one settler
government.

## 2. A different legal footing

**The constitution.** Canada's Constitution Act, 1982 states: "The existing
aboriginal and treaty rights of the aboriginal peoples of Canada are hereby
recognized and affirmed," and "aboriginal peoples of Canada includes the
Indian, Inuit and Métis peoples of Canada."[^const-1982] Federal power comes
from s. 91(24) of the Constitution Act, 1867: "Indians, and Lands reserved
for the Indians."[^const-1867] The Supreme Court of Canada has held that
"Non-status Indians and Métis are 'Indians' under s. 91(24) and it is the
federal government to whom they can turn."[^daniels]

**Status is statutory.** Under the Indian Act, "Indian means a person who
pursuant to this Act is registered as an Indian or is entitled to be
registered as an Indian," and a "band" is "a body of Indians" for whom
reserve land or money is held by the Crown, or one the federal cabinet
declares to be a band.[^indian-act-2] The Act separates two questions:

- *Registration (status)* is decided by a federal Registrar. A protest goes
  to the Registrar, whose decision "is final and conclusive," subject to an
  appeal to a provincial superior court.[^indian-act-14]
- *Band membership* can be taken over by the band: it may establish
  membership rules and "provide for a mechanism for reviewing decisions on
  membership."[^indian-act-10]

In the United States, by contrast, the Supreme Court has said that a
tribe's "right to define its own membership for tribal purposes has long
been recognized as central to its existence as an independent political
community."[^santa-clara]

**Councils.** The Indian Act defines the band council by one of four
routes, the last being "the council chosen according to the custom of the
band."[^indian-act-2] Band councils make by-laws "not inconsistent with this
Act or with any regulation."[^indian-act-81]

**Honour of the Crown and fiduciary duty.** In the United States, the
Department of the Interior describes the trust responsibility as a legal
obligation under which the United States "has charged itself with moral
obligations of the highest responsibility and trust" toward Indian tribes,
quoting *Seminole Nation v. United States* (1942).[^bia-trust] Canada's
doctrine is organized differently. "The honour of the Crown is always at
stake in its dealings with Aboriginal peoples," and it grounds the duty to
consult.[^haida] A fiduciary duty arises only "[w]here the Crown has assumed
discretionary control over specific Aboriginal interests," and the Court
added that "the term 'fiduciary duty' does not connote a universal trust
relationship encompassing all aspects of the relationship between the Crown
and Aboriginal peoples."[^haida] The honour of the Crown is "engaged by
constitutional obligations to Aboriginal groups."[^mmf]

**Self-definition and negotiation.** The *Desautel* majority wrote that the
authoritative interpretation of s. 35(1) is for the courts, but "[i]t is for
Aboriginal peoples, however, to define themselves and to choose by what
means to make their decisions, according to their own laws, customs and
practices." It added that "[n]egotiation has significant advantages for both
the Crown and Aboriginal peoples as a way to obtain clarity about Aboriginal
rights."[^desautel]

**The United Nations Declaration.** Canada's 2021 Act has the purpose to
"affirm the Declaration as a universal international human rights
instrument with application in Canadian law."[^undrip-act]

| --- | --- | --- |
| Constitutional anchor | Congress's power to regulate commerce "with the Indian Tribes" (U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 3); no clause recognizing Native nations' rights (project analysis) | s. 35(1) recognizes and affirms existing Aboriginal and treaty rights; s. 91(24) federal power[^const-1982][^const-1867] |
| Who is covered | Federally recognized Tribes; federal Indian classifications treated as political, not racial[^mancari] | First Nations, Inuit and Métis; Métis and non-status Indians are "Indians" under s. 91(24)[^const-1982][^daniels] |

research; the table as a whole is project analysis.

## 3. Criminal justice: Gladue, circles and the 2026 restorative justice part

**Sentencing.** The Criminal Code directs that "all available sanctions,
other than imprisonment, that are reasonable in the circumstances and
consistent with the harm done to victims or to the community should be
considered for all offenders, with particular attention to the
circumstances of Aboriginal offenders."[^cc-718] In *R. v. Ipeelee* the
Court restated *R. v. Gladue*, [1999] 1 S.C.R. 688: the provision is
remedial, and judges must consider the systemic and background factors
that brought the person before the court and the sentencing procedures and
sanctions suited to the person's Aboriginal heritage or
connection.[^ipeelee] It added that "courts must take judicial notice of
such matters as the history of colonialism, displacement, and residential
schools," and that case-specific information "is often brought before the
court by way of a Gladue report."[^ipeelee] *Gladue* itself was not read for
this note.

**The 2026 restorative justice part.** In 2026 Parliament repealed the old
alternative measures section (s. 717) and added Part XXII.2, "Alternative
Measures and Restorative Justice Processes."[^cc-717][^cc-part-22-2] It
defines restorative justice as "an approach to justice that seeks to repair
the harm caused by an offence to individuals and their
relationships."[^cc-part-22-2] Several features bear directly on a model
code:

- Measures under the part must give "particular attention to the needs of
  Aboriginal persons and Black persons" (s. 715.46(d)).[^cc-part-22-2]
- A restorative process may take the form of "a mediation or dialogue
  between the victim and the person alleged to have committed an offence or
  the offender, a restorative conference or a sentencing circle," at "any
  stage of a criminal justice process" (s. 715.54).[^cc-part-22-2]
- Participation is voluntary, and participants "may freely withdraw from
  the restorative justice process at any time" (s. 715.53(b)).[^cc-part-22-2]
- Alternative measures require that the person "accepts responsibility" and
  is told of the right to counsel, and they may not be used if the person
  denies involvement or asks for court (ss. 715.49-715.5).[^cc-part-22-2]
- A judge or other authorized person may convene a conference to
  "facilitate the use of alternative measures or restorative justice
  processes" (s. 715.55).[^cc-part-22-2]

The consolidated text read is current to Oct. 29, 2026; the coming-into-force
date of S.C. 2026, c. 19 was not checked.

**The Indigenous Justice Program.** The federal program is "cost-shared with
all thirteen provinces and territories and delivered in partnership with
Indigenous communities," and funds programming including "prevention,
diversion, civil and family mediation, reintegration, and supporting
individuals on bail and probation."[^ijp-eval] Its 2021 evaluation reports
that "[a]n estimated 90% of clients successfully complete the programming,"
but also that "[a] planned recidivism study and comparative costing analysis
could not be completed in the evalution [sic] timeframe."[^ijp-eval] The
completion figure is not evidence of reduced reoffending (project analysis).
This matches the peacemaking track's finding that outcome studies are
rare.[^peacemaking-note]

**Truth and Reconciliation Commission.** Call to Action 42 asks governments
"to commit to the recognition and implementation of Aboriginal justice
systems in a manner consistent with the Treaty and Aboriginal rights of
Aboriginal peoples," the Constitution Act, 1982 and the United Nations
Declaration.[^trc-42] Call 50 asks the federal government to "fund the
establishment of Indigenous law institutes for the development, use, and
understanding of Indigenous laws and access to justice."[^trc-50] A
Department of Justice report calls the Calls to Action "the most recent
mainstream support for Indigenous law in Canada."[^doj-rj-ilt]

| Teslin Tlingit Council, Peacemaker Court | Administration of Justice Agreement in effect June 30, 2011, under s. 13.3.17 of the 1995 Self-Government Agreement[^teslin-fact-sheet] | "all matters arising under Teslin Tlingit law," such as adoption, wills, settlement land and wildlife; no criminal law jurisdiction; appeals to the Supreme Court of Yukon[^teslin-fact-sheet] | Stage One is "a consent-based dispute resolution process"; Stage Two is adjudication and appeal[^teslin-fact-sheet] |

**Teslin Tlingit in more detail.** The federal fact sheet says the
Peacemaker Court will operate "in accordance with principles of judicial
independence, impartiality and fairness," that the Canadian Charter of
Rights and Freedoms applies to it, and that Stage Two would be suspended for
about four years so that Teslin Tlingit peacemakers could develop the skills
to take it on.[^teslin-fact-sheet] The fact sheet is dated July 2011. In
February 2025 the governments signed a funding amendment for a land-based
healing camp within the nation's corrections system, and CBC reported that
the 2011 agreement supports restorative measures rooted in Teslin Tlingit
culture, "known as Haa Ḵusteeyí (Our Way)."[^cbc-teslin] Whether Stage Two
has started was not confirmed.

In the project's view, the Teslin design (consent-based first, adjudication
second, with a phased start while peacemakers train) and the Akwesasne
court's mix of voluntary and court-ordered mediation are the closest
Canadian parallels to the peacemaking courts the project has already
recorded in the United States.[^peacemaking-note]

## 5. Disputes between nations and the Crown

The comparative essay already covers three Canadian instruments: the
Specific Claims Tribunal Act, the Framework Agreement on First Nation Land
Management and chapter 19 of the Nisg̱a'a Final Agreement.[^essay-5-4] This
section adds the rest of the picture.

**Modern treaties use stages.** A federal guide states that "all modern
treaties now include some form of dispute resolution process" and that
"[s]ince 1999, agreements have generally included a staged approach to
resolving implementation-related disputes": "(a) informal discussions; (b)
assisted or facilitated negotiations, including mediation; and (c)
arbitration."[^cirnac-modern-treaty-guide] The guide says it does not
interpret any treaty and states only the Government of Canada's
view.[^cirnac-modern-treaty-guide]

**Treaty negotiation has a neutral facilitator in British Columbia.** One of
the BC Treaty Commission's three main roles is "to be the independent
facilitator of negotiations amongst First Nations in BC, the Government of
Canada, and the Government of British Columbia."[^bctc]

**Residential schools: a mass claims process.** The Independent Assessment
Process under the Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement received
38,257 applications and had approved $3.180 billion in payments by Mar. 31,
2019; of 38,178 claims resolved, 26,693 were decided after a hearing and
4,165 by negotiated settlement. The figures "include continuing Alternative
Dispute Resolution (ADR) claims," from the federal process that came
before the settlement.[^cirnac-iap] These are interim figures; the final
report was not reached (section 9).

**The Federal Court favors dialogue.** The Court's practice guidelines,
developed with Elders, record "their preference for dispute resolution
through dialogue: talking things out to resolve disputes by agreement." The
Court "will typically encourage parties to reach a settlement or narrow
their issues in dispute through agreement," but "it is ultimately for the
parties to decide."[^fc-guidelines] Under the Court's rules, discussions in
a dispute resolution conference "are confidential and shall not be
disclosed."[^fc-guidelines] The guidelines also provide for a neutral
advisor to the Court on Indigenous law.[^fc-guidelines]

**Child and family services.** The 2019 federal Act affirming First
Nations, Inuit and Métis jurisdiction over child and family services
provides that the authority to administer and enforce Indigenous laws
"includes the authority to provide for dispute resolution
mechanisms."[^c92] A dispute resolution mechanism set by regulation "may be
used to promote entering into a coordination agreement."[^c92]

## 6. Disputes inside First Nation governance

**Elections.** For First Nations under the First Nations Elections Act, an
election may be contested "only in accordance with sections 31 to 35," by
application to a court, within 30 days of the results.[^fnea] Most other
nations hold elections under custom codes. Reviewing a custom election
dispute, the Federal Court wrote in 2025 that "applicants who seek to
challenge an election code or to contest election results are required to
exhaust all internal recourses available to them and that court proceedings
are appropriate only as a last resort," because "[r]espect for
self-government necessitates ensuring that governance disputes are first
dealt with by Indigenous decision-making processes."[^rabbitskin]

**Membership.** As noted in section 2, a band that takes control of its
membership may set up its own review mechanism.[^indian-act-10]

In the project's view, the Federal Court's rule that internal remedies come
first is the Canadian counterpart of the United States tribal-exhaustion
doctrine,[^national-farmers-union] and it rewards nations that build an
internal appeal body.

## 7. What the comparison suggests (project analysis)

These are questions for the scope and outline, not provisions.

1. **The border.** Should the model code's commentary say that a nation's
   relatives, elders or courts may sit across an international border, and
   should a forum be able to recognize participants and agreements from a
   related nation on the other side? (Akwesasne, Blackfoot Confederacy,
   *Desautel*.)
2. **Stages.** Canadian modern treaties and the Teslin Tlingit court both
   put consent-based steps before adjudication. The project's comparative
   essay found the same pattern in the Nisg̱a'a chapter.
3. **Voluntariness and withdrawal.** Criminal Code s. 715.53(b) lets any
   participant withdraw at any time. The peacemaking note found nations that
   differ on this (Isleta requires attendance once referred).
4. **Internal remedies first.** The Federal Court's exhaustion rule rewards a
   working internal appeal body for election and membership disputes.
5. **Confidentiality.** Federal Court dispute resolution conferences are
   confidential by rule.
6. **Evidence.** Canada's main federal program has not completed a
   recidivism study. Claims about outcomes should stay modest.

## 8. Draft sidebar text

For review; about 230 words. Citations as in the sections above.

> **Beyond the border.** Many Native nations were here before the line
> between the United States and Canada, and some still live on both sides
> of it. Akwesasne is divided between New York, Ontario and Quebec. The
> Blackfoot Confederacy joins three nations in Alberta with the Blackfeet
> Nation in Montana. The Jay Treaty of 1794 promised free passage to
> "Indians dwelling on either side," but each country now decides who
> benefits: the United States by a blood-quantum statute, Canada by
> registration under its Indian Act.
>
> The legal status of a nation is a construct of each settler state's law.
> In the United States it rests on a general trust responsibility. In
> Canada it rests on section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982, and on the
> honour of the Crown, and the Supreme Court of Canada has said the Crown's
> fiduciary duty is not "a universal trust relationship." Canada's highest
> court has also held that a people living in Washington State can hold
> constitutional rights in British Columbia.
>
> Dispute resolution in Canada shows familiar patterns. The Teslin Tlingit
> Council's Peacemaker Court begins with a consent-based stage and moves to
> adjudication only after. Modern treaties move from talks to mediation to
> arbitration. A 2026 amendment to the Criminal Code names sentencing
> circles and lets participants withdraw at any time. The Federal Court
> asks people to use their nation's own processes before coming to court.
> This book does not cover nations in Canada, but a code for one side of
> the border should remember the other.
