# Dispute resolution among Indigenous peoples in Mexico: notes for a comparative sidebar

**Status:** research notes, written Nov. 21, 2026, for a short comparative sidebar in the book. They are not a finding about any people's or community's law or practice. They report what Mexican federal and state law, Mexican courts and a few named secondary sources say, and they say where the evidence is thin. Interpretations are labeled project analysis. English renderings of Spanish quotations are the project's own translations unless a source gives one. The search method and its limits are in the session log, [2026-11-21-mexico-indigenous-dispute-resolution](../sessions/2026/2026-11-21-mexico-indigenous-dispute-resolution.md). A separate note covers Canada.

**Why this note exists.** The lead author asked for this research because Native nations do not ascribe to the international border, and because the legal status of Native nations in the United States is largely a construct of their trust relationship with the federal government (lead author's framing, Nov. 21, 2026). Mexico is a useful contrast: several peoples live on both sides of the border, but on the Mexican side their law is recognized through a different constitutional route, with no treaties and no trust relationship (project analysis, sections 1 and 4).

**What this note leaves out on purpose.** Some sources describe ceremonies, pilgrimages or spiritual practice. None of that is recorded here, following the charter's rule on community-held knowledge.[^charter-5] Nothing here is a draft provision; the roadmap holds drafting until Gates 1 and 2. If a later edition covers Mexico, the charter says the project would follow the comparable data principles of the nations concerned.[^charter-11]

## 1. Two different legal footings: Mexico and the United States

**Mexico.** The Constitution states that the Nation "tiene una composición pluricultural y multiétnica sustentada originalmente en sus pueblos indígenas" (has a pluricultural and multiethnic makeup originally grounded in its Indigenous peoples), and that self-determination "se ejercerá en un marco constitucional de autonomía que asegure la unidad nacional" (will be exercised within a constitutional framework of autonomy that ensures national unity).[^cpeum-2] Since the reform of Sept. 30, 2024, Indigenous peoples and communities are recognized "como sujetos de derecho público con personalidad jurídica y patrimonio propio" (as subjects of public law with their own legal personality and assets).[^cpeum-2][^dof-2024] Recognition began in 1992, when article 4 first described the Nation's "composición pluricultural" and required that Indigenous "prácticas y costumbres jurídicas" be taken into account in agrarian proceedings.[^dof-1992] The 2001 reform moved the subject to article 2 and added the right to apply "sus propios sistemas normativos en la regulación y solución de sus conflictos internos."[^dof-2001]

Land is held mainly through the agrarian system. Article 27 recognizes "la personalidad jurídica de los núcleos de población ejidales y comunales" and says "La ley protegerá la integridad de las tierras de los grupos indígenas"; boundary and tenure disputes between ejidos and communities are federal and go to agrarian tribunals.[^cpeum-27] A federal government monograph on the O'odham (Tohono O'odham) contrasts the two countries this way: in the United States, a reservation "con una constitución propia, pero limitada por la de ese país" (with its own constitution, but limited by that country's); in Mexico, "la existencia de comunidades y ejidos, producto de las reformas y políticas agrarias" (communities and ejidos, the product of agrarian reforms and policies).[^cdi-papagos]

**United States.** The Supreme Court described Native nations as "domestic dependent nations" in 1831,[^cherokee] and later rested broad federal power on the nations' dependence on the United States.[^kagama] Nations have their own governments and courts, and their relationship with the United States runs through treaties, statutes and the trust responsibility, as the project's key cases digest explains.

**Comparison (project analysis).**

| Question | Mexico | United States |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Source of recognition | Federal Constitution art. 2 and state constitutions and laws[^cpeum-2] | Treaties, federal statutes, federal acknowledgment and case law[^cherokee][^kagama] |
| Who holds the right | "pueblos y comunidades indígenas," identified by self-identification and ethnolinguistic and settlement criteria[^cpeum-2] | Federally recognized nations as governments |
| Status | Subjects of public law with legal personality, within "un marco constitucional de autonomía que asegure la unidad nacional"[^cpeum-2] | Domestic dependent nations with retained sovereignty[^cherokee] |
| Land | Ejidos and comunidades under article 27 and the agrarian laws[^cpeum-27] | Trust and fee lands, reservations |
| Own dispute resolution | Express constitutional right, subject to validation by state courts "en los casos y procedimientos" set by law[^cpeum-2] | Inherent; tribal courts and peacemaking are tribal institutions |
| Main external check | Human rights, the Constitution's general principles and "la dignidad e integridad de las mujeres"[^cpeum-2] | Indian Civil Rights Act, enforced mainly in tribal forums[^santa-clara] |

In the project's view, the result is that a Mexican Indigenous community's dispute process is recognized as a right inside the national legal order and then checked by state courts, while a U.S. nation's process belongs to a separate sovereign, whose decisions other courts must decide whether to recognize. This is a difference of footing, not of quality.

## 2. The federal framework in Mexico

**Article 2, apartado A, fracción II.** Peoples and communities may "Aplicar y desarrollar sus propios sistemas normativos en la regulación y solución de sus conflictos internos, sujetándose a los principios generales de esta Constitución, respetando las garantías individuales, los derechos humanos y, de manera relevante, la dignidad e integridad de las mujeres. La ley establecerá los casos y procedimientos de validación por los jueces o tribunales correspondientes."[^cpeum-2] The 2024 reform added: "La jurisdicción indígena se ejercerá por las autoridades comunitarias de acuerdo con los sistemas normativos de los pueblos y comunidades indígenas, dentro del marco del orden jurídico vigente."[^cpeum-2][^dof-2024] (Indigenous jurisdiction is exercised by community authorities under the peoples' own normative systems, within the existing legal order.)

**Access to state courts.** Fracción XI guarantees full access to the State's courts; in every proceeding "se deberán tomar en cuenta sus sistemas normativos y especificidades culturales," with interpreters, translators, defenders and experts "especializadas en derechos indígenas, pluralismo jurídico, perspectiva de género, y diversidad cultural y lingüística."[^cpeum-2]

**Consultation.** Fracción XIII, added in 2024, requires consultation on legislative or administrative measures that may cause significant impacts, "con la finalidad de obtener su consentimiento o, en su caso, llegar a un acuerdo," and makes the peoples and communities "los únicos legitimados" to challenge a failure to consult.[^cpeum-2] Consultation has itself become a dispute-resolution arena (see the Yaqui and Cherán cases below).

**A general law is still pending.** Apartado D says "La ley general debe establecer las normas y mecanismos" to implement these rights.[^cpeum-2] A June 29, 2026 press report says the federal government planned to consult more than 16,000 communities on a draft general law before sending it to Congress.[^zeta-2026] The project did not confirm the draft's text or status.

**Criminal cases: article 420 of the national criminal procedure code.** Where a crime affects legal interests of an Indigenous people or community or the personal interests of a member, and both the accused and the victim (or the victim's family) "acepten el modo en el que la comunidad, conforme a sus propios sistemas normativos ... proponga resolver el conflicto," the criminal action is extinguished. The exceptions are solutions that do not consider "la perspectiva de género," affect "la dignidad de las personas, el interés superior de los niños y las niñas" or the right to a life free of violence against women; crimes that carry mandatory pretrial detention are excluded.[^cnpp-420]

**The 2024 general law on alternative mechanisms.** The Ley General de Mecanismos Alternativos de Solución de Controversias (Jan. 26, 2024) lists negotiation, collaborative negotiation, mediation, conciliation and arbitration "de manera enunciativa y no limitativa."[^lgmasc] For Indigenous participants it defers to article 2: facilitators who belong to Indigenous or Afro-Mexican peoples are governed by article 2 and state law (art. 41), and when a party identifies as Indigenous or Afro-Mexican "se estará a sus usos y costumbres de conformidad con la libre autodeterminación y autonomía" (art. 60).[^lgmasc] In the project's view, article 60 is the closest Mexican analogue to a model ADR code clause that makes a nation's own norms govern a mediation.

**Agrarian disputes.** Agrarian tribunals must consider the "usos y costumbres" of Indigenous parties "mientras no contravengan" the Constitution and the Agrarian Law (art. 164(I)), accept filings in Indigenous languages without translation (art. 164(II)), and, before judgment, "exhortará a las partes a una composición amigable," a settlement that, once approved, "tendrá el carácter de sentencia ejecutoriada" (art. 185(VI)).[^ley-agraria] The Procuraduría Agraria is charged to "Promover y procurar la conciliación de intereses" (art. 136(III)).[^ley-agraria] Much Indigenous land conflict in Mexico therefore passes through a federal conciliation-then-adjudication system rather than a community forum (project analysis).

**The federal Indigenous institute.** The INPI must support access to state courts so that "se tomen en cuenta sus sistemas normativos ... en el marco del pluralismo jurídico" (art. 4(XIII)), and recognizes "la Asamblea General Comunitaria como la máxima autoridad en la toma de decisiones" (art. 7).[^linpi]

**Treaty law.** Mexico ratified ILO Convention 169 in 1990, according to the Supreme Court's 2022 protocol.[^protocolo] The Supreme Court treats the Convention as part of the constitutional standard of review.[^cheran]

## 3. How Mexican courts read it

**The 2022 Supreme Court protocol.** The Supreme Court's *Protocolo para juzgar con perspectiva intercultural* (Nov. 2022) is a judges' guide, not a binding rule, but it collects the Court's holdings.[^protocolo] It reports that the Court has described Indigenous jurisdiction as covering "conocer, juzgar, resolver conflictos, definir derechos y obligaciones concretas, ordenar restricciones de derechos ... ordenar las prestaciones de servicios a la comunidad, la reparación de daños y perjuicios, y la disposición de bienes," citing Amparo Directo 6/2018.[^protocolo] It sets out a test for whether a case belongs to state courts or to Indigenous jurisdiction, built on "Factores personal, territorial, objetivo e institucional," and three principles: the greatest autonomy for communities that keep their own norms; human rights as "el mínimo obligatorio"; and "maximización o de mínimas restricciones de la autonomía indígena."[^protocolo] When the case belongs to Indigenous jurisdiction, "lo conducente sería declinar la competencia en favor de la jurisdicción indígena."[^protocolo] It also warns that the idea "de que los sistemas normativos indígenas son, a priori, violatorios de los derechos humanos suele ser un prejuicio discriminatorio."[^protocolo] The protocol cites, among others, Amparo en Revisión 202/2021, Amparo en Revisión 1041/2019 and Amparo Directo en Revisión 5465/2014; the project has read only the protocol's summaries of those cases.

**Amparo Directo 6/2018 (First Chamber, Nov. 21, 2019).** A non-member grazed cattle in a protected area of an Oaxaca community; the community authority sanctioned him; he went to state prosecutors; the community asked the criminal court to step aside, and its representatives brought a "Juicio de Derecho Indígena" before the Oaxaca high court's Sala de Justicia Indígena, which "reconoció la jurisdicción de la comunidad, convalidó el sistema normativo interno y su procedimiento." The Supreme Court denied amparo.[^ad-6-2018] In the project's view, this is the clearest published example of the validation route that article 2 contemplates.

**Cherán (Controversia Constitucional 32/2012, May 29, 2014).** After the federal electoral tribunal held (SUP-JDC-9167/2011) that the Indigenous community of Cherán, Michoacán, could elect its authorities by its own rules, Cherán's Council of Communal Government, acting as the municipal authority, sued the state legislature for amending the state constitution without consulting it. The Supreme Court held that ILO Convention 169 "forms part of the parameter of constitutional regularity," found that consultation forums were not held adequately, and invalidated the reform as to the parties.[^cheran]

**Yaqui water (Amparo en Revisión 631/2012, May 8, 2013).** Yaqui traditional authorities challenged the environmental approval of the Independencia aqueduct. The First Chamber, unanimously, confirmed amparo so that the tribe "sea escuchada para efectos de la operación (no construcción) del Acueducto," and distinguished consultation from consent: consultation must aim at consent, but its absence may not always stop the authorities, depending on the level of impact.[^ar-631] A separate constitutional controversy over the same aqueduct (109/2012) was still in a compliance phase in January 2022, with the consultation to respect "los tiempos que determine el Pueblo Yaqui, con base en sus usos y costumbres."[^cc-109]

## 4. State Indigenous justice systems

States implement article 2 differently. The project read the statutes below; dates are those printed in the copies read, and later amendments may exist.

| State | Statute | Forum and neutral | Matters | Process | Limits | Validation or review |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Quintana Roo | Ley de Justicia Indígena (1997; copy current to Dec. 31, 2012)[^qroo] | *Jueces tradicionales* appointed by the high court on the proposal of an Indigenous judicial council; must be "respetables de la comunidad, que dominen el idioma" (arts. 8-9) | Civil, family and listed criminal matters prosecuted on complaint (arts. 14-17) | Conciliation, then mediation, then arbitration "a conciencia y a verdad sabida" (art. 27) | Human rights (art. 10); serious crimes excluded (art. 18); conciliation and mediation "no se recomienda" in family violence (art. 27) | Settlement "homologado a una sentencia debidamente ejecutoriada," award is res judicata (art. 12); complaints to the magistrates for Indigenous affairs within three days (arts. 30-31) |
| Oaxaca | Ley de Derechos de los Pueblos y Comunidades Indígenas y Afromexicano (1998; copy current to Jan. 3, 2026)[^oaxaca] | Community authorities | Disputes where "ambas partes sean indígenas"; in mixed cases the respondent or claimant may choose the forum (art. 38) | The community's own | Human rights; excluding women is "contraria al respeto a los derechos humanos" (art. 29); detention limits, no torture, written decision (art. 38) | Decisions "serán compatibilizadas y convalidadas por las autoridades estatales" (art. 34); the Sala de Justicia Indígena helps enforce them (art. 40) |
| Chiapas | Ley de Derechos y Cultura Indígenas (1999, original text)[^chiapas-ldci]; Código de Organización del Poder Judicial (version not confirmed)[^chiapas-copj] | *Juzgados de Paz y Conciliación Indígena*; the judge "previamente deberá oír a las autoridades tradicionales del lugar" (Código art. 206) | Only where both parties are Indigenous (Ley art. 14) | "oralidad, conciliación, inmediatez, sencillez y pronta resolución" (Ley art. 12) | Human rights (Ley arts. 11, 13) | Appeal to a high court chamber that checks "que los derechos de los indígenas hayan sido respetados" (Ley art. 19) |
| Yucatán | Ley del Sistema de Justicia Maya (2014; amended 2017; parts invalidated by the Supreme Court in 2018)[^yucatan] | *Juez maya*, "la autoridad nombrada por la comunidad maya," with jurisdiction only in that community (arts. 2, 12) | Administrative infractions, matters open to settlement, non-serious crimes where pardon is available (art. 13) | Conciliation, then mediation, then arbitration with res judicata effect (art. 17) | Human rights (art. 16); no deprivation of liberty "en ningún caso" (art. 21) | Resolutions "válidas ante cualquier instancia" (art. 15, as amended; see the invalidity note) |
| Guerrero | Ley 701 (copy current to July 5, 2022)[^guerrero] | Community authorities and a community security system whose highest body is the General Assembly (arts. 38, 40) | Internal conflicts; non-members go to ordinary courts (art. 42) | Natural-resource conflicts by "la vía de la conciliación" (art. 74) | Constitution and human rights | Indigenous justice is "alterna a la vía jurisdiccional ordinaria" (art. 35); a 2018 amending decree was invalidated by the Supreme Court |
| Puebla | Ley de Derechos, Cultura y Desarrollo de los Pueblos y Comunidades Indígenas (Jan. 24, 2011)[^puebla] | Community normative systems | Not specified in the articles read | Not specified | "derechos fundamentales" (art. 54) | Decisions "serán convalidados por las autoridades jurisdiccionales del Estado" (art. 54) |

**Patterns (project analysis).** Two models appear. Quintana Roo, Chiapas and Yucatán build a state-appointed or community-named *judge* inside or beside the state judiciary, with a conciliation-mediation-arbitration ladder much like U.S. court-annexed ADR. Oaxaca, Puebla and Guerrero recognize the *community's own* authorities and then validate their decisions. Every statute read sets human rights as the outer limit, and several single out women, children and family violence. Quintana Roo's family-violence rule and Yucatán's bar on any deprivation of liberty are concrete design choices worth noting beside U.S. tribal peacemaking codes.

## 5. Peoples who live on both sides of the border

The border was drawn across these peoples' lands. The Tohono O'odham Nation says the 1854 Gadsden Purchase line "fragmentó las tierras ancestrales de la Nación y separó a muchas de sus familias," and that about 3,000 enrolled citizens still live on ancestral lands in Sonora.[^ton-brief] How each side governs and resolves disputes differs because the two states recognize them differently (project analysis).

**Tohono O'odham.** In the United States, the Nation governs from Sells, Arizona, with executive, legislative and judicial branches and eleven district governments.[^ton-brief] On the Mexican side, a federal monograph describes O'odham communities organized through ejidos and agrarian communities.[^cdi-papagos] A July 2026 report says Sonora communities, with their own traditional governors, want a role in decisions about the shared territory, as the Nation litigates a border-wall segment in federal court in Washington, D.C.[^mongabay] U.S. statute gives a border-crossing right to "American Indians born in Canada" but has no parallel for those born in Mexico.[^usc-1359] A 2001 bill would have let Tohono O'odham members in Arizona or Mexico apply for U.S. citizenship and "pass and repass freely" across the border; the project found no record that it was enacted.[^hr-1502] No source found describes how a dispute between O'odham on the two sides would be resolved.

**Kickapoo.** Congress found that the Texas Band "was forced to migrate from its ancestral lands to what is now the State of Texas and the nation of Mexico," let enrolled members apply for U.S. citizenship within five years of the roll, and provided that, notwithstanding the immigration laws, members "shall be entitled to freely pass and repass the borders of the United States and to live and work in the United States."[^kickapoo-act] The Kickapoo Traditional Tribe of Texas has a peacemaker system in its code (already in the project's records).[^kttt-peacemaker] The project did not find a primary Mexican source on governance of the Kickapoo community at El Nacimiento, Múzquiz, Coahuila.

**Yaqui (Yoeme).** A 2020 federal decree describes the Yaqui people in Sonora as eight communities, "todas ellas con una forma de organización, territorio ancestral y gobierno tradicional de conformidad con sus propios usos y costumbres," and created a presidential commission to prepare a "Plan de Justicia" on "tierras, territorio, agua y desarrollo integral," working with "los gobernadores tradicionales y sus secretarios electos conforme a sus usos y costumbres."[^yaqui-decree] The Supreme Court's aqueduct cases above were brought by these traditional authorities.[^ar-631] The Pascua Yaqui Tribe in Arizona is a separate federally recognized nation; no source found links its institutions to the Sonora communities' disputes.

**Cocopah (Cucapá).** A 2014 peer-reviewed article reports that Cucapá fishers in Baja California face fishing limits in the Colorado River delta, that the conservation framework "regula la zona en disputa desde 1993 excluyendo sus derechos," and that "cuando los cucapás desafían el marco legal de conservación ... se les criminaliza."[^cucapa] This is a dispute with the Mexican federal government, not a cross-border process.

**Kumeyaay (Kumiai).** The project could not retrieve a usable source on governance or dispute resolution in the Baja California Kumiai communities (the pages found refused automated access). This is an open gap.

## 6. What a sidebar could say (project analysis)

These are suggestions for a short sidebar, not draft text or provisions.

1. **The border is recent; the peoples are not.** The Tohono O'odham Nation's own account of the 1854 line and its citizens in Sonora makes the point in the Nation's words.[^ton-brief]
2. **Same people, different legal footing.** On the U.S. side a nation is a domestic dependent sovereign in a trust relationship; on the Mexican side its relatives are communities with a constitutional right to their own dispute resolution inside the national order, subject to validation by state courts.[^cherokee][^cpeum-2]
3. **Mexico writes the validation step into law.** Article 2 calls for "casos y procedimientos de validación"; Oaxaca convalidates community decisions; Quintana Roo turns a settlement into a final judgment; Amparo Directo 6/2018 shows the route in use.[^cpeum-2][^oaxaca][^qroo][^ad-6-2018] This invites comparison with how U.S. state courts recognize tribal judgments and how the model code might ask other forums to honor its outcomes.
4. **Party consent can move a criminal case into the community.** Article 420 of the national code ends a prosecution when both sides accept the community's resolution, with fixed exceptions.[^cnpp-420]
5. **Rights limits are explicit.** Mexican texts name women's dignity, gender perspective, children and family violence as limits.[^cpeum-2][^cnpp-420][^qroo]
6. **Status shapes access to ADR.** The 2024 general ADR law defers to "usos y costumbres" for Indigenous parties, so Mexican ADR is designed to yield to community norms rather than to sit beside a separate sovereign's courts.[^lgmasc]

## 7. Gaps and next steps

- **Primary texts not read:** the full Supreme Court judgments in AD 6/2018 and CC 32/2012 (only the Court's own summaries were read); AR 202/2021, AR 1041/2019 and ADR 5465/2014 (only as summarized in the 2022 protocol); the 1940 Yaqui decree (only as quoted in AR 631/2012); SUP-JDC-9167/2011.
- **States not covered:** San Luis Potosí, Michoacán (statute), Campeche, Hidalgo, Veracruz, Chihuahua and Sonora. The legal basis and current jurisdiction of Oaxaca's Sala de Justicia Indígena beyond articles 40 and 44 of the state law. Whether Quintana Roo's law and Chiapas's code were amended after the copies read. Guerrero's community police (the CRAC-PC) as an institution; Ley 701 does not name it.
- **Cross-border:** Kumiai governance; El Nacimiento Kickapoo governance from a Mexican primary source; any U.S.–Mexico agreement on border crossing for O'odham, Yaqui, Cocopah or Kumeyaay people; the Pascua Yaqui Tribe's links with Sonora.
- **Scholarship:** the project has not yet read the main English-language scholarship on Indigenous justice in Mexico (for example, work by María Teresa Sierra and Rachel Sieder on legal pluralism, which the 2022 protocol cites). A library or HeinOnline search is listed in the project's Westlaw list.
- **Pending law:** the federal general law on Indigenous and Afro-Mexican peoples' rights; recheck its status before the sidebar is written.
