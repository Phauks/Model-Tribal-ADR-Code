# Similar legal projects and what to learn from them

**Status:** research notes, written Nov. 23, 2026, in answer to Trevor's question whether anything like this project exists in the American legal system, for Native nations or for the general public, and what the project can learn from it. Nothing here has been built or decided. Section 6 lists recommendations, and the first of them (NILL-sourced constitution copies) waits for Trevor's choice. Numbers in brackets point to the Sources list in section 9. Statements marked "project analysis" are my reading of the sources or of the project's own data, not a source's words.

## 1. Short answer

Nothing in the American legal system does what this project does in one place, but each piece has a precedent. NARF's National Indian Law Library collects nations' laws [1], its Indigenous Peacemaking Initiative shares peacemaking laws [20], TLPI writes code drafting guides [8], and the Uniform Law Commission once drafted a model act with ten nations [21]. None pairs a model dispute resolution and peacemaking code with nation-by-nation profiles and essays (project analysis).

The five lessons that matter most:

1. **Consent.** NILL says a nation's permission to NILL is not consent to reuse elsewhere [1]. We serve 42 constitution copies captured from NILL, so that practice needs a decision.
2. **Gate 2 process.** Run it like a ULC drafting committee: open drafts, invited observers, case-study tests, and a line-by-line reading [21] [26].
3. **Commentary.** Pair each rule with a Comment, as the ABA and ALI do [27] [29], and add TLPI-style options and discussion questions [8].
4. **Overlap.** Link to NILL, IPI, NNI and the Restatement rather than rebuilding what they already do.
5. **Partners.** Approach NARF (NILL and IPI), NAICJA, TLPI, NNI and the MSU Indigenous Law and Policy Center first.

## 2. Native-focused resources

Each of these does one slice of what we plan to do. None combines a model code, commentary, nation-by-nation profiles and essays in one place.

### 2a. National Indian Law Library and its Tribal Law Gateway

The National Indian Law Library (NILL) is the law library of the Native American Rights Fund (NARF) in Boulder, Colorado, and has served the public since 1972 [4]. Its three public services are research help, the Indian Law Bulletins and the Tribal Law Gateway [4]. The Gateway lets users search 240+ tribal codes and 400+ constitutions [1].

NILL's Access to Tribal Law Project began collecting nations' laws in 1988 [3]. Over 300 nations take part, and participation is free because the library is "funded by donations and grants" [2]. A nation sends what it has, reviews NILL's work before it is added, and keeps "absolute control": it can redact, omit, or later withdraw any section, and NILL removes it "immediately" [2].

NILL's consent rule is the one that matters most for us. Permission to share in NILL's collection "does not serve as implied consent to use the materials in any other way," and reuse in another database needs "separate permission from the Tribal Nation itself" [1]. NILL also refuses "frequent or systematic" requests [1] and grants no republication rights to publishers [2]. Since 2020 NILL has piloted, with Open Law Library and partners under a federal library grant, a platform where a nation publishes its official laws and keeps control of the digital copy; the Stockbridge-Munsee Community was first [5].

### 2b. Tribal Law and Policy Institute and the Tribal Court Clearinghouse

The Tribal Law and Policy Institute (TLPI) is a "100% Native American operated" nonprofit that runs education, research, training and technical assistance for tribal justice systems [6]. Its Tribal Court Clearinghouse began as a volunteer project and links to codes nations post themselves, by state, with articles from people working in Indian country [7].

TLPI's Tribal Legal Code Resource series is the closest thing to model codes written for nations. The guides cover domestic violence, sexual assault and stalking, juvenile justice, and laws implementing the Tribal Law and Order Act and VAWA 2013 [8]. They are built from "examples, critical commentary, discussion questions, and exercises" so a nation can customize them, rather than from one text to enact [8]. Most were funded by federal agencies such as the Office on Violence Against Women and the Bureau of Justice Assistance [8]. The juvenile guide is the only one that reaches peacemaking courts [8].

### 2c. National American Indian Court Judges Association

The National American Indian Court Judges Association (NAICJA) was incorporated in 1969 to train tribal judges after the Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968 [9]. It is a membership association run by an elected board and a steering committee drawn from ten regions [9]. Its training arm is the National Tribal Justice Resource Center, which is rebuilding a repository of tribal bench books and practice guides [9]. NAICJA wrote the 1981 Model Code of Judicial Conduct for Indian Court Judges, already discussed in the project's comparative essay [23].

### 2d. Native Nations Institute

The Native Nations Institute (NNI) at the University of Arizona runs the Indigenous Governance Database and the Constitutions Resource Center. The database launched in 2012, holds more than 1,600 resources and passed 500,000 views in 2023 with no paid advertising [11]. The Constitutions Resource Center launched at NCAI's convention in 2016 and pairs excerpts of nations' constitutions with videos of Native leaders on reform [10]. NNI frames reform as nation-led: "Nothing works if it is decided for you by someone else" [10]. Parts of NNI's database need a login [1].

### 2e. Turtle Talk

Turtle Talk is a blog run by Professor Matthew L.M. Fletcher that posts primary documents from Indian law cases. Fletcher says it was "designed to allow tribal lawyers to have access to legal materials, primary source documents, without paying law firms or Westlaw/Lexis" [12]. It started as a way to post the *United States v. Michigan* consent decree materials, "Then we just kept going" [12]. It is fast and free but is one person's editorial choices, with no formal consent process (project analysis).

### 2f. Cohen's Handbook of Federal Indian Law

Cohen's Handbook is the field's treatise. Felix S. Cohen wrote the first edition for the Interior Department from roughly 4,000 treaties and statutes [14]. The 2024 edition, published by LexisNexis, has eleven editors led by Nell Jessup Newton [13]. Both the majority and the dissent cited it in *Arizona v. Navajo Nation* (2023) [15]. It is authoritative but sold commercially and covers federal Indian law, not each nation's own law.

### 2g. VAWA and ICWA resource centers

NCAI's Violence Against Women resource center collects code resources for nations exercising special jurisdiction under VAWA. It points to an intertribal working group's code checklist, TLPI's guides and the pilot nations' own code language [16]. It also says plainly that "tribes will need to work with an attorney to develop codes that are tailored to their unique needs" [16]. The National Indigenous Women's Resource Center (NIWRC) is a Native-led nonprofit giving training, technical assistance and policy work on violence against Native women [17]. On child welfare, the National Indian Child Welfare Association (NICWA) offers ICWA training and a summary of tribal child welfare codes [18].

### 2h. NARF's Indigenous Peacemaking Initiative

The Indigenous Peacemaking Initiative (IPI) is our nearest neighbor. NARF founded it in 1992 to help nations "implement and recover traditional Indigenous dispute resolution practices" [19]. It offers a place for nations to share materials, technical assistance, training and advocacy [19]. Its Tribal Models pages list nations' peacemaking laws with one-line summaries, "shared by them to help other Tribes" [20]. Its home page states that "peacemaking is not alternative dispute resolution" [19], which the project's method records already follow.

## 3. Earlier model codes for nations

No intertribal model ADR or peacemaking code exists that we have found; the comparative essay reached the same conclusion with a non-exhaustive search [23]. The models that do exist fall into three kinds: drafting guides, enacted laws shared between nations, and one uniform-law-style model act.

| Model | Who wrote it | How it is built | What it teaches us |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tribal Legal Code Resource series | TLPI, with federal agency funding | Sample provisions, commentary, discussion questions, exercises [8] | Give choices and questions, not one text to enact |
| VAWA code checklist and pilot code language | Intertribal working group, collected by NCAI [16] | Checklist citing enacted codes of four nations [16] | Point to real enacted provisions as the evidence |
| Model Code of Judicial Conduct for Indian Court Judges (1981) | NAICJA | Short code that expects nations "will modify some provisions" [23] | Ethics for non-lawyer neutrals |
| Model Tribal Secured Transactions Act (2005) | Uniform Law Commission drafting committee with representatives of 10 nations [21] | Statute text kept close to UCC Article 9, adapted for nations [21] | The nearest precedent for our Gate 2 process |
| Tribal peacemaking laws | Each nation; collected by NARF's IPI [20] | Enacted laws shared nation to nation [20] | Our evidence base; the IPI list overlaps ours |
| Navajo Peacemaking Program | Judicial Branch of the Navajo Nation | Statute (7 N.N.C. §§ 409-413) plus a Plan of Operations [24] | A living program, not a model; its rules are cited, not copied |

The Model Tribal Secured Transactions Act is worth a closer look because its process maps onto our Gate 2. A ULC committee started it in 2001 "at the request of several tribes" [21]. More than a dozen commissioners worked with counsel for several rancherias and representatives of ten nations, including the Crow Nation, Navajo Nation, Cherokee Nation and Little Traverse Bay Bands of Odawa Indians [21]. They met twice a year, wrote case studies to test each section, and debated them as a group [21]. Where tribal policy conflicted with the UCC, the model changed: it drops self-help repossession [21]. The Crow Nation's legislature then debated it for several months and adopted it 17-1 in April 2006 [22].

## 4. General-public analogues

For the general public, three institutions write model law and four publish free legal information. We resemble the first group in method and the second in delivery.

### 4a. Uniform Law Commission

The Uniform Law Commission (ULC) was established in 1892 and has produced more than 300 acts [25]. Its commissioners are unpaid volunteers appointed by the states [25]. Most of its money comes from state appropriations, plus publisher licensing and occasional grants [25]. It calls an act "model" rather than uniform when its purposes can be met "even if the act is not adopted in its entirety" [25], which is our situation.

Its process is the part to copy. A Scope and Program committee screens proposals, a study committee decides whether to draft, and a drafting committee with a reporter does the work [25]. Each committee gets an ABA advisor and invites "observers" from interested groups; meetings are open, and every draft is posted [26]. Committees meet about three times a year for at least two years, ending in a "line-by-line reading and debate" at the Annual Meeting [26].

### 4b. American Law Institute

The American Law Institute (ALI) writes Restatements, Principles and model codes such as the Model Penal Code. Restatements are addressed to courts, Principles to legislatures and agencies, and codes to legislatures in "prescriptive statutory language" [27]. A reporter drafts; advisers review; a Members Consultative Group reads drafts "the way the project's intended audience would read the drafts" [27]. Drafts move from Preliminary to Council to Tentative Draft and need a membership vote [27].

The ALI already did one project in our field. Its Restatement of the Law of American Indians, with reporters including Matthew L.M. Fletcher and Wenona T. Singel, both tribal citizens, was approved in 2021 and was the first Restatement of Indian law [28]. It restates federal Indian law in six chapters [28]; it does not restate any nation's own law or dispute processes.

### 4c. ABA Model Rules

The ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct are a model for state regulators, "leaving room for state-specific adaptations" [29]. Every state has adopted rules based at least in part on them [29]. Each rule is followed by "Comments" that are "not rules per se" but guide interpretation [29]. That split between rule and comment is the format our Commentary part already plans.

### 4d. Legal Information Institute (LII)

The Legal Information Institute at Cornell Law School publishes law free online for about 30 million visitors a year [30]. About 60% of its money comes from the law school, 20% from donations and 20% from a lawyer directory and ads [30]. Its Wex dictionary and encyclopedia is drafted by Cornell law students and recent graduates "Under LII's supervision" [31].

### 4e. Justia

Justia is a for-profit company founded in 2003 that offers free case law and codes and earns money from an attorney directory and web hosting [33]. Free access paid for by lawyer marketing is a model we should not copy, because it would put advertising beside nations' pages (project analysis).

### 4f. LawHelp.org

LawHelp.org, run by Pro Bono Net, is a network of statewide legal aid websites built on a shared template. The Legal Services Corporation gave the startup money in 2000 through its Technology Initiative Grants, and later state, foundation and IOLA money followed [32]. By about 2009, 29 states and territories had copied the New York site [32]. The lesson is a shared platform that local partners run themselves.

### 4g. Wiki-style references

Wikipedia lets anyone edit, under neutrality and no-original-research rules, and has no consent process for the communities it describes (project analysis, from Wikipedia's published policies). Wex moved the other way, to supervised student drafting [31]. Our contribute-and-review model with per-document permissions sits between the two.

## 6. What we should learn

These are recommendations drawn from the sources above; each says what it rests on.

### 6a. Fix a consent gap in how we host constitutions

NILL says a nation's permission to NILL "does not serve as implied consent" to use its laws elsewhere, and reuse needs "separate permission from the Tribal Nation itself" [1]. Decision record 0031 serves our own copies of nations' constitutions with a removal link. In `research/constitutions/documents.csv`, 51 documents for 48 nations were captured from NILL (publisher `national-indian-law-library`), and 42 of them are the primary copy we show (project analysis). Serving those copies is the reuse NILL says it cannot authorize. I recommend linking to NILL's copy for those 51 until each nation says yes, and keeping our copies for documents found on the nation's own site, in BIA files or in the University of Oklahoma collection. NILL's goodwill matters more to us than any one file.

**Decided Nov. 22, 2026.** Trevor chose to link to NILL until nations consent and to keep the copies in the bucket. Decision record [0042](../../docs/decisions/0042-link-to-the-national-indian-law-library-for-its-copies.md) applies the rule to every copy whose only address is NILL's: 54 constitution copies and 471 law copies, for 236 nations, are no longer served.

### 6b. Run Gate 2 like a ULC drafting committee

The ULC and the Model Tribal Secured Transactions Act give a tested process [21] [25] [26]:

1. Name a reporter (Trevor) and a small committee (Trevor, Patrick Chapin, Danielle Finn).
2. Invite observers: tribal judges through NAICJA, IPI staff, TLPI, and any nation that asks. Observers speak but do not vote, as at the ULC [26].
3. Post every draft on the site with a version label, as the ULC posts all committee drafts [25].
4. Test each provision against written case studies before adopting it, as the secured transactions committee did [21].
5. Hold a final line-by-line reading, open to observers, before calling a division done [26].
6. Add a reader group like the ALI's Members Consultative Group: non-experts who read "the way the project's intended audience would" [27]. Community members and court clerks fit here.

### 6c. Write commentary in the ALI and ABA pattern, plus TLPI's questions

Each provision would carry the rule, then a Comment that is "not rules per se" [29], then illustrations and reporter's notes in the ALI style (project analysis). From TLPI, add discussion questions and options so a community can choose, not just adopt [8]. From the ULC, use bracketed alternatives inside the text, such as the family law arbitration act's "[The term includes a federally recognized Indian tribe.]" [23]. Label the whole work a "model" act in the ULC sense, meant to work "even if the act is not adopted in its entirety" [25].

### 6d. Adopt NILL's consent practices for nation pages

NILL lets a nation review material before it is posted, redact or omit any part, and withdraw later with immediate removal [2]. Its staff "constantly reach out" to confirm materials are current [2]. Our request process already promises takedown within seven days; NILL's "immediately" is the higher bar. We should add a "reviewed by the nation" marker and a last-confirmed date to each nation page (project analysis).

### 6e. Cite tribal law in a form the profession is adopting

The Bluebook's 22nd edition added tribal law citation rules for the first time, written with Native law librarians, and a table of federally recognized nations [34]. We use the Indigo Book, so we should compare our tribal law citations against the new Bluebook rules and adopt its nation names table where it differs (project analysis). One of the librarians involved notes that oral traditions should count as law, not "mere testimony" [34], which supports how we treat peacemaking stories.

### 6f. Avoid funding models that do not fit

Grant-funded guides age: TLPI's code guides were last revised in 2012 and 2015 [8], and NAICJA's resource center is "gearing up" again [9]. LII mixes institutional support, donors and a directory [30]; Justia relies on lawyer marketing [33]. Our nonprofit, book sales and donations fit LII's mix without ads (project analysis). LawHelp.org shows that seed grants plus local partners running their own pages can scale [32].

## 7. Overlap and duplication risks

| Overlap | With | How to avoid duplicating |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A searchable library of nations' codes and constitutions | NILL Tribal Law Gateway [1] | Link to NILL; our value is analysis of dispute processes, not the law library |
| A list of nations' peacemaking laws | IPI Tribal Models [20] | Cite IPI's list; offer IPI our structured data and fuller summaries |
| Links to codes nations post | Tribal Court Clearinghouse [7] | Cite where it is the source; do not rebuild a link list |
| Constitution excerpts and reform videos | NNI Constitutions Resource Center [10] | Link to NNI for reform; we hold full documents and history |
| Federal Indian law in Foundations | Restatement of American Indians [28]; Cohen's Handbook [13] | Cite both; Foundations keeps to dispute resolution history |
| Case news and primary documents | Turtle Talk [12] | Link from our timeline; do not mirror |
