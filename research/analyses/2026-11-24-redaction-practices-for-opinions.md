# How courts and publishers keep names out of opinions, and what the site should do

**Status:** research spike, written Nov. 24, 2026, at Trevor's request ("Before redacting, find best practices and expected practices for the behaviour"). Nothing was redacted or changed on the site. Nothing here is legal advice. Numbers in brackets point to the Sources list in section 9, where each source is marked **read** (I read the passage myself on Nov. 24, 2026), **earlier note** (read for an earlier research note and cited from it) or **not read**. Statements marked "project analysis" are my own reasoning from those sources, not a source's words.

It follows the note on children, families and protection orders in the hosted Tribal court opinions (PR #468), which found 477 sensitive opinions of 2,368 [26]. All 477 are hidden until each is reviewed and redacted (PR #472).

## 1. Recommended policy

1. **Who is replaced.** Children, in every kind of case. Protected persons in protection order and domestic violence cases. Victims in criminal cases involving a child or a sexual offense. Wards, vulnerable adults and people in civil commitment cases. Any relative whose full name would identify one of those people. This list follows California's rule on privacy in appellate opinions almost item for item [7], and it covers what the Fort Peck, Navajo, Ho-Chunk, Penobscot and Pawnee laws protect [12]–[16]. Judges, lawyers, advocates, agencies and Tribal officials keep their names.
2. **What replaces them: real initials, with periods** ("J.D."), the form the nations' own rules name. The Saint Regis Mohawk appellate rules call for "the child's initial (i.e., IN RE: ABC), or other appropriate designation (i.e., IN RE: Doe)" [11]. The Pawnee rule calls for "initials or other similar designations" [12], and the Indian Law Reporter's index lists Tribal juvenile cases by initials [17]. Where a court already used initials or a pseudonym, keep exactly what the court wrote. Give each person the same initials in every opinion. Switch to a role word in brackets ("[the child]", "[Child 2]") when two people share initials, or when the initials would still point to one family in a small community. Do not use California's "first name and last initial" [7][8], because first names are distinctive in small communities (project analysis). Federal practice is moving from initials to pseudonyms for minors, and the change is out for public comment [3] (section 4). Revisit this point if it is adopted.
3. **Parents and relatives get initials in every case centered on a child.** That means juvenile, child welfare, adoption and guardianship cases, and also custody, paternity and child support cases. Here I recommend going further than the earlier note, which kept parents' full names in custody, paternity and support cases [26]. The reason is that a parent's surname usually is the child's surname. California, Texas and Washington all extend protection to relatives for that reason [8][9][10], and the Navajo Children's Code treats a parent's name as information that identifies the child [15]. The other parent, when a respondent in a protection order case, keeps their name.
4. **Remove details that point to a person.** Remove home addresses, birth dates, Tribal enrollment or census numbers and school names of the protected people. Remove anything that shows where a protected person lives or works. Federal law forbids governments from posting protection order information online when it "would be likely to publicly reveal the identity or location of the party protected" [6]. The federal criminal rule keeps only the city and state of a home address [2][3].
5. **Show every change.** Put each substitution in brackets ("[J.D.]", "[address removed]"). The Indigo Book says "[a]ny substitutions into quoted material should be bracketed" [23]. Initials the court itself used carry no brackets, so a reader can tell the court's choices from ours. At the top of each redacted page, add one line saying that names and details were replaced to protect privacy. This is what CourtListener does when it redacts by court order [18]. Lists show a small "Names replaced" badge. The page drops its "Original" link to static.case.law, as the earlier note proposed [26].
6. **Captions and citations use the initials without brackets.** Example: *In re J.D.*, 5 Am. Tribal Law 123 (…). The reporter volume and page do not change, so the case can still be found. When an essay quotes a redacted opinion, it quotes the redacted text, brackets included. Bracketed alterations are the standard way to change a quotation without misquoting [23], so this fits the rule that quotes match the opinion verbatim, read as "verbatim except for marked alterations" (project analysis; Trevor may want that wording added to the rule).
7. **Keep these pages out of search engines even after redaction.** The whole site is marked `noindex` until its first release (`site/static/robots.txt`). When that changes, opinions in the sensitive kinds should keep `noindex` and stay out of the sitemap. A name a redaction pass missed is then still hard to find by searching. Blocking search engines is CourtListener's first response to any privacy request [18], and it also keeps names out of its sitemaps [19].
8. **Make it reversible, and keep originals private.** The unredacted text stays only in the private bucket copy, which the project already keeps for every cited source. The names map stays private (bucket or D1), never in git. Names are replaced when the page is built, so a correction is a map edit and a rebuild under the same key. Do not rewrite git history; the earlier note explains why [26]. After filing, clear the cached copies and rebuild the search index.
9. **Requests from people named.** People named in a sensitive kind of case, or their relatives, get initials on request. They need no court order and no documents, only enough to find the opinion. People named in other cases can have the page blocked from search engines on request. Their names are replaced only if their nation's law or a safety reason calls for it. A page is removed only on a court order or at the nation's request. This is more generous than CourtListener [18], because the site presents these opinions on a nation's page and children cannot speak for themselves (project analysis). A nation's own restriction (Show, Cite only, Hide) applies on top, and the stricter rule wins. Requests are logged privately, and the site never confirms to anyone else whether a person appears in a case.
10. **Hidden until redacted.** This is already in place (PR #472).

One addition to the earlier scope: adult victims of sexual offenses are protected under California's rule [7] but were not part of the 477, which were chosen for children, protection orders and vulnerable adults. A short script pass over the other 1,891 opinions would find them (project analysis).

## 2. Tribal court rules

- **Fort Peck Tribes.** "[T]he names of any youth involved shall not be published" in youth offender, dependency and adoption matters (9 CCOJ §§ 309(b), 507(b); 10 CCOJ § 113(b)) [14]. Earlier note.
- **Navajo Nation.** The news media may attend closed children's hearings only if they "refrain from divulging information that would identify any child involved in the proceedings or the parent or guardian of that child" (9 N.N.C. § 1115(B)). Adoption hearings are confidential (§ 613), and a protection order petitioner need not reveal their address except to the judge (§ 1658) [15]. Earlier note. The Navajo Rules of Civil Appellate Procedure may say how the Supreme Court captions these cases, but the court's site did not load from this session [25]. Not read.
- **Ho-Chunk Nation.** "Records in children cases shall be withheld from public inspection" (4 HCC § 3.4(a)) [16]. Earlier note.
- **Penobscot Nation.** No name or identifying information may appear in any opinion "involving children, adults in need of protection, or other confidential proceedings" (§ 5(D)(1)). Judges may also withhold a victim's name in other sensitive cases (§ 5(D)(2)) [13]. Earlier note.
- **Pawnee Nation.** Papers in juvenile appeals "shall contain only initials or other similar designations and not the names of the parties" (tit. 2, § 112(a)) [12]. Earlier note.
- **Saint Regis Mohawk Tribe.** In appeals from the Family Court, "the names of the child shall not appear in the record on appeal. The cases shall be designated by the child's initial (i.e., IN RE: ABC), or other appropriate designation (i.e., IN RE: Doe)" (Tribal Code § 2.05.250 [Rule 23]) [11]. Read.
- **What Tribal reporters show.** The National Indian Law Library's index of Indian Law Reporter cases lists juvenile matters by initials with the docket number given as "Confidential". An example is *In the Matter of A.G.*, No. Confidential (Confederated Tribes of Grand Ronde Tr. Juv. Ct., Sept. 18, 2003), 31 ILR 6146 [17]. Read. The same index also lists child welfare cases by a parent's full surname, so courts were not consistent, which matches what the earlier note found [26]. I found no published policy saying whether the Indian Law Reporter itself changes names. Not found.

So the Tribal rules agree on two things: no names of children in juvenile and child welfare matters, and initials as the usual replacement. Navajo and Penobscot go further and treat anything that identifies the child, including a parent's name, as covered (project analysis).

## 3. Federal and state court rules

- **Federal filings.** Filings may include only "the minor's initials" (Fed. R. Civ. P. 5.2(a)(3)) [1]. The criminal rule adds that only the city and state of a home address may appear (Fed. R. Crim. P. 49.1(a)) [2][3]. These rules govern what parties file, not what courts write in opinions [3]. Read.
- **Federal opinions in Social Security and immigration cases.** In 2018 and 2023, the Judicial Conference's case management committee urged courts to use "only the first name and last initial of any non-government parties in opinions and orders" in these cases [4]. A tool the federal courts' administrative office built, which courts can turn on, replaces the case and party names in these opinions' public data with "Unavailable" [3]. Read. This is the closest federal precedent for changing names in opinions already published online.
- **Child victims in federal criminal cases.** Everyone working on the case must keep "the name or any other information concerning a child" in a secure place, and papers that disclose it are filed under seal (18 U.S.C. § 3509(d)(1)–(2)) [5]. Read.
- **Protection orders.** 18 U.S.C. § 2265(d)(3) bars "[a] State, Indian tribe, or territory" from posting protection order information online when it "would be likely to publicly reveal the identity or location of the party protected under such order" [6]. Read. It binds governments, not the project, but it is the clearest outside benchmark [26].
- **California.** In all opinions, courts "should consider" referring "by first name and last initial or, if the first name is unusual or other circumstances would defeat the objective of anonymity, by initials only" to eleven groups. These include children in all family law proceedings, protected persons in domestic violence cases, wards, mental health patients, victims in criminal cases, and persons "in other circumstances in which use of that person's full name would defeat the objective of anonymity" for any of the others (Cal. R. Ct. 8.90(b)) [7]. In juvenile court appeals this is required, and it extends to relatives whose full names "would defeat the objective of anonymity for the juvenile" (Cal. R. Ct. 8.401(a)) [8]. Read.
- **Texas.** In parental-rights termination and juvenile appeals, "the court must, in its opinion, use an alias to refer to a minor and to the minor's parent or other family member." An alias is "one or more of a person's initials or a fictitious name" (Tex. R. App. P. 9.8(a), (b)(2)) [9]. The comment says a fictitious name "should not be pejorative or suggest the person's true identity." It also explains the rule's reason: courts' efforts to disguise minors in opinions "may be defeated if the same children are fully identified in briefs and other court papers available to the public" [9]. The rule does not permit "alteration of the original appellate record" except by court order (9.8(d)) [9]. Read.
- **Washington.** Since Sept. 1, 2018, juvenile offender appeals are captioned "using the juvenile's initials." Parties must refer to "any related individuals in such a way as to not disclose the juvenile's identity" (RAP 3.4) [10]. Read.

The pattern across these rules: initials (or first name and last initial) for the child, the same for relatives whose names would identify the child, protection for victims and protected persons, and no change to the court's own record. A publisher's copy is not the record, so changing names in it is allowed; the Texas point is about keeping the original intact somewhere, which section 1, point 8 does (project analysis).

## 4. Initials or pseudonyms

Initials are what most current rules use: the federal filing rules [1][2], Washington [10], Pawnee [12], Saint Regis Mohawk [11] and the Indian Law Reporter's captions [17]. California prefers first name and last initial [7][8]. Texas allows initials or a made-up name [9].

The weakness of initials is that they can still identify someone, especially next to other facts. In March 2024 the U.S. Department of Justice proposed that federal filings "refer to minors by pseudonyms rather than by their initials" [3]. At their spring 2026 meetings, the civil, criminal and bankruptcy rules committees agreed and recommended the change. The amendments were set for public comment in August 2026. The appellate committee is still weighing how this "might interact with the way in which minors were referred to in a state court record or even in the caption of the case" [3]. Read. Trial lawyers' and crime victims' groups supported the proposal and urged "gender-neutral pseudonyms and pronouns" [22]. Read.

**Recommendation (project analysis):** use real initials by default, for three reasons. Captions then match how the nations' own rules and the Tribal reporters designate these cases [11][12][17]. A redacted case stays findable when another opinion cites it by initials. And the initials are only part of the protection, because section 1 also replaces relatives' names and removes locations, which is what makes initials identifying in the first place. Use a role word in brackets wherever initials would still identify someone. If the federal pseudonym amendment is adopted, consider switching children in juvenile and child welfare cases to pseudonyms. That would be a map change and a rebuild (section 1, point 8).

## 5. How others mark redactions

- **CourtListener.** It does not remove "any public document from our database without a court order." On request it blocks search engines with the robots meta tag or the `X-Robots-Tag` header. With a court order it will "anonymize or redact cases by replacing names with initials or black boxes, and placing a note at the top of the document explaining the change" (Content Removal Policy, last modified July 23, 2026) [18]. It also marks its sitemaps `noindex`, because sitemap links "contain the names of the parties in a case" [19]. Read.
- **Justia.** A federal court dismissed a suit asking Justia to remove a state appellate opinion naming the plaintiff, *Bonner v. Justia, Inc.*, 2019 WL 3892858 (D.N.J. Aug. 19, 2019), as quoted in [20]. Read in the blog's quotation; the decision itself was not read. Justia's own removal page blocked automated access [24]. Not read.
- **Federal courts.** Courts will not retroactively pseudonymize what a party filed without a motion showing a need for anonymity. *Smith v. Solomon* (C.D. Cal. 2026), as quoted in [21]. Read in the blog's quotation.
- **Caselaw Access Project.** It released the reporters with only the publisher's headnotes removed [26][27]. Earlier note. I did not find a published redaction policy.

The open-law publishers treat opinions as public records and redact only on a court order. The site has reasons to go further. It presents Tribal opinions on a nation's page, several nations' laws bar these names, and the project is not a court reporter (project analysis, as in [26]).

## 6. Keeping quotes verbatim

The Indigo Book treats a bracketed substitution as a proper alteration of a quotation: "Any substitutions into quoted material should be bracketed" (R8.2.3) [23]. Read. So "[J.D.] lived with [his] grandmother" is a faithful quotation of an opinion that named the child, as long as the brackets stay. Two practical rules follow (project analysis):

1. The build script replaces whole name tokens only, including possessives and first names used alone. It never rewrites other words. A test checks that the redacted text minus the bracketed parts equals the original minus the replaced names.
2. Essays and the book quote the redacted page, not the private original. A pull quote must never reintroduce a name.

## 7. Captions and citations

Captions keep the court's wording with the names swapped for initials: "In re the Custody of [Surname]" becomes "In re the Custody of J.D." Captions and citations leave out the brackets, because brackets inside an italic case name read badly and the citation is not a quotation. The reporter citation is unchanged, so the case can be found, and the page notice says the caption was altered. The appellate rules committee's concern about how pseudonyms "interact with … the caption of the case" [3] shows that courts have not settled this; using initials keeps the site's caption close to what a court would have written under the Pawnee, Saint Regis Mohawk or Washington rules [10][11][12] (project analysis).

The short captions already in `research/tribal-court-opinions/opinions.csv` and `research/capture-index.csv` are corrected going forward, not removed from git history [26].

## 8. What the build thread needs to know

1. Section 1 changes the earlier proposal in one place: parents get initials in custody, paternity and support cases too. That widens the name edits from about 165 opinions to most of the 321 where a parent is named [26].
2. Brackets go in the body text. Captions, titles, lists and citations use initials without brackets.
3. The page notice and the "Names replaced" badge are new UI, and they need a mockup before they are built.
4. `noindex` and leaving pages out of the sitemap for the sensitive kinds need to survive the site's first public release.
5. The request path in section 1, point 9 belongs with the nation restrictions work and the existing removal path.
