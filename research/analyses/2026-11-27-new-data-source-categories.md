# New kinds of data sources beyond the tier 2 and tier 3 plans

**Status:** research study, written Nov. 27, 2026, in answer to Trevor's question "so beyond states and what is stated as going on in tier 2, what could we gather?" (project thread "Find new data sources beyond tiers 2 and 3", Nov. 27, 2026). Nothing here is built, and nothing here is added to the [data roadmap](../../docs/data-roadmap.md) until Trevor picks. Every source below was checked against the data roadmap, the [tier 2 and 3 note](2026-11-26-tier-2-and-3-data-sources.md) and the Parked research table so that none repeats a planned collection.

**Method.** Each source's site was fetched from the cloud with `curl` on Nov. 27, 2026, to see whether a script can reach it (status code recorded below). Counts marked "counted" were read from the source that day (page text, or an OAI-PMH listing for the university repositories). Counts marked "project estimate" are the project's judgment and say so. Ranking weighs four things: legal value to a central repository of Native legal information; whether a script can fetch the whole set from the cloud; whether the hosting rules let the site serve it; and how much the site already holds of it.

Ranks 1 to 4 are the best value for the work: each is a self-contained set, mostly reachable now, that the site can serve, and none is held today. Ranks 5 to 8 are high value but each needs more care (licenses for some briefs, older volumes from HathiTrust, a key for the decrees). Ranks 9 to 12 are good but narrower. Ranks 13 to 16 are low priority, blocked or sensitive.

## 2. Each source

### 2.1 Federal approvals of nations' own laws (rank 1)

The HEARTH Act of 2012 lets a nation lease its trust land under its own regulations once the Secretary approves them, and the Bureau of Indian Affairs keeps a dated list with links to each approved regulation [1]. The page listed 135 dated approvals from 2013 to June 22, 2026 on Nov. 27, 2026. The Environmental Protection Agency lists every nation approved for "treatment in a similar manner as a state": 109 approvals to run regulatory programs across the Clean Air Act, Clean Water Act, Safe Drinking Water Act and Toxic Substances Control Act [2], and, for water quality standards, 85 nations found eligible and 54 whose standards EPA has approved, each with its dates [3]. The approved standards are each nation's own law. The same pattern fits two smaller lists already partly in the nations' jurisdiction search logs: the nations exercising special Tribal criminal jurisdiction under the Violence Against Women Act (the 2022 Act caps participating nations at 30 absent notice to Congress) [4], and tribal probate codes approved under the American Indian Probate Reform Act (not counted). These become nation-page facts ("Leasing regulations approved June 22, 2026") and documents in the nations' law. Cost is low: two list pages and the linked files.

### 2.2 Indian water rights settlements (rank 2)

As of November 2024, 39 Indian water rights settlements had been federally approved, 35 of them by Act of Congress [5]. The Bureau of Indian Affairs keeps the list of enacted settlements [6], and the University of New Mexico's Native American Water Rights Settlement Project holds 116 items in one set and 113 in a second, with sub-collections for settlements such as Gila River, Fort Hall, Fallon Paiute Shoshone, Northern Cheyenne and San Luis Rey (counted by OAI-PMH, Nov. 27, 2026) [7]. The Acts are partly in our Statutes at Large records; the settlement agreements and the water compacts nations signed with states are new. Value is high: water is among the largest bodies of modern Indian law, and Nevada (Fallon Paiute Shoshone, Pyramid Lake, Walker River) is in it.

### 2.3 Congressional Serial Set documents on Indians, 1817-1899 (rank 3)

The University of Oklahoma Law Library's digital collection "American Indian and Alaskan Native Documents in the Congressional Serial Set: 1817-1899" lists 8,744 documents with titles, dates, Serial Set numbers and one-line descriptions (OAI-PMH `completeListSize`, Nov. 27, 2026) [8]. Its PDFs refused a script (403), but the same documents are public domain and GovInfo's Serial Set collection holds 233,921 packages (GovInfo collections list, Nov. 27, 2026) [9], so a script could take the list from Oklahoma and the files from GovInfo. The federal laws note listed the Serial Set only as a source of committee reports for Acts; this is the whole 19th-century record of the relationship, a large set that pairs with the treaties, the Commissioner's annual reports and the Indian Claims Commission.

### 2.4 Historic laws of the nations (rank 4)

A first Internet Archive title search found 34 volumes such as *Constitution and Laws of the Cherokee Nation* (1875), *Compiled Laws of the Cherokee Nation* (1881), *Constitution and Laws of the Muskogee Nation* (1880 and 1893) and *Constitution and Laws of the Choctaw Nation* (1894) [10]. The constitutions catalog collects constitutions; the codes and session laws of the 19th-century national governments are not collected anywhere on the site. The Library of Congress items already listed as not in our records overlap with part of this. These are public domain and the nations' own law, so they fit the hosting rules squarely.

### 2.5 Supreme Court briefs, argument transcripts and audio (rank 5)

The Supreme Court posts argument transcripts from the 2000 term [11], and the Solicitor General posts the United States' briefs [12], which are government works. Of the 628 cases on our list, 53 were decided in or after 2000 and 185 since 1955, when the Court began recording arguments (counted from `research/supreme-court-coverage/native-cases-scdb.csv`). Recordings before 2010 are at the National Archives; Oyez's copies are under a noncommercial license, so we would link them rather than host. Parties' and amici's briefs are copyrighted by their authors, so private copy and link.

### 2.6 Public-domain treatises and decisions (rank 6)

Cohen's 1942 *Handbook of Federal Indian Law* was written in the Interior Department and printed by the Government Printing Office; we have a source record for it (`content/sources/c/cohen-handbook-of-federal-indian-law-1942.yaml`) but not a served copy. The 1958 Interior revision, *Federal Indian Law*, is also a government work. *Decisions of the Department of the Interior* (Land Decisions from 1881, Interior Decisions from 1930) hold a century of allotment, land and Indian affairs rulings, about 100 volumes by project estimate, in full view on HathiTrust [13]. *American State Papers: Indian Affairs* (two volumes, 1789-1827) is the earliest federal record; the Library of Congress's own pages refused a script (403), so the copy would come from HathiTrust or the Internet Archive.

### 2.7 Justice statistics (rank 7)

The Bureau of Justice Statistics must run a tribal crime data collection under section 251(b) of the Tribal Law and Order Act and publishes a yearly *Tribal Crime Data Collection Activities* report [14], along with *Jails in Indian Country*, the Census of Tribal Justice Agencies (2002) [15] and the National Survey of Tribal Court Systems. All are government works and feed the Data and charts pages.

### 2.8 Treaty rights in practice (rank 8)

The Great Lakes Indian Fish and Wildlife Commission publishes the off-reservation conservation codes its member nations adopt for the 1837 and 1842 ceded territories [16]; the Columbia River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission and the Northwest Indian Fisheries Commission do the same work for the Columbia and Puget Sound. The decrees behind them (*United States v. Washington*, *United States v. Oregon*, *United States v. Michigan*, the *Voigt* litigation) are federal court records, reachable through CourtListener's docket archive with the project's key. This is the living law of the treaties the site already holds.

### 2.9 Canada and cross-border nations (rank 9)

Canada lets anyone reproduce its federal laws and the decisions of its federal courts without permission, so long as the copy is accurate and not presented as official (Reproduction of Federal Law Order, SI/97-5) [17]. That covers the Indian Act and Supreme Court of Canada decisions. Crown-Indigenous Relations recognizes 70 historic treaties signed from 1701 to 1923 with 364 First Nations [18], plus the modern treaties. The Lands Advisory Board publishes 130 First Nations land code files; once a land code takes effect about 44 sections of the Indian Act stop applying [19]. This is where the parked Canada note would draw its texts, and it matters for nations divided by the border.

### 2.10 to 2.16

Treaty originals at the National Archives need a free catalog key [20]. The Census language-assistance file [21] and the National Park Service's Tribal Historic Preservation Officer program [22] are small facts for nation pages. The three open journal repositories answer OAI-PMH, so a citation index is cheap, but the articles stay private copy and link, and the Indian law journals session already began this list. The UN, Inter-American Court, ILO and Australian sites refused scripts (403), so those stay by hand. NCAI's resolutions are its own copyrighted works.

## 3. A caution on rolls and enrollment records

The Dawes rolls, the Indian census rolls of 1885-1940 and similar rolls are public domain federal records, but they name individual ancestors, they underlie living people's citizenship, and some (the Freedmen entries above all) are the subject of current disputes. The CARE principles ask that Indigenous data be governed by the peoples it describes. The project recommends not building these, or building only a link to the National Archives' own finding aids, until the Indigenous advisory council Trevor proposed on Nov. 27, 2026 can weigh in.
