# Peacemaking programs in the United States: a first survey

**Status:** research notes, written Nov. 21, 2026, for the peacemaking track.
It is not a finding about any nation's law or practice. It reports what each
nation, or a named secondary source, has published about how its program
works, and it says where the evidence is thin. Interpretations are labeled
project analysis. The search method and its limits are in the session log,
[2026-11-21-peacemaking-programs](../sessions/2026/2026-11-21-peacemaking-programs.md).

Following the charter, peacemaking is treated as its own track, not as a kind
of alternative dispute resolution.[^charter-3] The Native American Rights
Fund's Indigenous Peacemaking Initiative states that "peacemaking is not
alternative dispute resolution."[^narf-ipi-home] Each nation's process has its
own name, form and rules, so nothing here should be read as describing
"Native peacemaking" in general.[^method]

**What this note leaves out on purpose.** Several program pages describe
prayer, smudging, clan relationships, teachings or other ceremonial and
spiritual content. None of that is recorded here, even where it is public.
The charter keeps community-held knowledge out unless the community approves
in writing.[^charter-5] Where a program has such an element, the note says only
that it exists.

## 1. What earlier work already covered

Before this session the project had collected about forty peacemaking codes
and rules, mostly through the Native American Rights Fund's model-law pages,
and recorded peacemaking forums on 37 nations' profiles.[^narf-laws][^analysis]
Section 2 of the dispute resolution practice analysis counted structural
features in those records: placement in the court, consent to enter, return
to court, and agreements entered as judgments.[^analysis] This note builds on
that work. It does not re-read the codes itself; section 2.1 summarizes a separate
pass over the full code texts.

What was missing was the operating picture: who the peacemakers are, how
cases arrive, what happens to agreements, how many cases programs handle, and
whether anyone has measured results.

## 2. How the programs are built: patterns across nations

The table summarizes design choices that the nations' own pages or codes
state outright. A blank cell means the source read did not say, not that the
program lacks the feature. (project analysis, from the sources in section 3)

| Nation and program | Placement | Who serves | How a case enters | What happens to an agreement |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Navajo Nation, Peacemaking Program[^nn-plan] | Program of the Judicial Branch, with offices at each district court | Staff specialists, chapter-certified community peacemakers, or a peacemaker the parties choose | Walk-in request or referral; matters needing a court order, and domestic abuse, are filed in court first | Summary goes back to the referring court; parentage and child support agreements filed with the child support division are court-enforceable |
| Chickasaw Nation, Peacemaking Court[^chickasaw-page] | Division of the District Court | Named peacemakers | Parties must agree to peacemaking and to the choice of peacemakers | |
| Choctaw Nation of Oklahoma, Peacemaking Act[^choctaw-act] | Under the District Court; roster kept by the clerk | Peacemakers certified by the court | Motion for referral; silence by another party counts as an objection | Endorsed agreement becomes a "final, non-appealable order," enforceable like other orders |
| Mashpee Wampanoag Tribe, Peacemakers Court[^mashpee] | Part of the Tribal Judiciary | Roll of peacemakers certified by the Judiciary Committee and appointed by Tribal Council; two per case, one male and one female | Written request; silence by the other party counts as agreement to take part | Written agreement; peacemakers report to the court |
| Oneida Nation, Peacemaking[^oneida] | Run by the Trial Court, at no cost | Trained facilitators | Voluntary, at any stage, including appeals | Binding, with "the same force and effect as any court order"; violation may be contempt |
| Prairie Band Potawatomi Nation, Peacemakers Circle[^pbpn] | Separate from the courts | Ten peacemakers with four-year terms | Written consent of every party | Filed agreements and awards are enforceable like court orders; no appeal |
| Confederated Tribes of Coos, Lower Umpqua and Siuslaw, Peacegiving Court[^coos] | Division of the Tribal Court, under the Chief Judge | Trained volunteer Peacegivers with yearly background checks | By agreement, whether or not a case is filed in Tribal or state court | |
| Confederated Tribes of Grand Ronde, Peacemaker Program[^grand-ronde] | Part of the Tribal Court since 2007 | Members at least 25, approved by Tribal Council | Request form | No counsel at peacemaking conferences |
| Little River Band of Ottawa Indians, Odenaang Enjinoojimoying[^lrboi] | Tribal judicial program (on hold for rebuilding) | Two peacemakers, one male and one female, at least 21 | Voluntary; no court case needed | Written agreement signed by all |
| Organized Village of Kake, Circle Peacemaking[^kake-hn] | The Tribe's court; works alongside the Alaska state court | Village volunteers with a keeper of the circle | State court may send a juvenile's sentencing to the circle with prosecutor, defender and offender agreeing | Non-compliant offenders return to state court for sentencing |

Four choices recur and differ in ways that matter for drafting (project
analysis):

- **Inside or beside the court.** Most programs sit in the judiciary, as a
  division or a court-run program. The Prairie Band Potawatomi circle is
  described as separate from the courts, and the Ho-Chunk Traditional Court
  and Wilton Rancheria Traditional Court are elder bodies that also advise on
  custom.[^pbpn][^ho-chunk][^wilton]
- **What silence means.** The Choctaw Nation of Oklahoma treats a party's
  failure to respond to a referral motion as an objection; the Mashpee
  Wampanoag Tribe treats a non-response as a "declaration to participate."
  [^choctaw-act][^mashpee] These are opposite defaults on the same question.
- **Who decides.** Most programs leave the outcome to the parties. The
  Choctaw Act lets a peacemaker hear a matter only when "all parties to the
  dispute agree to be bound by the decision of the peacemaker," and the
  Prairie Band Potawatomi code lets a peacemaker issue an award when no
  agreement is reached.[^choctaw-act][^pbpn] The earlier analysis found seven
  nations' codes with a peacemaker who decides only with everyone's
  consent.[^analysis]
- **Finality.** Oneida, Choctaw and Prairie Band Potawatomi give agreements
  the force of a court order, and Choctaw and Prairie Band bar appeal. Several
  program pages say nothing about enforcement at all.[^oneida][^choctaw-act][^pbpn]

### 2.1 What the code texts add

A second thread read the full texts of the peacemaking codes already in the
dataset and tagged each peacemaking provision with a design feature. It found
580 provisions from 39 nations.[^code-pass] These are machine readings, checked
by script against the text and sampled by a second reviewer, but not yet
reviewed by the lead author, so the counts are a guide only. Quotations from
that pass stay private under the project's link-only rule for Tribal law; this
note uses counts and paraphrase.

| Feature | Provisions | Nations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Placement of the forum (court division or separate) | 138 | 37 |
| How a case enters (consent, referral, request) | 132 | 32 |
| Return to court when no agreement is reached | 90 | 27 |
| How peacemakers are selected | 77 | 24 |
| Agreement entered as a judgment or order | 47 | 20 |
| Peacemaker qualifications or training | 37 | 22 |
| Confidentiality | 32 | 19 |
| Matters covered | 29 | 18 |
| Fees or compensation | 27 | 15 |
| Appeal, review, or removal of a peacemaker | 18 | 13 |
| Peacemaker decides only with all parties' consent | 17 | 11 |
| Matters excluded | 14 | 8 |
| Participation of non-members | 7 | 6 |

A provision can carry several tags, and 127 provisions had none. Twenty-one
passages had ceremonial or spiritual content and were listed without quotation
or summary.[^code-pass]

Patterns the code texts show (project analysis, from the machine summaries):

- **A shared drafting family.** Several codes use nearly the same rule: a
  peacemaker mediates by traditional methods and may decide a disputed matter
  only if every party agrees to that authority, usually in writing. It appears
  in the Kickapoo Traditional Tribe of Texas, Northern Arapaho, Sokaogon
  Chippewa, Spirit Lake and Stockbridge-Munsee codes, and the
  Alabama-Coushatta and Choctaw Nation of Oklahoma codes bar a peacemaker from
  deciding a matter the parties cannot settle.[^consent-family] The likeness
  suggests a common model, which this note has not traced.
- **A way out of peacemaking.** The Northern Arapaho, Pit River and Kickapoo
  codes let a party, witness or participant ask the Tribal Court to end or
  limit peacemaking for harassment or similar conduct.[^way-out]
- **Who pays.** The Siletz, Kickapoo, Sokaogon and Stockbridge-Munsee codes
  set a small fee paid by the parties to compensate the peacemaker, while the
  Coquille code makes Peacegivers unpaid volunteers.[^fees]
- **No appeal, but a way to adjust.** The Siletz code bars appeal of a
  Community Law Court judgment but lets a party move to modify it, and the
  Mashpee ordinance makes an agreement final without appeal.[^no-appeal]
- **Reaching beyond members.** The Nez Perce code lets its Peacemaker Court
  serve people outside the Tribal Court's jurisdiction on minor matters, and
  the Chitimacha code lets parties choose, with court approval, a peacemaker
  who is not a Tribal member.[^beyond-members]

## 3. Program notes by region

### Navajo Nation

The Navajo Nation's program is the oldest court-connected peacemaking
program in the dataset and the most studied. The Plan of Operations dates
it to 1982: "In 1982, the Navajo Peacemaker Court was created by Navajo
judges."[^nn-plan] A 2016 presentation to the New Mexico Legislature traces
it from that start to a Peacemaking Division and to the Navajo Nation
Council's recognition of Diné Fundamental Law in 2002.[^nn-handout] The
statutory basis is 7 N.N.C. §§ 409-413.[^nn-code]

How it runs, from the Judicial Branch's own documents:

- **Peacemakers.** The Plan names three kinds: full-time staff specialists,
  community peacemakers certified each year by chapters, and peacemakers the
  parties choose, often a family elder.[^nn-plan]
- **Entry.** People may ask for peacemaking themselves, and courts,
  prosecutors, probation, schools and agencies refer cases. Matters that need
  a court order (such as divorce, guardianship or a protection order), and
  domestic abuse, "must first be filed in court" and may then be
  referred.[^nn-plan][^nn-brochure] The brochure lists a fee (yeel) of $80 or
  as negotiated.[^nn-brochure]
- **Agreements.** The peacemaker does not write the agreement for the
  parties: "The peacemaker is not allowed to write the agreement for
  you."[^nn-plan] Outcome summaries go back to the referring court.[^nn-plan]
- **Volume.** In fiscal year 2022 the program reports 473 new cases and 615
  handled, and in the fourth quarter of fiscal year 2025, 100 new cases and
  97 pending.[^nn-fy22][^nn-fy25] These figures were read through a fetch
  tool and should be re-checked against the reports before publication.

The program's own pages describe a ceremonial dimension; it is not recorded
here.

### Great Lakes and Northeast

- **Little Traverse Bay Bands of Odawa Indians.** The Tribal Court's
  program, Mnodaawin, uses peacemaking circles, beginning with juvenile
  matters such as truancy, with civil disputes planned. The page states that
  "Circles are not available to juveniles who don't admit to
  wrongdoing."[^ltbb]
- **Little River Band of Ottawa Indians.** Odenaang Enjinoojimoying handles
  civil disputes, employee grievances, divorce and juvenile delinquency, and
  is voluntary. The page says it is on hold during a rebuilding
  phase.[^lrboi]
- **Ho-Chunk Nation.** The Traditional Court sits in the Judicial Branch
  beside the Trial Court and Supreme Court and takes referrals from courts,
  social services or the person. It "meets every Monday."[^ho-chunk]
- **Oneida Nation (Wisconsin).** The Trial Court offers peacemaking and
  mediation free, at "all stages of litigation, including appeals."
  Agreements are binding as court orders, and "An agreement is not announced
  to the community."[^oneida]
- **White Earth Nation.** Judicial Services offers voluntary peacemaking for
  civil matters, employee grievances, divorce, Indian child welfare cases and
  some minor criminal cases from county courts, with or without a court
  case.[^white-earth]
- **Pokagon Band of Potawatomi Indians.** The Native Justice program has a
  court strand and a community forum for citizens' civil disputes; its page
  states that "Circle keepers are not judges; they do not decide the
  outcome."[^pokagon]
- **Lac Courte Oreilles Band.** A secondary summary of its children's code
  describes peacemaking as an option in informal disposition of child
  welfare matters, by mutual consent.[^lco]
- **Mashpee Wampanoag Tribe.** The most detailed public operating rules in
  this sweep: a certified roll of peacemakers, civil matters only
  ("Peacemaking shall not be allowed in criminal matters"), two peacemakers
  per case, and a notice procedure in which silence counts as
  participation.[^mashpee]
- **Seneca Nation of Indians.** The Peacemakers Court, established in 1848,
  is a trial court whose three Peacemakers per court "are elected every four
  years," with appeals to a Court of Appeals.[^seneca] Despite the shared
  word, it is an adjudicating court, not a consensual process (project
  analysis).

### Pacific Northwest and California

- **Confederated Tribes of Grand Ronde.** The Tribal Court began its
  Peacemaker Program in 2007; peacemakers must be members at least 25 years
  old, "known and respected in the community," and are approved by Tribal
  Council.[^grand-ronde] A database summary of the court ordinance says no
  party may be represented by counsel at a peacemaking conference.[^grand-ronde-302]
- **Confederated Tribes of Coos, Lower Umpqua and Siuslaw.** The Peacegiving
  Court, created by resolution in 2005, "is a division of the Tribal Court
  and is subject to the supervision of the Chief Judge," and any type of
  dispute may be referred.[^coos] A proposed amendment to its rules was
  posted in February 2026; whether it passed was not checked.[^coos-2026]
- **Cow Creek Band of Umpqua Tribe of Indians.** Peacemakers are appointed by
  the Tribal Court under Title 45, and nine members had been appointed when
  the Tribe announced a 2024 training.[^cow-creek][^cow-creek-2023]
- **Tulalip Tribes.** The Tribal Court's Elders Panel is a diversion program
  for young, low-risk offenders: "Offenders volunteer to participate on the
  panel." The court page reports that of more than 65 young people referred
  over nine years, "Fewer than ten percent" returned to court.[^tulalip] A
  second Tulalip document gives a different age range and caseload, so the
  figures need checking before use.
- **Wilton Rancheria.** A five-member Traditional Court of Elders is
  established by the constitution and may advise the Tribal Court on custom
  and tradition.[^wilton]
- **Confederated Tribes of the Colville Reservation.** A 2012 interview
  describes a peacemaking program the Tribes "launched and grown"; nothing
  more current was opened.[^colville]

Several other nations in the region have peacemaking codes in the dataset
(for example Coquille, Klamath, Nez Perce, Siletz, Hopland and Pit River),
but their court pages, as opened, describe no program
operations.[^analysis]

### Oklahoma, Texas and the South

- **Chickasaw Nation.** The Peacemaking Court is a division of the District
  Court, described as "a forum for resolving conflicts using Chickasaw
  traditions, customs and culture," and "requires the parties to agree to
  peacemaking and to the selection of the peacemakers."[^chickasaw-page]
- **Choctaw Nation of Oklahoma.** The Peacemaking Act sets out certification,
  a roster, a referral motion with a fifteen-working-day response period, and
  entry of agreements as final, non-appealable orders (sections 4, 10, 11,
  13).[^choctaw-act]
- **Kiowa Tribe.** The Peacemaking Act makes peacemakers a division of the
  District Court; only its section list was available.[^kiowa]
- **Kickapoo Traditional Tribe of Texas.** The Tribal Court "shall have the
  power to administer a Peacemaker Program" and hires, trains and supervises
  peacemakers; the Peacemaker System is part of the court system but
  independent of its judges.[^kickapoo]
- **Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians.** A 2005 Honoring Nations
  presentation describes the Peacemaker Court as "an alternative venue within
  the Tribal Court System," with two peacemaker judges and referrals from
  courts, law enforcement and lawyers when the parties agree.[^mbci]
- **Prairie Band Potawatomi Nation (Kansas).** See the table in section 2.
  The code was read through a fetch tool; a later direct request found that
  the code has moved to a new host, so the section letters need
  re-checking.[^pbpn]

### Alaska

Alaska matters for a different reason: most Alaska Native villages have no
Indian country after *Alaska v. Native Village of Venetie Tribal
Government*, so Tribal forums there rest on jurisdiction over members rather
than territory.[^venetie] The Alaska Supreme Court has held that "Tribal
courts in Alaska have jurisdiction to adjudicate custody disputes involving
tribal members," concurrently with state courts, and later that Tribal courts
have "inherent, non-territorial subject matter jurisdiction" over child
support for member children.[^john-v-baker][^ccthita-2016]

- **Organized Village of Kake.** The Tribe "adopted Circle Peacemaking as its
  tribal court in 1999."[^narf-kake] A state judge may, with the prosecutor,
  defender and offender agreeing, "turn the juvenile's case over" to the
  circle for sentencing, and offenders who do not comply return to state
  court.[^kake-hn] The Indian Law and Order Commission cited Kake as a model
  and recommended that "More Tribal villages need Tribal courts and
  sentencing circles."[^iloc-ak]
- **Central Council of the Tlingit & Haida Indian Tribes of Alaska.** Its
  code provides a Court of Elders that gives advisory opinions on customary
  law and may mediate at the parties' joint written request, with decisions
  filed as final judgments.[^ccthita-code]
- **Sitka Tribe of Alaska and Kenaitze Indian Tribe.** Their codes, as
  summarized in a national database, let the Tribal court use traditional
  dispute resolution in children's cases (Sitka) and refer cases to a
  Traditional Conflict Resolution Program (Kenaitze).[^ncorj]

## 4. What the evaluations show, and what they do not

Only one comparison study of a Tribal peacemaking program turned up: Eric
Gross's study of the Navajo program, funded by the National Institute of
Justice.[^gross]

- Peacemaking participants rated their process fair more often than Family
  Court participants (81% against 50%), more often saw their case as settled
  (79% against 51.4%), and more often said they could voice their feelings
  (86% against 50%).[^gross]
- The problem recurred for 29% of peacemaking participants and 64% of Family
  Court participants, but Gross reports that "the difference was not found to
  be statistically significant," and warns that "the sample size was small
  and the methodological design was not random."[^gross]

Other figures are program reports, not evaluations:

- Kake's Honoring Nations profile reports, over four years, 80 sentenced
  offenders of whom "Only two" returned to state court, and a 97.5 percent
  completion rate against 22 percent for the Alaska court system. It states
  no method or comparison.[^kake-hn] The Indian Law and Order Commission
  repeats a claim of recidivism "as much as 40 percentage points lower" than
  the state figure, citing the same kind of source.[^iloc-ak]
- Tulalip's Elders Panel figure (fewer than ten percent returning) is the
  court's own count.[^tulalip]

The best-documented outside evaluation is of a state-court program modeled
on Native peacemaking, not a Tribal one. The Center for Court Innovation's
study of the Red Hook Community Justice Center in Brooklyn found that 79
percent of participants completed, but says it is "not an impact evaluation"
and that "a comparison group is not included." It also found that some
defendants "did not understand the voluntary nature of the program," and that
prosecutors recommended peacemaking only in low-level cases.[^lambson]

(project analysis) The evidence supports saying that participants in at least
one large program found peacemaking fairer and more satisfying than court.
It does not yet support claims about lower reoffending or recurrence. A model
code's commentary should say so, and could invite nations to build
evaluation into their programs, as Grand Ronde's ordinance does by making the
court responsible for program evaluations.[^grand-ronde-302]

## 5. Critiques and open problems

- **Domestic violence.** Donna Coker's empirical study of Navajo peacemaking
  found it may help some battered women, but also that "some women are
  coerced into participation, agreements are difficult to enforce," and some
  peacemakers favored keeping marriages together.[^coker-1999] Her 2006
  article returns to the question of screening and safety.[^coker-2006] The
  Navajo Plan's rule that domestic abuse matters must first be filed in court
  is one response.[^nn-plan] Both articles were read at abstract level only.
- **Voluntariness under pressure.** The Red Hook study found that defendants
  sent by a judge did not always grasp that they could decline.[^lambson]
  Programs that take court or prosecutor referrals face the same risk
  (project analysis).
- **Use outside the community.** Carole Goldberg's 1997 article asks whether
  Tribal peacemaking can be applied in disputes with non-Indians; the Center
  for Court Innovation's planning paper raises the same question for state
  courts.[^goldberg][^wolf-2012] Goldberg was read at abstract level only.
- **Lawyers and rights.** Grand Ronde excludes counsel from peacemaking
  conferences, and the Choctaw and Prairie Band Potawatomi codes bar appeal
  from agreed outcomes.[^grand-ronde-302][^choctaw-act][^pbpn] How these
  choices sit with the Indian Civil Rights Act, and with state recognition of
  the resulting orders, was not researched here.
- **Program durability.** Little River's program is on hold for rebuilding,
  and several programs publish no caseload at all.[^lrboi] (project analysis)
  Staffing and funding may matter as much as code text.

## 6. Questions for the peacemaking track (project analysis)

These are questions, not drafting, and they wait for Gates 1 and 2 under the
roadmap.[^roadmap] The roadmap already suggests that the code might only
connect a court to a nation's own peacemaking forum (referral, return, entry
of agreements) without defining peacemaking itself.[^roadmap]

1. **Connecting provisions only?** The programs differ most in who serves
   and how sessions run, and least in how cases come from and go back to
   court. That supports the roadmap's option of connecting provisions that
   leave the process itself to each nation.
2. **Default for silence.** Should a referral provision offer both defaults
   (Choctaw: silence is refusal; Mashpee: silence is participation) as
   lettered alternatives?
3. **Screening.** Should every connecting provision carry a domestic
   violence and safety screen before referral, as the Navajo Plan does by
   requiring court filing first?
4. **Finality and review.** Should entry of an agreement as an order be paired
   with a limited path back to court, given that some codes bar appeal?
5. **Off-reservation and Alaska settings.** Should commentary address
   member-based jurisdiction, following *John v. Baker*, for nations without
   Indian country?
6. **Evaluation.** Should commentary encourage nations to keep the counts
   that would let them evaluate their own programs, on their own terms?

## 7. Gaps and next steps

- **Not reached:** most Plains and Southwest nations, several California,
  Puget Sound and Nevada nations, and Alaska regional organizations (see the
  session log). A second sweep should start there.
- **To read in full** (added to the project's single pull list): Yazzie
  1994, Zion 1983, Bluehouse & Zion 1993, Coker 1999 and 2006, Melton 1995,
  Valencia-Weber 1994, Goldberg 1997, and Nielsen & Zion 2005.[^yazzie][^zion][^bluehouse][^melton][^valencia][^nielsen-zion]
- **To re-check:** the Navajo Judicial Branch figures and brochure, the
  Prairie Band Potawatomi code at its new host, and the Tulalip figures.
- **Version history:** the Coos amendment and the Navajo Plan's 2013
  amendment are candidates for document-history records once the format in
  pull request #47 is merged.
- **Codes:** the code-parsing pass is summarized in section 2.1; its
  provisions need the lead author's review before any is cited in commentary.
- **Consultation:** every program described here belongs to a nation that
  can correct or remove it (charter; requests process). Before any of this
  appears in commentary, the nations named should be asked.
