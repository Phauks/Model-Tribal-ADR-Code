# Removal routes for the map: what route data exists beyond the Trail of Tears

**Status:** research spike, written Nov. 27, 2026, in answer to Trevor's request for research on adding more removal routes to the map's historic layer. Nothing was built and no site or content was changed. A research subagent did the first sweep, as Trevor asked; I then re-ran the key counts and re-read the key quotes myself (marked "rechecked"). Numbers in brackets point to the Sources list in section 6. "Project analysis" marks my own reasoning or estimate. Everything was read on Nov. 27, 2026.

## 1. Short answer

Only one more route has ready-made public line data: the Nez Perce National Historic Trail, which the Forest Service publishes as a 532-line feature service [5]. It is the 1877 flight of the Nez Perce, a war and a flight rather than a removal, so it fits only if the layer is called something like "Removals and forced journeys" (project analysis). Every other candidate either has no line data at all or has line data only as figures in a federal report or as unlicensed student layers.

The Trail of Tears "additional routes" that the 2009 amendment added are already in the layer we use, so there is nothing to add there [1][3].

**Recommendation (project analysis).** Add the Nez Perce trail first, because it is the only candidate that is federal, public domain, designated by Congress and ready to download, and label it honestly as the 1877 flight, not a removal. For the removals themselves, the next step is two requests rather than tracing: ask the Park Service's National Trails office in Santa Fe for the Long Walk route files behind the 2009 study maps, and ask the Potawatomi Trail of Death Association for its route. Because the Navajo Nation Tribal Council and the Mescalero Apache Tribe did not support designating the Long Walk [8], the Long Walk should go on the map only after asking both nations, under the charter's rule that a nation may restrict or remove material about it (commitment 6), which the map's homelands layer already follows [18]. Choctaw, Muscogee, Chickasaw and Seminole routes have no data anywhere we looked and should wait for work with those nations.

## 2. Cherokee: the Trail of Tears additional routes

The Omnibus Public Land Management Act of 2009, Pub. L. 111-11, § 5206, rewrote 16 U.S.C. 1244(a)(16)(C) to add "The Benge and Bell routes," the land parts of the designated water routes in Alabama, Arkansas, Oklahoma and Tennessee, the routes from the collection forts to the emigration depots, and related campgrounds [3] (rechecked). (The thread's first brief cited section 7208; the right section is 5206.)

Those routes are already in the Park Service layer the site uses. The layer has 2,006 line pieces [1] (rechecked), and its route-name field `TRALTNAME` holds 11 route names: Bell, Benge, Disbandment, Drane, Hildebrand, Northern, Overland Water, Roundup, Taylor, Water and Whiteley-Drane [1] (rechecked). Our build script keeps every piece and every name (`site/tools/build-historic-maps.mjs`, `buildRemovalRoutes`), so nothing is filtered out. The campgrounds are points, which our line layer does not carry; whether the Park Service publishes them as a separate layer was not checked.

The Park Service's metadata says the line "was identified before ground-verification could occur and consequently the designated trail does not always coincide with historic trail trace," that it "should not be displayed or used at scales that exceed 1:100,000," and that "No tests for horizontal accuracy were performed" [2] (rechecked). The site already caps the layer at zoom 10, which is roughly that scale (project analysis). The Forest Service also republishes a Trail of Tears layer [7]; it adds nothing we need.

## 3. Nez Perce: the 1877 flight

**Legal status.** Congress designated "The Nez Perce National Historic Trail, a route of approximately eleven hundred and seventy miles extending from the vicinity of Wallowa Lake, Oregon, to Bear Paw Mountain, Montana," as shown in the Department of Agriculture's 1982 trail study [3] (rechecked). The Forest Service administers it.

**Data.** The Forest Service Region 1 service `R01_NezPerceNeeMePoo_NationalHistoricTrail_Centerline` has 532 lines [5] (rechecked). Its extent runs from about 117.9° to 108.2° west and 43.5° to 48.4° north [5] (rechecked), which covers Wallowa Lake to Bear Paw (project analysis from those coordinates). Its description says it "contains the public route of the Nez Perce National Historic Trail (NPNHT), the interim public route of the NPNHT, and researched historic trail locations related to the 1877 flight of the Nez Perce," and that the data "could be an approximation and may not represent the exact location of the trail" [5].

**Accuracy.** The line mixes three things. Of the 532 lines, 456 have no trail status, 52 are "Existing", 22 "Planned" and 2 "Decommissioned" [5] (rechecked). The route comments include "Auto tour route and FS roads chosen due to lack of obvious public roads near historic location" and "Connector from trail to Highway 29" [5] (rechecked), so some lines are driving routes, not the 1877 path. A build would have to sort lines by the `SOURCE` and `ROUTE_COMMENTS` fields and keep the researched historic locations (project analysis).

**License.** The Forest Service item gives only a no-warranty disclaimer [5]. The Bureau of Land Management's Oregon and Washington copy states: "None, these data are considered public domain" [6] (rechecked). As a federal work it is public domain either way (project analysis from 17 U.S.C. 105).

**Framing.** The 1877 trail is a flight during a war. After the surrender at Bear Paw, the Nez Perce were sent to Fort Leavenworth and then Indian Territory, which was a removal (from memory, not checked against a source in this spike); no line data for that exile was found. The map label should say "Flight of the Nez Perce, 1877" and not "removal" (project analysis).

**Effort.** Download through the same paged query as the Trail of Tears, sort the lines, join the pieces, and cut a tile set; about half a day (project analysis based on `buildRemovalRoutes`).

## 4. Long Walk of the Navajo and Mescalero Apache, 1863-1866

**Legal status.** The Long Walk is on the study list only. 16 U.S.C. 1244(c)(42), added by Pub. L. 107-214 (2002), describes "The Long Walk Trail, a series of routes which the Navajo and Mescalero Apache Indian tribes were forced to walk beginning in the fall of 1863 as a result of their removal by the United States Government from their ancestral lands" [3] (rechecked); Senate Report 107-184 is the report on that bill [15]. The Park Service's Draft Environmental Impact Statement was noticed on April 10, 2009 (74 Fed. Reg. 16,419) and the Abbreviated Final on Dec. 14, 2010 (75 Fed. Reg. 77,897), which says "The feasibility study determined the Long Walk routes fully meet the criteria for designation as national historic trails" [8] (rechecked). The Record of Decision (76 Fed. Reg. 71,601, Nov. 18, 2011) chose no action: "The National Park Service decision to support the no action alternative as the selected action is based on the lack of support for designation by the Navajo Nation Tribal Council and the Mescalero Apache Tribe" [8] (rechecked). Congress has not designated it; the only Long Walk entry in § 1244 is the study-list entry [3].

**Data.** No Park Service GIS file was found in ArcGIS Online or the Park Service's planning site [9]. The 2009 draft study (168 pages) has route maps with the legend "Feeder routes and main removal routes / Navajo return route / Mescalero escape routes" and speaks of "approximately 1,350 miles of land routes" [9]. They are pictures inside a PDF. The maps rest on Flint and Flint, *Maps of the Long Walk* (2005), a report for the Park Service's National Trails office in Santa Fe [10]. A University of Miami student layer `LongWalkRoutes` has 3 lines, one each for 1863, 1864 and 1866, with no description or license [11]; it should not be reused without permission.

**Accuracy.** Flint and Flint say gaps in the routes "resulted from incompleteness of, ambiguities in, and apparent internal conflicts within the documentary record, as well as shifts and changes in place names" [10].

**Effort.** If the Park Service shares the files it made the maps from, low; the maps look GIS-made, but that is an inference. If not, georeference the study maps and trace about 1,350 miles: medium to high (project analysis).

**Care.** Both nations declined federal designation [8]. The Record of Decision does not say why, and declining a trail is not the same as objecting to a line on a map, but the site should ask both nations before adding it (project analysis, applying the CARE principles and charter commitment 6 [18]).

## 5. Potawatomi Trail of Death, and the other removals

**Potawatomi Trail of Death (1838).** No federal or state agency line was found. ArcGIS Online has two student or classroom layers, each one line with no license or metadata (one from Indiana University with 27 camp points) [12]. Wikipedia, citing the Association, says "Placement of markers, signage and online maps of the route are coordinated by the Potawatomi Trail of Death Association," that 80 markers stood along the route in all four states as of 2013, and that the route was made a Regional Historic Trail in Indiana, Illinois and Kansas in 1994 and Missouri in 1996 [13]. It is not in 16 U.S.C. 1244 [3]. Not checked: the Association's own website (it did not answer), Indiana's state map layers, and state transportation data, so "no state layer" is unverified. The 1838 journal of the removal party and Father Benjamin Petit's letters are public domain by age (project analysis) and could support a reconstruction, but asking the Association first is cheaper.

**Choctaw, Muscogee (Creek), Chickasaw and Seminole.** The Trail of Tears trail is limited by statute to routes "traveled by the Cherokee Nation" [3]. The Park Service's Trail of Tears pages say "most of the major tribes – the Choctaws, Muscogee Creeks, Seminoles, and Chickasaws – agreed to be relocated" but map only Cherokee routes [4]. The Park Service's planning list for the National Trails office shows no study of these routes [14]. ArcGIS Online has a Choctaw story map of one removal party's route, with no stated license [16]. Grant Foreman's *Indian Removal* (1932) has maps, but its copyright renewal could not be checked (the HathiTrust catalog refused automated lookups); a 1932 work that was renewed stays protected through 2027 (project analysis from 17 U.S.C. 304), so it should not be used until a renewal search is done.

**Others.** ArcGIS Online searches found no route lines for the Ponca (1877), Nome Cult Trail (1863), Dakota (1863), Modoc (1873), Northern Cheyenne (1878), Yavapai (1875), Chiricahua Apache (1886), Ho-Chunk, Sauk and Meskwaki, Shawnee, Lenape, Wyandot, Odawa, Miami and Kickapoo removals. A bill to study a Ponca trail was sought in 2014 [17], but § 1244 lists none of these [3]. This means "none found," not "none exist"; state agencies were not searched one by one.
