# Tribal rules of evidence and dispute resolution: a first spike

**Status:** research notes, written Nov. 21, 2026, as a short spike to decide whether rules of evidence deserve a place in the model code project. It is not a finding about any nation's law or practice. It reports what a nation's published code, rule or court opinion says, and where the reading was thin. Interpretations are labeled project analysis. Method and limits are in the session log, [2026-11-21-tribal-rules-of-evidence](../sessions/2026/2026-11-21-tribal-rules-of-evidence.md). Tribal legal texts are link-only by default, so this note summarizes them instead of quoting them.[^link-only]

## Recommendation

**Pursue it, narrowly.** Evidence matters to the model code at the points where a dispute resolution process meets a court, and almost nowhere else (project analysis, from sections 1 to 3):

1. **Confidentiality and privilege for dispute resolution communications.** This is the strongest case. The project's own provision data already holds 85 provisions from 38 nations that make mediation, peacemaking, conciliation or settlement talks confidential, privileged or inadmissible, and they differ in ways a drafter has to choose among.[^provisions] The Uniform Mediation Act shows that a dispute resolution statute can carry its own privilege rules without a general evidence code.[^uma]
2. **What happens when a matter returns to court.** Agreements entered as orders, reports by the neutral, and whether the neutral can be called as a witness all turn on evidence rules. Nations answer these differently (section 2).
3. **How custom is proven, and how sacred material is protected,** as commentary rather than provisions. Nations already use elders, expert witnesses, judicial notice and referral to traditional courts (section 1.4). Colville's rule barring evidence that would force a peacemaking participant to disclose protected teachings is directly relevant to CARE and to the peacemaking track.[^colville-sacred]

**Do not pursue** a general survey of Tribal court evidence codes or a model evidence code. That is court procedure, not dispute resolution; model texts already exist; and the first scholarly survey of Tribal evidence codes was published this year.[^morote] That survey (section 1.6) does not discuss mediation, peacemaking or settlement confidentiality at all, so the dispute resolution questions above are still open, and they are the part of evidence law this project is placed to add. The survey itself suggests an intertribal model evidence code; that would be a separate project, and this note does not recommend taking it on here (project analysis).

## 1. Rules of evidence in Tribal courts

### 1.1 Federal law sets no evidence code for Tribal courts

The Federal Rules of Evidence apply to proceedings in listed federal courts and the territorial district courts; Tribal courts are not among them.[^fre1101] The Indian Civil Rights Act gives criminal defendants the rights to confront witnesses and to compulsory process, but prescribes no evidence code.[^icra-a] One federal statute does push nations toward written rules: a nation that imposes more than one year of imprisonment must, before charging, make its rules of evidence publicly available, and the special Tribal criminal jurisdiction under the Violence Against Women Act carries the same duty.[^icra-c][^vawa] Courts of Indian Offenses are bound by the Federal Rules of Evidence except where the court's order or inconsistent Tribal rules supersede them, and they apply the customs of the nation where consistent with the regulations.[^cfr]

### 1.2 Three patterns in written rules

The nations' own texts read for this spike fall into three patterns (project analysis; the counts come from a machine pass over the project's captures and are not checked by a person):[^machine-pass]

| Pattern | What the text does | Examples read |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Own evidence code | A full code enacted by the nation, often modeled on the federal rules, with its own changes | Pascua Yaqui (1991, recodified 2006); Oneida Nation; Saint Regis Mohawk (2008); Miami Tribe of Oklahoma; Wyandotte Nation (2024); Blue Lake Rancheria[^own-codes] |
| Federal or state rules adopted or used as a guide | The Federal Rules govern, or are a guide or gap filler; one nation adopts a state's rules | Hopi (federal rules, except where they conflict with Hopi law and tradition); Swinomish; Three Affiliated Tribes; Stockbridge-Munsee (guide only); Lac Courte Oreilles (federal and Wisconsin rules persuasive, not controlling); Penobscot (Maine rules)[^adopt] |
| Not bound by formal rules | The court admits what is relevant and reliable, without common law, state or federal rules | Warm Springs; Karuk; Lower Elwha Klallam[^not-bound] |

The machine pass found passages that adopt the Federal Rules for some or all proceedings in 16 nations' captures, use them as a guide in 7, and use or adopt a state's rules in 2 (Michigan as a guide for Grand Traverse Band; Maine for Penobscot).[^machine-pass] It also found 40 nations whose texts say the court is not bound by formal rules in at least some proceedings.

### 1.3 Rules are often relaxed in informal hearings

Many codes switch formal rules off for particular proceedings: juvenile dispositions, child welfare hearings, exclusion hearings, enrollment appeals, employment rights (TERO) hearings, workers' compensation appeals and gaming license revocations.[^machine-pass] Wyandotte lets the court relax some rules for parties without counsel in civil disputes, and Rincon adopts the Federal Rules but lets the court relax or suspend them.[^own-codes][^machine-pass] One gaming example points the other way: Redding Rancheria's dispute procedure for gaming patrons' claims applies the Federal Rules at its evidentiary hearing.[^machine-pass]

Project analysis: for the model code this means an ADR provision cannot assume any one evidence regime will apply when a matter goes back to court. It has to say what it protects in its own words.

### 1.4 Proving custom and tradition

Custom raises an evidence problem every Tribal court that applies customary law must solve: who says what the custom is. The texts read use five methods, often in combination (project analysis):

- **Elders' advice to the court.** Karuk lets the court ask elders or other credible people familiar with custom; Fletcher collects similar White Earth, Oglala Sioux, Bay Mills and Winnebago provisions.[^karuk][^fletcher] The machine pass found elder-advice provisions in 31 nations' captures.
- **Elder or expert testimony.** Northern Cheyenne's draft evidence title would let custom be used as evidence, proven by an expert, defined as an elder or other person the community recognizes, or by the Chief Judge, with the custom stated in the findings.[^nc] The Navajo Nation Supreme Court in *In re Estate of Belone* listed three routes: recorded opinions and treatises, judicial notice of custom generally known in the community, and expert testimony, including from people who learned custom orally; it suggested a pretrial conference with court-appointed experts where custom is disputed.[^belone]
- **Party pleading.** *Belone* also requires a party relying on custom to plead it.[^belone]
- **Referral to a traditional body.** Ho-Chunk's civil rules let parties consent to have the Traditional Court decide matters of custom, and let the Trial Court ask the Traditional Court for help.[^ho-chunk] The Tlingit and Haida code authorizes referral to a Court of Elders.[^machine-pass]
- **Free admission.** Warm Springs admits relevant evidence of custom freely.[^not-bound]

Fletcher reports that a formal hearing procedure for unwritten custom at Hoopa Valley had, in practice, kept customary law from being applied.[^fletcher] The Navajo rules for declaratory rulings on questions of Navajo law include rules headed "Evidence in Customary Law Cases" and "Sacred Matters," which the spike could see only as headings.[^navajo-decl]

### 1.5 Privileges and protected knowledge

Several codes extend ordinary privileges to traditional roles. Pascua Yaqui and the North Dakota model rules extend the physician privilege to traditional healers and the clergy privilege to traditional spiritual leaders; Oneida, Absentee Shawnee and Wyandotte name Native American Church roadmen, firekeepers or medicine people; Lac Courte Oreilles has a spiritual advisor privilege; Puyallup and Tulalip add traditional spiritual advisors to the clergy privilege.[^own-codes][^privileges] The machine pass found such privileges in 13 nations' captures.[^machine-pass]

Colville goes further. Its evidence rules make evidence inadmissible if it would require a participant to share protected traditional teachings, including those shared in peacemaking, and say this rests on the protected nature of the material, not on an ordinary privilege.[^colville-sacred]

### 1.6 The first survey: Morote (2026)

Morote sorts Tribal evidence codes into three approaches to the Federal Rules: full acceptance, modified adoption and full rejection.[^morote] The article names examples but does not say how many codes it read or how it chose them.[^morote-method] Its points that matter here:

- **Full acceptance** is attractive because the federal rules are tested and federal courts read an identical code faithfully, but a nation that copies them may be left with an outdated version and should say how federal case law applies (Swinomish makes it persuasive).[^morote-full]
- **Modified adoption** is the largest and most varied group. Saint Regis Mohawk cuts the hearsay exceptions from 23 to six; Seminole writes Tribal governments into self-authentication and expands privileges; Tulalip extends the medical-expenses rule to protect gestures of sympathy; Northern Cheyenne admits custom and tradition through elder experts; Pit River adds a hearsay exception for Tribal oral history.[^morote-modified]
- **Full rejection** is illustrated by Coos, whose code is not bound by common law, state or federal rules and admits hearsay when the court finds it credible.[^morote-reject] (Warm Springs uses the same words; see section 1.2.)
- **Federal courts and Tribal documents.** The article notes *United States v. Alvirez*, holding that Tribal documents are not self-authenticating under the Federal Rules, and a proposal before the federal Advisory Committee to change that.[^alvirez] Project analysis: this bears on proving a Tribal agreement, award or judgment in a state or federal court, a topic for the enforcement commentary.
- **Recommendations.** It urges information sharing among Tribal courts, possibly an intertribal model evidence code on the pattern of the Model Penal Code, and guides for litigants where a code departs from the federal rules.[^morote-recs]

The article does not cover mediation or peacemaking confidentiality, compromise offers (beyond the Tulalip example), arbitration, or how evidence works outside the courtroom (project analysis, from a full reading).

## 2. Evidence in Tribal dispute resolution and peacemaking

### 2.1 What the provisions protect

Of the 85 provisions with a stated confidentiality rule, a keyword reading of their summaries (a floor, not a count of features) shows this spread:[^provisions]

| Design feature in the summary | Provisions | Nations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Statements inadmissible or not to be used later | 36 | 25 |
| Juvenile, child or youth setting | 26 | 16 |
| Called "privileged" | 13 | 11 |
| Waiver or release only in writing | 10 | 8 |
| Neutral cannot be called or compelled to disclose | 9 | 5 |
| Child abuse or neglect reporting exception | 9 | 6 |
| Treated as settlement negotiations | 7 | 7 |
| Non-disclosure agreement or oath | 6 | 4 |
| No record kept, or notes destroyed | 6 | 5 |

### 2.2 Four drafting models

Reading the provisions and the full texts checked for this spike, four models recur (project analysis):

1. **Settlement-negotiation analogy.** Peacemaking statements are treated like settlement talks and kept out of later proceedings: Chickasaw, Choctaw Nation of Oklahoma, Alabama-Coushatta, Mashpee Wampanoag, Coquille and the Confederated Tribes of Coos, Lower Umpqua and Siuslaw.[^settlement] This is the closest analogue to Federal Rule of Evidence 408, which bars compromise evidence only to prove or disprove a claim.[^fre408] The Coos chapter, in the 2026 text posted for first reading, also says the privilege does not reach communications made for other purposes, lets a Peacegiver testify only with all parties' permission and the court's consent, and provides contempt for breaches.[^coos]
2. **Evidence-code privilege.** The rule sits in the nation's evidence code: Lac Courte Oreilles bars admission and discovery of mediation and peacemaking communications in any judicial or administrative proceeding, with a child abuse reporting exception; Colville and Ely Shoshone also place their rules in evidence titles.[^lco][^provisions]
3. **Full confidentiality with listed exceptions.** Ponca makes negotiation and mediation statements confidential, privileged and inadmissible unless waived, with exceptions for signed agreements, threats of violence, crimes, abuse or neglect and claims of mediator misconduct.[^provisions] This is the closest to the Uniform Mediation Act's structure of privilege, waiver and exceptions.[^uma]
4. **Process secrecy.** Mashantucket Pequot requires non-disclosure agreements from peacemakers, staff and participants and destroys notes after review; Kake bars recording of circles; Spirit Lake bars calling the peacemaker or coordinator as a witness; Navajo's peacemaking plan makes contacts confidential except for abuse reports, but not the signed agreement.[^provisions][^navajo-plan]

Two points matter for drafting (project analysis):

- **The agreement itself is usually not confidential.** Navajo, Potawatomi, Mashantucket and Coos make the signed agreement fileable or enforceable even where everything else is protected.[^navajo-plan][^potawatomi][^coos]
- **Reports to the court are a leak point.** Codes differ on what a neutral may tell the judge: Yurok sends nothing about the mediation itself; Coos sends a status report without confidential communications; Potawatomi lets the judge ask for status reports.[^yurok][^coos][^potawatomi]

### 2.3 The Uniform Mediation Act as a comparison

The Uniform Mediation Act was approved by the Uniform Law Commission in 2001 and the American Bar Association in 2002; Ohio was the fourth state to enact it, in 2005.[^ohio-uma] One secondary source counted 10 states and the District of Columbia as of 2011.[^rsi-uma] No Native nation's code read for this spike cites the Act or Rule 408 by name.[^coos-yurok-note] Whether any nation has adopted the Act, and whether its definitions reach Tribal mediation, were not checked; both are on the pull list.

## 3. Does this relate to the model code?

Project analysis:

- **Yes, at the seams.** Every ADR or peacemaking provision the project has collected either protects what is said or is silent. Silence leaves the answer to whatever evidence regime the court uses, which section 1 shows can be the Federal Rules, a state's rules, the nation's own code or no formal rules at all. A model provision that states its own confidentiality rule avoids that dependence, as the Uniform Mediation Act does.
- **Yes, for custom.** Where a model provision lets a neutral or a court apply the nation's custom, commentary should point to the proof methods in section 1.4 and to the protection of sacred material, without prescribing one.
- **No, for the rest.** Hearsay, authentication, character evidence and the other bulk of an evidence code govern trials, not dispute resolution.
- **Gaming tie-in.** Gaming patron claims and gaming license hearings already show both regimes (Federal Rules at Redding Rancheria; formal rules off for revocation hearings), so the gaming chapter may need a cross-reference.

## 4. Limits

- The machine pass read passages of about 700 characters around loose keyword matches in 1,616 captured text layers. It did not read 1,753 scanned PDFs without text, or ecode360 and Municode codes, which refuse scripts.[^tribal-law-texts] Its classifications are in [evidence-spike-passages.csv](../tribal-law/evidence-spike-passages.csv), unchecked by a person.
- The Navajo rules of evidence and declaratory ruling rules were seen only as tables of contents. The Karuk rule was read only through a fetch tool, because the code site refuses automated downloads. Northern Cheyenne's evidence title is a draft for public comment. The Umatilla rules hosted by the Department of Justice carry a placeholder where the adopting authority should be.
- Morote's article, the only survey found, was read in full from a copy Trevor supplied (the repository serves a browser challenge to the cloud session); the private copy is in the project's working files.
- Nothing here says how often these rules are used or how courts read them, apart from *Belone* and *Arakawa*.
