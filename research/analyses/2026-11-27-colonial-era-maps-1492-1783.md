# Map data from 1492 to 1783

Research note, Nov. 27, 2026. Question from Trevor: can the Map page's history reach back before 1783, to 1492 if possible, and show the first settlers? Research only; nothing is built here.

## 1. Short answer

Yes, back to 1629 with a dataset of legal standing, and to the 1500s only as hand-placed points. The Newberry Library source the Map page already uses for state and territory borders has a companion county file that starts on March 4, 1629, the Massachusetts Bay charter, with dated county creations and changes through 1783 (Newberry Library, U.S. Historical Counties metadata, Beginning_Date 16290304, https://digital.newberry.org/ahcb/documents/US_HistCounties_Metadata2.htm). The Library lets anyone reuse the Atlas files "for any lawful purpose, commercial or non-commercial, without licensing or permission fees" (https://publications.newberry.org/ahcb/downloads/united_states.html). Before 1629 there is no boundary data worth drawing: European presence from 1492 to the 1620s was a handful of posts and missions (St. Augustine 1565, Santa Fe about 1610, Jamestown 1607, Quebec 1608, Plymouth 1620), which suit a points layer we compile from records ourselves.

No source gives a defensible single map of Native territories "at contact." Contact came at different times in different places, centuries apart, and the best federal map says so (section 3.4). The site should not draw a "1492" map of Native lands. It should keep showing findings with their maker and date, the nation's own account first.

Royce's cessions do not help before 1784: the earliest cession date in our `site/geography/land-cessions.json` (717 cessions, built from the Forest Service tracing of Royce 1899) is October 22, 1784, the Treaty of Fort Stanwix (our analysis of that file). Royce's own 1887 Cherokee study does cover earlier cessions (section 3.3).

## 3. Notes on each source

### 3.1 Newberry counties

The county file is the same project, the same terms and the same date fields the `borders` layer already uses (`site/tools/build-historic-maps.mjs`, lines 7 and 57-58). Its limits, from the Library's own metadata: the large, indefinitely bounded areas "in western Virginia, New York, and the New England states during much of the seventeenth century" are left empty rather than drawn; and "areas under the jurisdiction of foreign governments after the colonial period were excluded," which leaves out Spanish Texas and Florida and British Florida. French and Spanish colonies are therefore missing. The map must say what a county was: a colony's claim of jurisdiction over land that Native nations held, not settled or empty land. A colonial county is not evidence of a lawful transfer.

### 3.2 Boundary lines from 1763 to 1783

No public domain vector exists for the 1763 Proclamation line or the treaty lines of 1768 to 1775 that we could find. Tracing from period maps in the Library of Congress gives us lines we own, credited to the map they came from, as in the 1909 pilot. The Proclamation itself is a legal source the site can link: the line it drew is one of the first legal limits on colonial settlement of Native land.

### 3.3 Cessions before 1784

Royce's 1887 Cherokee study is the one federal source that maps pre-1784 cessions in detail. Its key lists ten before 1784: 1721 and 1755 to South Carolina; 1768 and 1773 to the British Superintendent of Indian Affairs; 1770 at Lochaber; 1772 to Virginia; 1775 to Richard Henderson and others; 1777 to South Carolina and Georgia and, separately, to Virginia and North Carolina; and May 31, 1783 to Georgia (Royce 1887, cession key, read on Project Gutenberg, https://gutenberg.org/ebooks/46493). Colonial deeds and treaties elsewhere (Pennsylvania's purchases, the Iroquois cessions at Albany and Fort Stanwix in 1768, Virginia's and the Carolinas' treaties) have no single traced dataset; each would be traced from period maps and the treaty texts, nation by nation.

### 3.4 Native territories and CARE

The CARE principles (collective benefit, authority to control, responsibility, ethics) and the charter's commitment 6, that a nation may ask that material about it be restricted or removed and its request wins (`docs/charter.md`; see decision record 0056), apply in full. The 1967 USGS map shows why one "contact" map misleads: its own note dates its boundaries from the 1600s in the Southeast to the late 1800s elsewhere. The site already shows the Indian Claims Commission areas as dashed findings, credited, never as borders; colonial-era layers should follow the same rule, and a nation's own map, when it offers one, goes first and may replace ours. The nation can ask us to withhold a layer for its lands.

## 4. Cost

Everything recommended to build (Newberry counties, 20 to 40 Library of Congress scans, the Royce 1887 plates) fits in about 1 GB of R2, roughly $0.015 a month at R2's storage price (our estimate from the file sizes above). Tile sets built from the county file would add a few megabytes. The real cost is tracing time for the lines in 3.2 and 3.3.
