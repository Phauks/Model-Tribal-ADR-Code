# Self-Governance and Self-Determination Programs: What They Are, Who Takes Part and How to Show Them on Nation Pages

**Status:** research study, written Nov. 26, 2026, in answer to Trevor's request for "a list and information relating to native american self-governance programs" and his follow-up to "somehow include it in the info for tribes" (project thread "Gather tribal self-governance programs", Nov. 26, 2026). Nothing here is built; the recommendation in section 5 and the page proposal in section 6 wait for Trevor. The participant lists gathered for this study are in [`research/self-governance/2026-11-26/`](../self-governance/2026-11-26/README.md). Every count below names its source and date; a count marked "(counted)" is the project's own count of a list retrieved on Nov. 26, 2026.

## 1. Short answer

Federal law lets Native nations take over, and run under their own priorities, programs that a federal agency would otherwise run for them. The Congressional Research Service groups these under the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act (ISDEAA) and a set of later laws that borrow its contracting and compacting model ([CRS, R48256, Jan. 10, 2025](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-01-10_R48256_1e2ac650f3a833d81f7d7f3051da5e0f984aba11.html), pp. 1-2, 14-20). Eleven programs fit that description, and two more (Tribal TANF and Tribal Title IV-E) let a nation run a program directly in place of the state.

## 2. The Programs

### 2.1 Self-Determination Contracts (ISDEAA Title I)

The Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975 (Pub. L. No. 93-638, 25 U.S.C. §§ 5301 et seq.) let Interior and Health and Human Services contract with nations and Tribal organizations to run programs the departments would otherwise run themselves ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-01-10_R48256_1e2ac650f3a833d81f7d7f3051da5e0f984aba11.html), p. 5). The contracts are called "638 contracts" after the public law number. If a proposal meets the statute's criteria the agency must award the contract within 90 days, and either side can end it: the agency can reassume a program for endangerment or "gross negligence or mismanagement," and the nation can retrocede it ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-01-10_R48256_1e2ac650f3a833d81f7d7f3051da5e0f984aba11.html), pp. 5-6, citing 25 U.S.C. §§ 5321, 5330 and 25 C.F.R. §§ 900.240-.245).

The Assistant Secretary for Indian Affairs testified that in March 2024, 526 of 574 nations (92 percent) had self-determination contracts with Interior; the Bureau of Indian Affairs manages more than 9,500 of them ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-01-10_R48256_1e2ac650f3a833d81f7d7f3051da5e0f984aba11.html), pp. 1, 5, citing the Assistant Secretary's [testimony of March 6, 2024](https://naturalresources.house.gov/uploadedfiles/testimony_newland_624.pdf) and the Bureau's fiscal year 2025 budget justification). The Indian Health Service had 246 contracts and annual funding agreements with 206 nations and Tribal organizations, and Tribes administer over 60 percent of the Service's budget ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-01-10_R48256_1e2ac650f3a833d81f7d7f3051da5e0f984aba11.html), p. 6, citing the Service's fiscal year 2025 justification, p. 221).

The contracts carry two funding guarantees that have reached the Supreme Court: contract support costs ([*Salazar v. Ramah Navajo Chapter*, 567 U.S. 182 (2012)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/11-551)) and support costs on third-party payments such as Medicaid ([*Becerra v. San Carlos Apache Tribe*, 602 U.S. 222 (2024)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/23-250)) ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-01-10_R48256_1e2ac650f3a833d81f7d7f3051da5e0f984aba11.html), pp. 12-14).

**Per-nation list.** No list of Interior contractors by nation was found. For the Indian Health Service, contract payments are reported under Assistance Listing 93.441 ("Indian Self-Determination"); USAspending shows 197 recipients and $1,039 million in fiscal year 2025 obligations (counted, [USAspending API](https://api.usaspending.gov/), recipient totals for Oct. 1, 2024 to Sept. 30, 2025).

### 2.2 Interior Self-Governance Compacts (ISDEAA Title IV)

Congress began self-governance as a demonstration in 1988 (Pub. L. No. 100-472, title III) and made it permanent at Interior in the Tribal Self-Governance Act of 1994 (Pub. L. No. 103-413) ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-01-10_R48256_1e2ac650f3a833d81f7d7f3051da5e0f984aba11.html), pp. 7-8). A compact differs from a contract in scope and freedom: a compacting nation signs one compact with the whole department, negotiates annual funding agreements bureau by bureau, and can "redesign or consolidate" programs and move funds within a year without federal approval ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-01-10_R48256_1e2ac650f3a833d81f7d7f3051da5e0f984aba11.html), pp. 8-9). To enter, a nation must finish a planning phase, request participation by resolution, and show three years of clean audits under its existing contracts; Interior may admit up to 50 new nations a year (25 U.S.C. § 5362; [CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-01-10_R48256_1e2ac650f3a833d81f7d7f3051da5e0f984aba11.html), p. 9).

The PROGRESS for Indian Tribes Act ([Pub. L. No. 116-180](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/plaw/116/public/180?link-type=html)) rewrote title IV in 2020, and Interior's final rule carrying it out appeared on Dec. 11, 2024 ([89 Fed. Reg. 100228](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/12/11/2024-28302/self-governance-progress-act-regulations), amending 25 C.F.R. pt. 1000).

**Who takes part.** The Office of Self-Governance's list of July 24, 2024 has 140 entries with each one's first year and region; 11 are consortia, and the list gives the number of member governments for each (13 for the Aleutian Pribilof Islands Association, 51 for the Association of Village Council Presidents, 33 for Tanana Chiefs Conference), 177 in all ([list](https://www.bia.gov/sites/default/files/media_document/2024_self_governance_tribes_alphabetically_as_of_07.24.24.pdf); counted). That fits Interior's estimate of 306 nations under 138 compacts in 2024 ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-01-10_R48256_1e2ac650f3a833d81f7d7f3051da5e0f984aba11.html), p. 8, citing the fiscal year 2025 budget justification) and the Assistant Secretary's figure of 295 nations (51 percent) in March 2024 (same, p. 1). The office's own page says self-governance nations are "approximately 50 percent" of federally recognized nations ([Office of Self-Governance](https://www.bia.gov/as-ia/osg)).

The dollar figures disagree. The office's page, not updated since 2021, says it would distribute "approximately $1.2 billion to 285 tribes" that year ([Office of Self-Governance](https://www.bia.gov/as-ia/osg)); the 2025 budget justification estimated about $700 million in 2024 ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-01-10_R48256_1e2ac650f3a833d81f7d7f3051da5e0f984aba11.html), p. 8); USAspending shows $1,230 million in fiscal year 2025 obligations to 143 recipients under Assistance Listing 15.022 (counted). The project has not worked out which costs each figure counts.

**Other Interior bureaus.** Each year Interior publishes in the Federal Register the programs of its other bureaus that self-governance nations may compact, and the agreements in force. For fiscal year 2024 the notice lists funding agreements with the Bureau of Land Management (2 nations), the Bureau of Reclamation (4), the National Park Service (7 agreements with 6 nations, such as Santa Clara Pueblo at Bandelier National Monument and Valles Caldera National Preserve), the Fish and Wildlife Service (1), the Geological Survey (1), the Bureau of Trust Fund Administration (2) and the Appraisal and Valuation Services Office (30); the Office of Natural Resources Revenue had none ([89 Fed. Reg. 103858, 103859-60 (Dec. 19, 2024)](https://govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-12-19/pdf/2024-30252.pdf); counted).

### 2.3 Indian Health Service Self-Governance Compacts (ISDEAA Title V)

Self-governance came to the Indian Health Service as a demonstration in 1992 (Pub. L. No. 102-573) and became permanent in the Tribal Self-Governance Amendments of 2000 (Pub. L. No. 106-260, 25 U.S.C. §§ 5381 et seq.) ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-01-10_R48256_1e2ac650f3a833d81f7d7f3051da5e0f984aba11.html), pp. 7, 10). The entry rules match title IV. A compacting nation or Tribal organization may take its share of any Service program, including area and headquarters functions, and the Service may reassume a program only on written notice and a hearing, for imminent danger to public health or gross mismanagement ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-01-10_R48256_1e2ac650f3a833d81f7d7f3051da5e0f984aba11.html), pp. 10-11, citing 25 U.S.C. §§ 5383-5387).

**Who takes part.** The Office of Tribal Self-Governance lists 149 nations and Tribal organizations, by Indian Health Service Area, each with the fiscal year it entered, and states that "As of July 1, 2025, the IHS has entered into 120 Compacts and 147 Funding Agreements with Self-Governance tribes and tribal organizations across all 12 IHS Areas" ([Indian Health Service](https://www.ihs.gov/selfgovernance/tribes/); counted). Many entries are Tribal organizations acting for several nations: in the Alaska Area, 26 entries include Southcentral Foundation and the Alaska Native Tribal Health Consortium, and in the Navajo Area, all three are health corporations (same list). The CRS reports that "More than two-thirds of Tribes participate in Title V programs" ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-01-10_R48256_1e2ac650f3a833d81f7d7f3051da5e0f984aba11.html), p. 1). USAspending shows 151 recipients and $3,801 million in fiscal year 2025 obligations under Assistance Listing 93.210 (counted). The page carried a banner on Nov. 26, 2026 saying its information "may not be up to date" because of the federal funding situation, so the list may lag.

The project already cites this list on 104 nation pages, inside the note of the `indian_health_service` fact (source `indian-health-service-self-governance-tribes`; counted from `content/tribes/`), but not as a fact of its own.

### 2.4 Self-Governance Elsewhere in Health and Human Services (ISDEAA Title VI)

Title VI only directed a study of self-governance in Health and Human Services agencies other than the Indian Health Service. The 2003 study found it feasible; a later tribal-federal workgroup reached the same view, but further legislation would be needed and "No such legislation has been enacted to date" ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-01-10_R48256_1e2ac650f3a833d81f7d7f3051da5e0f984aba11.html), pp. 11-12). There is no program to list.

### 2.5 Tribally Controlled School Grants

Under the Tribally Controlled Schools Act of 1988 (Pub. L. No. 100-297, 25 U.S.C. §§ 2501-2511), a nation or Tribal organization may run a Bureau of Indian Education school under one grant that combines the Bureau's and the Department of Education's formula funds, with administrative cost grants in place of contract support costs ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-01-10_R48256_1e2ac650f3a833d81f7d7f3051da5e0f984aba11.html), pp. 19-20). About two-thirds of the Bureau's schools ran under these grants by fiscal year 2023, out of 169 elementary and secondary schools and 14 dormitories (same, pp. 3, 20). The rules are in [25 C.F.R. pt. 44](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-25/chapter-I/subchapter-E/part-44). A per-school list of operators (grant, contract or Bureau-run) was not looked for in this pass; the Bureau's school directory is the likely source (project analysis).

### 2.6 "477" Plans

The Indian Employment, Training and Related Services Demonstration Act of 1992 (Pub. L. No. 102-477) let a nation merge funds from several federal employment and training programs into one plan with one budget and one report; the 2017 amendments (Pub. L. No. 115-93) made it permanent, extended it to eight more departments (12 in all) and gave the Secretary of the Interior sole authority to approve plans ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-01-10_R48256_1e2ac650f3a833d81f7d7f3051da5e0f984aba11.html), pp. 15-16; [Bureau of Indian Affairs](https://www.bia.gov/bia/ois/dwd/477Initiative/477PlanDirectory)). A nation may ask to carry its plan under an ISDEAA agreement (25 U.S.C. § 3412), and Interior's data service records each plan as either a "Self-Governance Compact Agreement" or a "Self-Determination Contract Agreement" (counted below).

**Who takes part.** Interior's 477 Plan Directory is backed by public map services. On Nov. 26, 2026 they held 84 plans (72 nations and 12 consortia; 49 under self-governance compacts and 35 under self-determination contracts), with start and end dates, and a second table of 686 plan-program pairs naming each federal program merged into each plan, 62 programs in all ([plans service](https://biamaps.geoplatform.gov/server/rest/services/Hosted/PL102_477_Contracts/FeatureServer/0), [programs service](https://biamaps.geoplatform.gov/server/rest/services/Hosted/PL102_477_PartnerEnrollment/FeatureServer/0); counted). The CRS figure for 2024 was 78 plans with 298 nations across 38 programs ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-01-10_R48256_1e2ac650f3a833d81f7d7f3051da5e0f984aba11.html), p. 16). The plans service also holds the names and email addresses of Tribal leaders and federal officers; the study did not copy them, and a build must not (charter, commitment 1; [data model, Contacts](../../docs/data-model.md#contacts)).

### 2.7 Tribal Transportation Self-Governance Program

Section 1121 of the FAST Act (Pub. L. No. 114-94, 23 U.S.C. § 207) set up self-governance compacts at the Department of Transportation, borrowing ISDEAA's title I and IV terms ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-01-10_R48256_1e2ac650f3a833d81f7d7f3051da5e0f984aba11.html), p. 17). The final rule is at [85 Fed. Reg. 33494 (June 1, 2020)](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2020/06/01/2020-11618/tribal-transportation-self-governance-program), now 49 C.F.R. pt. 29.

**Who takes part.** The Cherokee Nation signed the first compact in 2022, and Ohkay Owingeh, the Citizen Potawatomi Nation and the Forest County Potawatomi Community followed by August 2024 ([ICT News, Aug. 20, 2024](https://ictnews.org/news/potawatomi-tribe-signs-self-governing-compact-with-transportation-department)). The Catawba Nation signed in February 2025, "making the Catawba Nation only the fifth" ([WRHI, Feb. 17, 2025](https://www.wrhi.com/2025/02/catawba-nation-signs-historic-self-governance-compact-with-usdot-196558)). On June 30, 2026 the Central Council of the Tlingit & Haida Indian Tribes announced its intent to join, "becoming one of nine Tribes participating nationwide" ([Tlingit & Haida](https://tlingitandhaida.gov/news/jerry-hope-navigates-tlingit-haida-through-the-federal-tribal-transportation-self-governance-program/)), which puts the others at eight; the three not named above were not identified in this pass. The Department's own program pages and its June 16, 2026 program guide refused requests from the cloud (HTTP 403), so the official list is a hand pull.

### 2.8 FDPIR Self-Determination Demonstration

Section 4003(b) of the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018 (Pub. L. No. 115-334) let the Food and Nutrition Service sign 638 contracts with Tribal organizations that run the Food Distribution Program on Indian Reservations, so they can buy some foods for the package themselves; the foods must be domestic, must replace (not add to) foods in the package, and must be of "similar or higher nutritional value" ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-01-10_R48256_1e2ac650f3a833d81f7d7f3051da5e0f984aba11.html), pp. 17-18; [Food and Nutrition Service](https://www.fns.usda.gov/fdpir/self-determination)). The program itself serves "nearly 100,000 citizens of 276 Tribes" ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-01-10_R48256_1e2ac650f3a833d81f7d7f3051da5e0f984aba11.html), p. 18, citing 2024 testimony).

**Who takes part.** The Service's page names each project, the foods bought and the vendors. Round 1 (from October 2021) has seven projects for eight Tribal organizations: the Oneida Nation and the Menominee Indian Tribe of Wisconsin jointly, the Little Traverse Bay Bands of Odawa Indians, the Red Cliff Band of Lake Superior Chippewa, the Chickasaw Nation, the Alaska Native Tribal Health Consortium, the Lummi Nation and the Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians. Round 2 (from August 2023) has eight: the Cherokee Nation, the Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation, the Gila River Indian Community, the Quinault Indian Nation, the Sokaogon Chippewa Community, the Spirit Lake Tribe, the Tohono O'odham Nation and the White Mountain Apache Tribe ([Food and Nutrition Service](https://www.fns.usda.gov/fdpir/self-determination); counted). The page now gives round 1 as "just under $7.0 million" where the CRS gave $5.7 million; round 2 is $4.4 million in both.

This study found no comparable self-determination authority for the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, and the CRS report describes none ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-01-10_R48256_1e2ac650f3a833d81f7d7f3051da5e0f984aba11.html), pp. 17-18); a further search could confirm it.

### 2.9 Tribal Forest Protection Act 638 Agreements

The Tribal Forest Protection Act of 2004 (Pub. L. No. 108-278, [25 U.S.C. § 3115a](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/25/3115a)) lets a nation propose projects on Forest Service or Bureau of Land Management land that borders its trust or restricted forest land. Section 8703 of the 2018 farm bill ([25 U.S.C. § 3115b](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/25/3115b), "Tribal forest management demonstration project") let both agencies use 638 contracts for those projects ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-01-10_R48256_1e2ac650f3a833d81f7d7f3051da5e0f984aba11.html), pp. 18-19). The Forest Service told the CRS it had signed 22 agreements worth $41.7 million by November 2023, 13 of them in fiscal year 2023 (same, p. 19). The Forest Service's page lists its fiscal year 2024 projects by national forest and nation, such as the Organized Village of Kake on the Tongass and the Southern Ute Indian Tribe in Colorado, without saying which are 638 agreements ([Forest Service](https://www.fs.usda.gov/es/node/761889642)).

Co-stewardship and shared decision-making agreements under the 2021 joint secretarial order are related but separate; the Government Accountability Office reviewed about 40 of them in January 2026 ([GAO-26-106626](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-26-106626/index.html)). They could be a later collection (project analysis).

### 2.10 Indian Housing Block Grants (NAHASDA)

NAHASDA ([Pub. L. No. 104-330](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/plaw/104/public/330?link-type=html), 25 U.S.C. §§ 4101 et seq.) provides housing funds as a block grant that each nation, or the Tribally designated housing entity it names, spends under its own housing plan ([24 C.F.R. pt. 1000](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-24/subtitle-B/chapter-IX/part-1000)). The CRS treats it as a self-determination authority outside ISDEAA ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-01-10_R48256_1e2ac650f3a833d81f7d7f3051da5e0f984aba11.html), p. 2 n.10).

**Who takes part.** HUD's fiscal year 2026 formula list names 594 recipients by state and city with each final allocation, totaling $1,126,213,956, which matches the list's own total line ([HUD](https://www.hud.gov/sites/default/files/PIH/documents/FY26-IHBG-Formula-Allocations-Congressional-Notification.pdf); counted). Some recipients are regional bodies, such as AHTNA, Incorporated and the Aleut Corporation in Alaska. The project already matched the fiscal year 2025 list to 566 nation pages for its enrollment figures (source `hud-indian-housing-block-grant-fy2025-final-allocation`; counted from `content/tribes/`), so most of the matching is done.

### 2.11 Programs a Nation Runs in Place of the State

Two programs are not self-determination contracts, but a nation that takes them on runs the program itself, as a state would. Under Tribal TANF (42 U.S.C. § 612; 45 C.F.R. pt. 286), a nation sets its own service area, eligibility, time limits and work rules; the Office of Family Assistance reports 76 programs serving "more than 285 federally recognized Tribes and Alaska Native Villages" as of 2022 (the same page also says 75 as of June 16, 2022) ([Office of Family Assistance](https://acf.gov/ofa/programs/tribal/tribal-tanf)). Under 42 U.S.C. § 679c, a nation may run its own Title IV-E foster care, adoption and guardianship program; the Children's Bureau publishes the approved nations as a data set ([data.gov](https://catalog-old.data.gov/dataset/tribes-with-approved-title-iv-e-plans)), which the [jurisdiction session of Nov. 23, 2026](../sessions/2026/2026-11-23-jurisdiction-and-federal-ties.md) did not cover for its `child-welfare-agreement` facts. The 477 table above shows that Tribal TANF, the Child Care and Development Fund and Title IV-B funds are also often merged into 477 plans ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-01-10_R48256_1e2ac650f3a833d81f7d7f3051da5e0f984aba11.html), p. 16).

## 3. Who Takes Part: The Lists and How Well They Match Nation Pages

The study saved each list it could fetch, as data, in [`research/self-governance/2026-11-26/`](../self-governance/2026-11-26/README.md), and tried a quick name match against the 579 names and aliases of federally recognized nations in `content/tribes/`. "Exact" means the names agree after removing punctuation and case; "loose" means they agree after also removing common words such as Tribe, Band, Indians and state names; "close" is a near spelling. Loose and close matches are candidates, not facts (project analysis).

| List | Rows | Exact | Loose or close | No match | Why the rest did not match |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Interior self-governance, July 24, 2024 | 140 | 48 | 53 | 39 | Consortia (11) and spellings such as "Bois Forte Band Of Chippewa" |
| Indian Health Service self-governance, July 1, 2025 | 149 | 39 | 51 | 59 | Mostly health corporations and consortia |
| 477 plans | 84 | 66 | 1 | 17 | Consortia, and Minnesota Chippewa bands written as "Minnesota Chippewa Tribe, Minnesota (Mille Lacs Band)" |
| HUD housing block grants, fiscal year 2026 | 594 | 101 | 315 | 178 | Short names ("Akiachak") and housing entities; the 2025 crosswalk on nation pages covers most of these |
| USAspending, fiscal year 2025, four programs | 143 to 375 each | 46 to 105 | 40 to 69 | 29 to 201 | Recipient names in capitals, housing authorities and health corporations |

The lists name a consortium or health corporation, not its members, in more than a third of the self-governance rows. To credit a member nation, the build needs each consortium's member list. The Interior list gives only the count; the members' names would come from the consortia themselves (the Interior list counts 33 member governments for Tanana Chiefs Conference) and, for the Alaska Tribal Health Compact, by the Indian Health Service in its funding agreements (project analysis; not yet checked one by one). Members would show as taking part "through" the consortium.

## 4. What Exists Already

The site already holds the laws: Title 25 chapters 36 (477), 43 (NAHASDA) and 46 (ISDEAA) and C.F.R. Title 25 parts 44, 900 and 1000 have records (`usc-25-chapter-36`, `usc-25-chapter-43`, `usc-25-chapter-46`, `cfr-25-part-44`, `cfr-25-part-900`, `cfr-25-part-1000` in `content/sources/`). The Indian Health Service list sits in notes on 104 nation pages, and the HUD lists feed the population facts on 566 (section 2.3 and 2.10). No nation page says which programs the nation runs.

## 5. Recommendation: Make It a Collection

The project recommends a small collection, "Self-governance and federal programs," in tier 1 (project analysis). The reasons: the participant lists are federal government works that can be served; four of them are machine-readable or a single table and can be rescraped by script each year; and the answer to "does this nation run its own health, housing, roads or job programs" is a basic fact of its government that no nation page shows today.

It would have two parts. The first is about 13 program pages (one per row of the table in section 1, with Interior's non-BIA bureaus as a section of the title IV page), each in the shared InfoPage layout with the statute, rules, agency, a short account and a table of participants. The second is per-nation facts, a new `federal_programs` list on each profile (section 6.1), about 1,500 to 2,500 facts from the official lists (project estimate: 594 housing recipients, about 306 Interior self-governance nations, 149 Indian Health Service compactors and their members, 84 477 plans, and the smaller programs).

The cost is low for the lists that are files or data services (Interior self-governance, Indian Health Service, 477, HUD, USAspending, the Federal Register notice, Food and Nutrition Service): about one day of scripts, then a small-model pass over unmatched names, with a person settling consortium members (project estimate). The Department of Transportation list, Forest Service 638 agreements, tribally controlled schools, Tribal TANF and Title IV-E need hand pulls or a second search. Each list gets a source watcher, since all of them change yearly (decision record 0059). Hosting is Serve: every list is a United States government work.

The row added to [docs/data-roadmap.md](../../docs/data-roadmap.md) marks the collection Spiked, and [docs/roadmap.md](../../docs/roadmap.md) has a Parked research row.

## 6. Proposal for Nation Pages

### 6.1 The Data

A new profile list, `federal_programs`, one fact per program per source, with the usual citation fields (`source`, `confidence`, `verification`, `as_of`, `locator`, `note`):

| Field | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `program` | Open vocabulary, for example `isdeaa-contract-interior`, `isdeaa-contract-indian-health-service`, `interior-self-governance-compact`, `interior-non-bia-funding-agreement`, `indian-health-service-self-governance-compact`, `tribally-controlled-school-grant`, `pl-102-477-plan`, `tribal-transportation-self-governance-compact`, `fdpir-self-determination-demonstration`, `tribal-forest-protection-act-agreement`, `indian-housing-block-grant`, `tribal-tanf`, `tribal-title-iv-e-plan` |
| `agency` | The federal agency or bureau, as the source names it (for a non-BIA agreement, the bureau and park or unit) |
| `since` | The year the nation entered, when the source gives it (both self-governance lists do) |
| `through` | The consortium, Tribal organization or housing entity that holds the agreement, when it is not the nation itself, written as the source writes it |
| `programs_included` | For a 477 plan, the federal programs merged into it, as the source names them |
| `amount` and `fiscal_year` | Optional: the year's allocation or obligation, from HUD or USAspending, with the recipient named in `through` when it is not the nation |

A nation credited only through a consortium's member list carries `verification: inferred` and says so in its note. A list that names the nation directly is `bulk-import`. As with other facts, absence from a list is never written as "does not take part" (data model, [Every fact is cited](../../docs/data-model.md#every-fact-is-cited)).

### 6.2 On the Page

The project proposes a new table, "Self-governance and federal programs," inside the existing "Treaties and federal ties" section, after Treaties and before the Gaming Commission enforcement actions (project analysis). It uses the same `DataTable` as the treaties table, with columns Program (linking to the program page), Agency, Since, Through and a citation mark, sorted by Since, with the usual .csv and .xlsx download. For the Cherokee Nation, for example, the table would read: Interior self-governance compact (1991, Office of Self-Governance list); Indian Health Service self-governance compact (1994); a 477 plan (Bureau of Indian Affairs data service); a transportation compact (2022); a round 2 FDPIR project; non-BIA funding agreements with the Bureau of Trust Fund Administration and the Appraisal and Valuation Services Office (fiscal year 2024); and its fiscal year 2026 housing block grant (sources in section 2). Amounts, where held, show in a column that is hidden by default.

Four smaller additions would make the data useful outside the page (project analysis):

1. **At a glance:** one label row, "Self-governance," naming the compacts (for example "Interior, Indian Health Service, Transportation"), shown only when there is at least one.
2. **Nations directory:** a "Self-governance" filter under "Add filter" and a column in the table builder data set, so a reader can list, say, every nation with both compacts.
3. **Program pages:** each program page lists its participants as a table, including consortia and their members, and links back to each nation.
4. **Map:** a layer on the shared map block coloring nations by the compacts they hold.

A nation with no facts would show "None Identified," as other empty sections do, with the search in the nation's search log under the topic `federal-programs`.

Per the standing rule, a mockup of the table and the At a glance row comes before any build, and the built page is compared with it side by side.

## 7. Limits and Open Questions

The lists are of different dates: Interior's self-governance list is from July 24, 2024, the Indian Health Service list from July 1, 2025 (with a banner that it may be out of date), HUD's from fiscal year 2026 and the 477 services current as of Nov. 26, 2026. Each fact keeps its own `as_of`.

No published source found in this pass says which nations hold Interior 638 contracts, which programs they cover, or which Bureau of Indian Education schools each nation runs. USAspending shows money by program, not whether a program runs under a contract or directly, except for the Indian Health Service listings 93.210 and 93.441 (project analysis).

Whether Trevor wants dollar amounts on nation pages is a choice for him; the amounts are public, but they invite comparisons between nations that the page cannot explain (project analysis).
