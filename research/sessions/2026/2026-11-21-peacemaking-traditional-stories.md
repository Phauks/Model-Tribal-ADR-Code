## What this session added

- Three story records, each quoted in the teller's own words. They are the Seminole Tribe of Florida's "Little Frog," told by Betty Mae Jumper, and the Oneida Indian Nation's "How the Clans were Chosen" and "My Home." "My Home" is quoted only from the Nation's description, because the story itself was not read.
- A second telling, from the Haudenosaunee Confederacy's own site, for the existing record on the founding of the Great Peace.
- Three source records for nations' statements that some stories are told only in winter (Citizen Potawatomi Nation, Salt River Pima-Maricopa Indian Community, Gila River Indian Community).
- Section 9 of [research/analyses/2026-11-21-peacemaking-stories.md](../../analyses/2026-11-21-peacemaking-stories.md).

## Excluded on purpose

- **Chickasaw Nation, "The Migration Story":** the Nation publishes it openly, and it turns on a disagreement that talk could not settle. The Nation also describes its oral histories as sacred stories, so no record was made until the Nation says how the story may be shared.
- **Creation stories** on the Seminole Tribe of Florida's and the Oneida Indian Nation's sites: creation accounts may be sacred and the pages do not say otherwise. The Seminole page also says outsiders' interpretations may confuse.
- **Ponca Tribe of Nebraska, "Voices of our Culture":** the page calls the collection sacred. Nothing about it is recorded beyond its existence.
- **Winter-only O'odham stories** (Salt River and Gila River newspapers): only the telling rule is recorded, never the stories.
- **Weak fits:** two stories turn on a monster or predator, not a dispute: the Tamástslikt Cultural Institute's Coyote Theater script and the Washoe Tribe's Ong legend. The Salish "Beaver Steals Fire" book and the Yakama and Tlingit story collections name no dispute story on the pages read. A Comanche Coyote story sits on a personal site. Two Oneida pages describe a story without telling it ("Raccoon & Crawfish," "How the Bear Lost His Tail").
