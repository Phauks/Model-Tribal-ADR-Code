# State statutes sweep, California, Nebraska, Utah, Maine and Florida: session notes

The method is the one in the first sweep's notes (research/sessions/2026/2026-11-27-state-statutes-sweep.md) and in `scripts/law/state_statutes/README.md`, with the readers' wrapper prompt in `research/state-statutes/reader-prompt.md` (which now has a note for each of these five states) and the spot check in `scripts/law/state_statutes/spot_check.py`. Each state's results are in `research/state-statutes/<state>/`. In every state the session read each strong hit a reader marked none and settled it by the rules the earlier batches used: a race or minority category with legal effect (a reporting, eligibility or diversity definition) is incidental; a role given to the Indian Health Service or the Bureau of Indian Affairs is incidental; a county boundary traced along a reservation line, findings with no rule, a federal cost circular's title, a civics topic and the federal Food Distribution Program on Indian Reservations named among eligibility programs are none.

The five states together list 2,236 Indian law sections (779 core, 1,457 incidental), of which three were already held by hand. Recall was under 1% in every state: of the nearly 3,000 sections the readers saw in the recall samples, three were Indian law, all in the near samples (two in Utah, one in Florida). A check after the merge found no unlisted section in force in any chapter whose name holds a nation's name, apart from the Coyote Valley Conservation Program in California's Public Resources Code (Cal. Pub. Res. Code §§ 35180 to 35186), which is named for a valley in Santa Clara County, not the Coyote Valley Band of Pomo Indians.

## California

The Legislative Counsel's site refuses scripts, so the adapter reads the bulk database it publishes for download: every version of every section, with the version in force chosen by its effective date (the latest on or before Nov. 27, 2026), and the table of contents for each section's place and its division, part, chapter and article names. The California codes have no official catchlines, so each record is titled by its code and number ("Welfare and Institutions Code section 224.1") and its chapter and article name; the constitution (CONS) is left out, as for the other states. A section's number here is the code's short name and its number ("WIC:224.1"), since each code numbers its own sections, and the citations use the Indigo Book's code names (Cal. Welf. & Inst. Code § 224.1). 161,508 sections in force in 29 codes; 990 strong and 1,317 weak-only hits; 1,270 Indian law sections (434 core, 836 incidental), the most of any state so far. The core sections include the California Indian Child Welfare Act provisions of the juvenile court law and the Family and Probate Codes (from Cal. Welf. & Inst. Code § 224 on), the California Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (Cal. Health & Safety Code §§ 8010 to 8030), the Native American Heritage Commission and the protection of Native American sacred sites (Cal. Pub. Res. Code § 5097.9 and following), the tribal-state gaming compacts ratified in the Government Code and the Tribal Government Gaming and Economic Self-Sufficiency Act (Cal. Gov't Code § 98000 and following), the Tribal Court Civil Money Judgment Act (Cal. Civ. Proc. Code §§ 1730 to 1741), the state-tribal agreements governing Indian fishing on the Klamath River (Cal. Fish & Game Code § 16510 and following) and solid waste facilities on Indian country (Cal. Pub. Res. Code § 44201 and following).

The readers marked 28 strong hits none, and the session read all 28 and put its verdicts in `settled.json`. It settled 15 as incidental: the Bureau of Indian Affairs dentists' exemption from licensing (Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 1626); the Native American categories in the board diversity laws (Cal. Corp. Code § 301.4 and Cal. Ins. Code § 927.3), in education equity, judicial and prison data (Cal. Educ. Code § 78220, Cal. Gov't Code § 12011.5, Cal. Penal Code § 2068) and in a funding formula (Cal. Health & Safety Code § 101320); Native American Day as a paid holiday for classified school and college employees (Cal. Educ. Code §§ 45203 and 88203); Native American lands as a criterion for historic parkways (Cal. Sts. & High. Code § 280); and the Native Hawaiian student achievement programs and data categories (Cal. Educ. Code §§ 79510, 79511, 89297 and 89297.1, Cal. Gov't Code § 8310.7). The Native Hawaiian sections are a judgment call: Native Hawaiians are not a tribe, but a program or category with legal effect for Native people is held incidental here as it is for American Indians and Alaska Natives, and Trevor's review can drop them. It agreed with 13 none verdicts, among them findings that cite Native statistics (Cal. Educ. Code §§ 300, 20051 and 42920, Cal. Ins. Code § 927), the federal reservation food program in two eligibility lists (Cal. Educ. Code §§ 8208 and 8213.5), a freeway's name (Cal. Sts. & High. Code § 510), the Indian wars in a veterans' exemption list (Cal. Rev. & Tax. Code § 205) and a federal cost circular's title (Cal. Welf. & Inst. Code § 14105.24). Neither recall sample found a miss, and the 40 verdicts read at random all agreed.

## Nebraska

The Legislature's chapter pages list every section with its catchline, repealed and transferred ones among them, so only the sections in force are downloaded: 49,908 listed, 32,372 in force. 246 strong and 208 weak-only hits; 283 Indian law sections (107 core, 176 incidental). The core sections include the Nebraska Indian Child Welfare Act (Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 43-1501 to 43-1524) and the adoption sections that apply it, the Commission on Indian Affairs (§ 81-2501 and following), the commitment of persons domiciled in Indian country and the health chapter's definitions of tribal courts (§§ 71-904.02, 71-914.01, 71-964 and 71-1226.01), the marriages of Indians by custom (§§ 42-402 to 42-407) and the American Indian Arts and Crafts Sales Act (§§ 69-1801 to 69-1808). The adapter takes the act that made a section from the head of its source note ("Laws 1985, LB 255", or "Laws 1925, c. 1" before the bill numbers); a few older notes name only later amendments and carry no year. The readers were told that Sioux, Dakota and Pawnee are also county and city names. They marked two strong hits none, and the session agreed with both (a county boundary along a former reservation line, § 22-171, and findings on sites significant to American Indians with no rule, § 70-1014.02); it also settled as none three county boundaries the readers had marked incidental (§§ 22-111, 22-163 and 22-187), as the earlier states held. Neither recall sample found a miss, and the 40 verdicts read at random all agreed.

## Utah

The Legislature publishes each title of the Utah Code as one XML file, in the version its page names; a section with a future version prints both, current first, and the adapter keeps the first (145 numbers). Each record links to the section's page on le.utah.gov. 27,753 sections in force; 214 strong and 219 weak-only hits; 270 Indian law sections (86 core, 184 incidental). The core sections include the Utah Division of Indian Affairs Act (Utah Code Ann. § 9-9-101 and following), the Navajo Trust Fund Act (§ 51-10-101 and following) and the Ute Indian Water Compact (§ 73-21-101 and following). The readers marked five strong hits none; the session settled one as incidental (county jails' reports of prisoners held for the Bureau of Indian Affairs, § 17-72-408) and agreed with four (a civics course covering tribal governments, § 53E-4-205.2; a heritage center's story, § 53H-4-305; the Indian Commerce Clause quoted, § 63C-4a-304; Native American rock art among the state symbols, § 63G-1-601). The near sample found two core sections with no Native term, the Navajo Trust Fund's board of trustees and trust administrator (§§ 51-10-202 and 51-10-203), settled core: 2 in 300. They show that the chapter-name test for pool E (`CHAPTER_LAW` in core.py) does not catch a chapter named only for a nation; the check above found no other such chapter with unlisted sections in these five states. The 40 verdicts read at random all agreed.

## Maine

The Revisor publishes one page per section, listed on each chapter's page, and keeps the pages of repealed and reallocated sections, which print only "(REPEALED)" or "(REALLOCATED ...)", under the same class as sections in force; the adapter takes their status from that text. A section with more than one version (text effective until or from a date, effective on a contingency, or two conflicting amendments) has one page per version, named with a suffix (title30sec7201-2.html); the heading prints the section's own number, and the adapter keeps the version in force on Nov. 27, 2026. This was found after the readers had read, so their answers were matched to the corrected numbers: four versions not in force were set aside, and eight sections not yet in force were dropped (seven of the new Interstate Compact on the Placement of Children, Me. Rev. Stat. tit. 22, §§ 4251 to 4268, effective on a contingency, and one section effective Jan. 1, 2027). The batch step's count of sections in force (25,506, in `pool-key.json`) is from before the fix. 46,093 pages, 25,327 sections in force; 242 strong and 301 weak-only hits; 317 Indian law sections (117 core, 200 incidental). The core sections are the Maine Implementing Act (Me. Rev. Stat. tit. 30, §§ 6201 to 6214), the Mi'kmaq Nation Restoration Act (tit. 30, §§ 7201 to 7210), the Maine Indian Child Welfare Act (tit. 22, §§ 3941 to 3955), the tribes' approval of legislation (tit. 3, §§ 601 to 603), the Tribal-State Collaboration Act (tit. 5, §§ 11051 to 11056), the Permanent Commission on the Status of Racial, Indigenous and Tribal Populations (tit. 5, § 25001 and following), Indian housing mortgage insurance (tit. 30-A, §§ 4932 to 4935) and the tribes' marine and elver fishing rights (tit. 12, §§ 6302-A and 6302-B). Two Implementing Act sections already held by hand (tit. 30, §§ 6204 and 7208) were left as they were. The citations use the Revisor's form, "Me. Rev. Stat. tit. 30, § 6212", since the text is the Revisor's and not the annotated edition; the two hand records use "Me. Rev. Stat. Ann.", which the review may want to match.

The readers were told that Penobscot is also a county, river and bay and Passamaquoddy a bay. They marked two strong hits none, and the session settled both as incidental: employees of the Maine Indian Tribal-State Commission in the state employees' health plan (tit. 5, § 285) and Indian Island, Penobscot Indian Reservation, as a member of a career and technical education region (tit. 20-A, § 8451). Neither recall sample found a miss, and the 40 verdicts read at random all agreed.

## Florida

The Legislature prints each chapter of the Florida Statutes whole on one page; the adapter splits it into sections and links each record to the section's own page. The history notes cite the session laws as "ch. 26888, 1951" before 1957 and "ch. 71-286" or "ch. 2004-2" after, and the adapter reads both. 24,993 sections in force; 77 strong and 403 weak-only hits (most of the weak hits are the Indian River and Seminole County place names); 96 Indian law sections (35 core, 61 incidental). The core sections are mostly chapter 285, Indian Reservations and Affairs (Fla. Stat. § 285.16 and following, including the Seminole Tribe gaming compact's authorization, § 285.710), whose § 285.16 was already held by hand and left as it was. The readers marked six strong hits none; the session agreed with two (a federal cost circular's title, § 350.81, and the federal reservation food program in an eligibility list, § 1002.395) and settled four as incidental (American Indian categories in voter registration and insurance licensing, §§ 97.052, 626.171 and 626.2415, and Indian Health Service prescribers' access to the prescription monitoring program, § 893.055). The near sample found one incidental section with no Native term, the gaming enforcement division that enforces the compact law (§ 16.711), settled incidental: 1 in 300. The 40 verdicts read at random all agreed.
