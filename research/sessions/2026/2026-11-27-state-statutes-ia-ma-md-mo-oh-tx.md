# State statutes sweep, Iowa, Massachusetts, Maryland, Missouri, Ohio and Texas: session notes

The method is the one in the first sweep's notes (research/sessions/2026/2026-11-27-state-statutes-sweep.md) and in `scripts/law/state_statutes/README.md`, with the readers' wrapper prompt in `research/state-statutes/reader-prompt.md` (this session's state notes are added there) and the spot check in `scripts/law/state_statutes/spot_check.py`. Each state's results are in `research/state-statutes/<state>/`. None of the six states has many Native nations within it (Iowa has one, Massachusetts two, Texas three, and Maryland, Missouri and Ohio none that are federally recognized), and every state came in under the pilot's estimate except Texas, whose 30 codes are the largest code read so far.

As for Minnesota, a section's enactment year is taken only from a session law at the head of its history line: Iowa's lines that open with a bracketed earlier code ("[C51, §...; R60, §...]") and Missouri's that open with "RSMo 1939" name only later amendments, so those sections carry no year. The Massachusetts General Laws, the Maryland code and the Ohio Revised Code print no history lines, so their records carry no year at all.

## Iowa

The Legislative Services Agency publishes each chapter of the Iowa Code as an XML file with the sections, their catchlines and their history lines marked; each record links to the section's own PDF (legis.iowa.gov/docs/code/<section>.pdf), as the two records already held do. 47,239 numbers, 25,438 sections in force (the rest are reserved numbers and repeals, which the Code keeps in place); 92 strong and 194 weak-only hits; 109 Indian law sections (37 core, 72 incidental). The core sections are the state's jurisdiction over the Meskwaki Settlement in Tama County (Iowa Code §§ 1.12 to 1.15A, of which § 1.12 was already held by hand and was left as it was), the Iowa Indian Child Welfare Act (ch. 232B), the recognition of tribal court civil judgments (ch. 626D, whose § 626D.5 was already held), tax agreements with tribes (§ 421.47), the homestead credit on Meskwaki trust land (§ 425.12) and the appropriation to the Settlement's tribal council for schooling (§ 256.30). The readers were told that many county and city names in Iowa are nations' names. They marked one strong hit none, and the session agreed (the registration exemption for native American styled canoes, § 462A.6A). Neither recall sample found a miss, and the 40 verdicts read at random all agreed.

## Massachusetts

The Legislature's open data service answers with each chapter's list of sections and each section's text; each record links to the section's page on malegislature.gov. Sections numbered with a fraction ("§ 4 1/2") are asked for with a tilde in place of the slash, as the Legislature's own pages do. The General Laws print no history lines, so the records carry no year. 26,779 sections, 24,566 in force; 38 strong and 282 weak-only hits; 51 Indian law sections (8 core, 43 incidental). The core sections are the Commission on Indian Affairs (Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 6A, § 8A), American Indian Heritage Week and Native American Day (ch. 6, §§ 12I and 12VV), the protection of American Indian skeletal remains and burial sites (ch. 7, § 38A; ch. 7C, § 43; ch. 9, § 26A; ch. 38, § 6) and the gaming commission's evaluation of whether to negotiate Class III gaming with the commonwealth's tribes (ch. 23K, § 67); most incidental sections count tribal court convictions and protection orders, or name tribes as states, employers or grantees. The readers were told that "Mashpee", "Aquinnah", "Natick" and "Nipmuc" are also names of towns and places. They marked no strong hit none. The session settled one core verdict incidental: the gaming commission's list of general powers, one of which is to help the governor negotiate a tribal compact (ch. 23K, § 4). Most weak-only hits were state park "reservations" and "allotments" of money. The near sample held only 121 sections, since few chapters had a strong hit; neither recall sample found a miss. Of the 40 verdicts read at random, 39 agreed and the other was ch. 23K, § 4, settled as above.

## Maryland

The General Assembly publishes each article of the Annotated Code as one HTML file. The official text prints no catchlines and no history lines (the publishers add those), so each record is named by its citation (Md. Code Ann., State Gov't § 9.5-302) and carries no year, and the readers saw no subtitle names. 39,025 sections, 39,011 in force; 65 strong and 218 weak-only hits; 81 Indian law sections (22 core, 59 incidental). The core sections are the Commission on Indian Affairs and its process for recognizing Maryland Indian status (State Gov't §§ 9.5-301 to 9.5-312), Indian child custody (Fam. Law § 9.5-103), tribal protective orders (Fam. Law § 4-508.1), Indian housing authorities (Hous. & Cmty. Dev. §§ 22-101 and 22-103), Native American remains found in state archaeology (State Fin. & Proc. §§ 5A-333 and 5A-339), burial sites (Real Prop. § 14-121), notarial acts under tribal authority (State Gov't § 18-211) and American Indian Heritage Month (Gen. Provis. § 7-507). The readers marked five strong hits none, all lists of race categories that a report, a form or a definition must use; the session settled them incidental, as the earlier states held for race categories with legal effect. Because the code prints no subtitle names, pool E could not find the Commission's sections that name no Native term, and the near sample did: five of its 199 sections were the Commission's (§§ 9.5-304, 9.5-305, 9.5-306, 9.5-308 and 9.5-312, its chair, meetings, staff, money and a ban on false statements), which the session settled core with the rest of the subtitle after reading it whole. No other recall sample found a miss, and the 40 verdicts read at random all agreed.

## Missouri

The Revisor of Statutes prints each section on its own page, with its catchline and history line; each record links to the section's page (revisor.mo.gov/main/OneSection.aspx). The chapter pages list only sections in force, and list a section once for each version in force (30,434 versions of 30,346 sections); the first listed is read. After about 850 pages asked six at a time, the site answered every request with a page that says the machine's address is "Blocked". The block lifted some hours later, and the adapter was slowed to one page every 1.5 seconds on two workers, which the site allowed: 8,794 pages were read from the cloud session, and, since long runs in this project go to GitHub (Trevor, Nov. 28, 2026), a temporary workflow on the branch read the rest there, starting from the session's download. GitHub's first job stopped after 25 failures in a row with 6,290 pages still to read; the second job, on another machine, read them all. The adapter now waits after a failed request too and stops after 25 failures in a row, so a block page is not asked again thousands of times. A Missouri catchline may hold dashes of its own ("... by director, when — purposes of corporation. — 1. The director ...") and the chapter pages cut it at the first, so the catchline is taken from the section's own page, up to the first dash the body goes on from with a capital, a number or a quotation mark; one section of 30,346 (§ 334.230, whose text opens with a misprinted "l.") still begins in lower case.

30,343 sections in force; 38 strong and 189 weak-only hits; 47 Indian law sections (6 core, 41 incidental). The core sections are Native American Heritage Month (Mo. Rev. Stat. § 9.356), the reinterment of unmarked human remains in conformity with the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (§ 194.408), Indian cemeteries (§ 214.450), Indian tribes as employers under the unemployment law (§ 288.037), the sale of American Indian art and crafts (§ 407.315) and the custody act's application to tribes (§ 452.715); most incidental sections count tribal court judgments and protection orders, name tribes as states in the uniform acts, accept a tribal document as identification for voting, or exempt tribes from the fuel tax. The readers were told that "Osage", "Neosho", "Seneca" and other Missouri place names are nations' names. They marked no strong hit none. Neither recall sample found a miss, and the 40 verdicts read at random all agreed.

## Ohio

The Legislative Service Commission prints every section of a chapter on one page, with its effective date and latest bill but no history line; each record links to the section's own page. 33,791 sections in force; 44 strong and 230 weak-only hits; 45 Indian law sections (4 core, 41 incidental). The core sections are Native American Indian Day (Ohio Rev. Code Ann. § 5.223), tribal-state gaming compacts (§ 107.25), American Indian burial sites (§ 149.3010) and custody proceedings for Indian children (§ 3127.03); most incidental sections count tribal court convictions in the sex offender and violent offender laws or name tribes as employers in the unemployment law. The readers were told that many Ohio county, city and river names are nations' names. They marked two strong hits none, and the session agreed (two county water and sewer sections that cite a federal cost circular for "State, Local, and Indian Tribal Governments", §§ 6103.02 and 6117.02, as Minnesota's session held). One reader answered one line under the section number instead of its id, and the session fixed the id. Neither recall sample found a miss, and the 40 verdicts read at random all agreed.

## Texas

The Texas Legislative Council publishes each code as a zip of HTML files, one per chapter; the adapter reads all 30 (the 28 codes, Vernon's Civil Statutes with its auxiliary water laws, and the Insurance Code articles not yet codified), numbers each section `<code>:<section>` because each code numbers its own, cites the codes and the articles each in its own Indigo Book form (Tex. Tax Code Ann. § 151.337; Tex. Code Crim. Proc. Ann. art. 2A.003) and gives the catchlines, printed in capitals, title case. Where a section is printed twice (two bills each added or changed it), the versions are read together as one section. 122,609 sections, 122,546 in force (the Special District Local Laws Code alone holds 31,717); 83 strong and 503 weak-only hits; 88 Indian law sections (16 core, 72 incidental). The core sections are the sales tax exemptions for the Alabama-Coushatta Tribe, the Ysleta del Sur Pueblo and the Kickapoo Traditional Tribe (Tex. Tax Code Ann. § 151.337), the Kickapoo Traditional Tribe's religious taking of deer (Tex. Parks & Wild. Code Ann. § 61.021), peace officers commissioned by a tribal council (Tex. Code Crim. Proc. Ann. art. 2A.003), the sale of Indian arts and crafts (Tex. Bus. & Com. Code Ann. §§ 17.851 to 17.854), the custody act's application to tribes (Tex. Fam. Code Ann. § 152.104), Indian tribes as employers under the unemployment law, American Indian Heritage Day, the protection of American Indian rock art (Tex. Nat. Res. Code Ann. § 191.132) and wagering on races on Indian lands (Tex. Occ. Code Ann. § 2033.008). The readers were told that many Texas county, city and lake names are nations' names. They marked three strong hits none; the session agreed with two (the "Native American bison meat" label, Tex. Agric. Code Ann. § 151.002, and the official state play presented at the Alabama-Coushatta Indian Reservation, Tex. Gov't Code Ann. § 3101.011) and settled one incidental (Native American descent among the race categories of the protective order registry, Tex. Gov't Code Ann. § 72.151). Neither recall sample found a miss, and the 40 verdicts read at random all agreed.
