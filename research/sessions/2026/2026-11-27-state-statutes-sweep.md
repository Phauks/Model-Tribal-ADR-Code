# State statutes sweep: session notes

The pipeline and how to add a state are described in `scripts/law/state_statutes/README.md`. Each state's results are in `research/state-statutes/<state>/`: `sweep.json` (the keyword pass and reading counts), `pool-key.json` and `readings.jsonl` (every verdict, so the recall figures can be checked), `indian-law-sections.csv` (the list the records are written from) and `settled.json` (hand fixes). The reading instructions are `research/state-statutes/reading-instructions.md`, the pilot's with the state left open and one rule added: a section of a chapter whose own name is Indian law (such as a definition in a state Indian child custody chapter) is part of that law even when its words name no Native term. Nevada's recall sample found two such sections, so every state's batches now include a fifth pool, E, of every non-hit in a chapter whose name has a strong term.

Oklahoma was moved onto the pipeline: `run.py seed OK` turns the pilot's list into the pipeline's list, and `run.py write OK` writes the same 380 records the pilot's writer did. The only changes are the writer named in each record, a full history line where the pilot cut it at 600 characters, and history lines no longer taking body text that wraps at "from".

Montana's code site prints one page per section, including numbers held open ("through 1-1-213 reserved") and pages that say a number has no text; those 2,969 are left out with the repealed ones, which leaves 30,077 sections in force. It also prints the court rules (civil and appellate procedure, evidence) in title 25 and 26; the one rule found, Mont. R. App. P. 15 (certified questions between the Montana Supreme Court and a tribe's highest court), is cited as a rule. The readers marked six strong hits none, and the session settled all six as incidental. Three of the 300 sections in the near sample were core misses, all in the Montana Repatriation Act (Mont. Code Ann. title 22, ch. 3, pt. 9), whose words speak of claimants and cultural affiliation rather than tribes; the session read the act's other six unflagged sections and settled them as core with it. The 40 verdicts the session read at random all agreed.

## Delaware

The Delaware Code is cited by title (Del. Code Ann. tit. 29, § 107), so the adapter numbers each section `<title>:<section>` and the records carry the title as the citation's volume. The code's pages give 23,244 sections, 22,304 in force. The keyword pass found 38 strong and 168 weak-only hits; the readers found 59 Indian law sections (29 core, 30 incidental). They marked one strong hit none, the new Interstate Compact for the Placement of Children (tit. 31, § 381), which the session settled as incidental (it has guidelines made with tribes for cases of Indian children). The near sample held five sections of two chapters whose own names are Indian law but have no strong term: chapter 54 of title 7 (Unmarked Human Burials and Human Skeletal Remains, whose purpose and definitions make it the state's Native graves law, with the Nanticoke and Lenape chiefs on its committee) and chapter 37 of title 29 (Delaware Commission on Indian Heritage and Culture). The session read both chapters whole and settled their unflagged sections as core, and widened pool E's chapter test (`CHAPTER_LAW` in core.py: "Indians", "Indigenous", "Tribes" and the like) so later states read such chapters in full. The 40 verdicts the session read at random all agreed.

## North Carolina

The General Statutes are printed one page per chapter; each record links to the General Assembly's page for its one section. 36,503 sections, 23,703 in force; 127 strong and 310 weak-only hits; 160 Indian law sections (75 core, 85 incidental). The readers marked five strong hits none; the session settled three as incidental (the Class III gaming exemption in § 14-306.3, capital needs reports naming the Historically American Indian University in § 116-13.1, and Bureau of Indian Affairs special officers holding state office in § 128-1.1) and agreed with two (a race category on the marriage license form, § 51-16, and a list of boards that names the state Commission of Indian Affairs, § 120-123). The near sample's 15 core verdicts were nearly all in chapter 70, "Indian Antiquities, Archaeological Resources and Unmarked Human Skeletal Remains Protection", where the readers had applied the chapter-name rule to every article. The session read chapter 70 by article: article 1 (Indian Antiquities) and article 3 (the Unmarked Human Burial and Human Skeletal Remains Protection Act, the state's Native graves law, with the Commission of Indian Affairs consulted) are core as a whole; articles 2 (the Archaeological Resources Protection Act) and 4 (the Archaeological Record Program) are general archaeology laws, so only their sections that give tribes or the Commission of Indian Affairs a role are kept, as incidental. After that the near sample's misses are five of 300 (four in article 3 and one in the Indian Housing Authority article, chapter 157), under 2%. Of the 40 verdicts read at random the session changed one: the special plate fee table (§ 20-79.7) is incidental, like the plate list it goes with (§ 20-79.4).

## South Carolina

The Code of Laws pages are one per chapter and have no anchor for a section, so each record links to its chapter's page. The unannotated code online leaves out most repealed sections: 31,090 sections, 30,629 in force. 46 strong and 257 weak-only hits; 66 records (31 core, 35 incidental), and a 67th section already held by hand (the Catawba Indian Claims Settlement Act's § 27-16-40) was left as it was. The readers marked two strong hits none, both "State" or "employer" definitions that name tribes (§§ 1-13-30 and 15-47-110), which the session settled as incidental. Neither recall sample found a miss. The 40 verdicts read at random all agreed.

## Alaska

The Legislature's print view of each title has no history lines, so the Alaska records carry no enactment year and the near sample is drawn only from sections printed next to a strong hit. 20,235 sections, 18,025 in force; 164 strong and 211 weak-only hits; 246 Indian law sections (74 core, 172 incidental). The readers were told one Alaska rule beyond the shared instructions: a Native regional or village corporation under the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act, an Alaska Native village or tribe and a Native allotment count as Native. They marked 14 strong hits none, most because the Bureau of Indian Affairs or the Indian Health Service is a federal agency; the session settled 11 as incidental (a role for those agencies in Alaska's schools, retirement credit and health payments is a rule about Native people, as in the other states), Elizabeth Peratrovich Day as core (like the readers' Katie John Day) and agreed that two bridge names are none. The near sample's seven misses were in articles whose names say Native (Regional Native Housing Authorities, Native Art Competitions, University of Alaska Scholarships for Natives, the Alaska Native art seal); the session read those articles whole and settled their four other unflagged sections as core. Of the 40 verdicts read at random the session changed one (§ 47.18.010, where Native organizations are among those the statewide plan must consult, is incidental).

## Virginia

33,354 sections, 31,080 in force; 63 strong and 228 weak-only hits; 69 Indian law sections (18 core, 51 incidental). Virginia's recognitions of its tribes are uncodified acts, so they are not in the code; the code's Indian law is mostly the Commission on Federally Recognized Tribes (Va. Code Ann. §§ 30-414 to 30-420, three of whose sections pool E found), tribal consultation duties of state agencies, hunting, fishing and tax exemptions for tribal members and the "State" definitions of uniform acts. The readers marked four strong hits none; the session settled three as incidental (Native Americans in the minority business definitions, § 2.2-1604; grants to tribes from the Black, Indigenous, and People of Color Historic Preservation Fund, § 10.1-2202.5; Indian Health Service training for dental assistants, § 54.1-2729.01) and agreed with one (a museum's duty to interpret American Indian history, § 23.1-3207). Neither recall sample found a miss, and the 40 verdicts read at random all agreed.

## Arizona

The Legislature's section pages have no history lines, so the Arizona records carry no enactment year, and the near sample is drawn only from sections printed next to a strong hit. 25,104 sections, 24,880 in force; 420 strong and 264 weak-only hits; 499 Indian law sections (95 core, 404 incidental), the most of any state so far, because Arizona's code names tribes throughout (tribal-state gaming compacts, tribal courts' orders, tribal governments on state boards and in planning duties). The readers marked 18 strong hits none. The session settled eight as incidental, each giving a tribe or tribal people a role in a general law (a five-mile buffer around tribal gaming facilities for event wagering, § 5-1307; a tribe as a public agency that may recover emergency response costs, § 13-1709; the guardianship act's "State" definition, § 14-12102; the code talker plate seal, § 28-2447, like the readers' § 28-2414), and agreed that ten are none (county boundaries, highway routes and survey monuments traced along reservation lines, and the historical society's duty to collect data on tribes, § 41-823). Pool E, which reads every section of a chapter whose name is Indian law, found 21 core sections with no strong term: the Indian reservation gaming chapter (§§ 5-603, 5-604, 5-606), the Indian reservation tobacco tax article (§§ 42-3303, 42-3305, 42-3306), the Indian arts and crafts section (§ 44-1231.05) and the Gila River Indian Community water rights settlement chapter (§§ 45-2602 to 45-2653). Neither recall sample found a miss, and the 40 verdicts read at random all agreed.
