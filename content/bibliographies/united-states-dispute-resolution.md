# Dispute resolution in the United States: an annotated bibliography

This is a working reading list on dispute resolution outside Native nations' own systems: federal law and programs, state law (with Nevada as a worked example), the five inhabited territories, the history of the field, its main treatises and journals, and the places where Tribal systems and others meet. The book needs this context because a nation's code operates alongside these systems, not in isolation (project analysis).

Every entry is a source record in `content/sources/`, cited by its key. Each record holds the full citation, an archived copy where one exists, and notes on how it was verified. How the list was built, and its limits, are in the research log for 2026-11-21 (`research/sessions/2026/2026-11-21-united-states-dispute-resolution.md`).

How to read an entry:

- The annotation says what the source is and why it matters here. Quotations carry pinpoints; judgments that are the project's own are marked "(project analysis)".
- The line in italics says whether the full text is free online or must be pulled from Westlaw or elsewhere, and which dispute resolution methods the source concerns. Each method name is a record in `content/methods/`, an open list that grows when a source describes a process not yet recorded (decision record 0012).
- [[peacemaking|Peacemaking]] and customary processes are listed for context and are not treated as forms of [[adr]]. The Native American Rights Fund's Indigenous Peacemaking Initiative states that "peacemaking is not alternative dispute resolution" [@narf-ipi-home].
- Nothing here has been checked in a citator yet, and no annotation is legal advice.

## History of dispute resolution in the United States

- [@sander-1976-varieties-of-dispute-processing]. Frank Sander's address at the April 7-9, 1976 'Pound Conference' (National Conference on the Causes of Popular Dissatisfaction with the Administration of Justice), printed in the conference proceedings at 70 F.R.D. 79, with Sander's paper at 111-34. Sander proposes "to explore alternative ways of resolving disputes outside the courts" (at 112). He asks what the "significant characteristics" of processes such as adjudication, arbitration, mediation and negotiation are, and how they can yield "rational criteria for allocating various types of disputes to different dispute resolution processes" (at 113). He then imagines that "by the year 2000 not simply a court house but a Dispute Resolution Center, where the grievant would first be channelled through a screening clerk" to "the process (or sequence of processes) most appropriate to his type of case," with rooms for mediation, arbitration, fact finding, a malpractice screening panel, the superior court and an ombudsman (at 130-31). He also describes a "screening-adjudication model," such as Massachusetts's malpractice board (at 128-29), and the "Michigan Mediation System" (at 130). Brazil (2002) calls Sander 'the spiritual father of court ADR' and cites this paper at 130-31 for the multi-door vision, and Menkel-Meadow (2000) identifies it as a common starting date for the ADR movement. (project analysis) The paper itself does not use the phrase "multi-door courthouse"; it says "Dispute Resolution Center." When Sander says institutions "such as prisons, schools, or mental hospitals" could establish "indigenous dispute resolution processes" (at 131), "indigenous" means internal to the institution, not Indigenous peoples; quote it with that caution. The multi-door design is an intake-and-referral structure, not a dispute resolution method. A model Tribal code can adopt it to route matters to court, to ADR, or (by the parties' choice) to a separate peacemaking track. *Not free online; pull from Westlaw, HeinOnline, a library. Methods: mediation, arbitration, negotiation, fact-finding, ombuds. Details need checking.*

- [@friedman-2007-popular-dissatisfaction-retrospective]. A centennial retrospective on Roscoe Pound's 1906 address to the American Bar Association, 'The Causes of Popular Dissatisfaction with the Administration of Justice'. The 1976 Pound Conference took its name and theme from that address. Friedman's footnote 1 dates the address to the ABA Annual Convention of Aug. 29, 1906, and his text places the gathering in the Capitol building in St. Paul, Minnesota. Friedman's note 1 cites a reprint 'in 14 AM. LAW. 445'; the year printed there appeared as 1996 in our reading tool and is probably a misprint for 1906. (project analysis) This is a citable secondary source for the history paragraph of the commentary. Trevor should cite Pound's address from its original printing, conventionally 29 A.B.A. Rep. 395 (1906), which was NOT verified here. *Free online; pull from HeinOnline.*

- [@menkel-meadow-2000-mothers-and-fathers-of-invention]. Menkel-Meadow's essay on the intellectual founders of ADR. The abstract names the usual starting points (Sander's multi-door courthouse paper at the 1976 Pound Conference, Fisher and Ury's Getting to Yes, and 1960s-70s community efforts) and argues that dating the movement to the 1970s-80s overlooks 'earlier "intellectual" founders of ADR'. (project analysis) The commentary can use it to place modern U.S. ADR in a longer history, and to make the contrast with Indigenous peacemaking, which has its own, much older lineage. *Free online; pull from HeinOnline. Methods: negotiation, mediation, arbitration.*

- [@brazil-2002-court-adr-25-years-after-pound]. Magistrate Judge Wayne Brazil (N.D. Cal.) looks back at court-connected ADR 25 years after the Pound Conference. He calls Sander 'the spiritual father of court ADR' and argues that ADR is 'about being in addition to', not 'better than', litigation. He reports that courts 'have also fallen far short of making real Professor Sander's vision of the multi-door courthouse', because many programs 'offer essentially only one process-a malleable, hybrid form of mediation', while nonbinding court-connected arbitration is 'threatened with extinction'. (project analysis) This is a useful caution for a Tribal code: a menu of processes on paper tends to shrink to a single default unless intake, funding, and neutral rosters support each option. *Free online; pull from HeinOnline. Methods: mediation, nonbinding (court-annexed) arbitration, early neutral evaluation, settlement conference.*

- [@auerbach-1983-justice-without-law]. A historical study of American dispute settlement outside the courts. The NCJRS abstract describes the book as tracing non-litigation methods in religious, utopian, ethnic, and business communities, starting with colonial settlement 'based in religious community', and arguing that modern calls for ADR reflect an overburdened legal system. Publication by Oxford University Press (New York, 1983) is confirmed by Eric H. Steele's review essay, 'Morality, Legality, and Dispute Processing: Auerbach's Justice Without Law?', 9 Am. B. Found. Res. J. 188-205 (1984). (project analysis) Auerbach's central point, that informal justice works best inside cohesive communities with shared values, bears directly on Tribal ADR and on why peacemaking that is rooted in community and kinship differs from imported court-annexed mediation. *Not free online; pull from a library. Methods: mediation, arbitration, conciliation. Confirmed in a catalog; full text not read.*

- [@fiss-1984-against-settlement]. The canonical critique of the ADR movement. Fiss notes that the movement 'has even received its own acronym-ADR (Alternative Dispute Resolution)' and argues that settlement 'is a truce more than a true reconciliation'. In his view it is 'the civil analogue of plea bargaining' and 'should be neither encouraged nor praised'. His concerns are coerced consent, imbalances of power, lack of authority to bind, and the loss of public adjudication. In a 1985 reply ('Out of Eden', 94 Yale L.J. 1669, also opened), Fiss answered McThenia & Shaffer's 'For Reconciliation', 94 Yale L.J. 1660, which offered a religious-community model of reconciliation. (project analysis) A model Tribal code should answer Fiss directly: protect against coercion and power imbalance, keep certain matters in court, and explain why peacemaking, which seeks reconciliation rather than a 'truce', is a separate track and not 'settlement'. *Free online; pull from HeinOnline, Westlaw. Methods: negotiation, settlement conference.*

- [@dispute-resolution-act-1980]. The LII codification note for the Title 28 Appendix summarizes Pub. L. 96-190 (Feb. 12, 1980), 94 Stat. 17, 'known as the Dispute Resolution Act'. The Act provided for mechanisms for resolving minor disputes. It created a Dispute Resolution Resource Center and a Dispute Resolution Advisory Board, authorized $1,000,000 a year (FY 1980-1984) for the Center and Board and $10,000,000 a year (FY 1981-1984) for grants, and required an annual report by the Attorney General. (project analysis) This was Congress's attempt to scale up the Neighborhood Justice Center experiment nationally. A model Tribal code drafter can use it as a precedent for a funding-and-resource-center structure. Whether the grant money was ever appropriated was not verified here; check the Attorney General reports or later scholarship. *Free online; pull from HeinOnline. Methods: mediation, arbitration, conciliation.*

- [@sheppard-roell-cook-1978-njc-field-test-implementation]. A 309-page LEAA-funded evaluation (grant 78NI-AX-0012) of the three federal Neighborhood Justice Centers in Atlanta, Kansas City, and Los Angeles. The NCJRS abstract says the centers opened 'during March, 1978' after staff hiring, a training workshop in Reno, referral-source development, and recruiting and training volunteer mediators. The three centers had different sponsors: in Atlanta, a private nonprofit citizen organization; in Kansas City, the city's Community Services Department; in Los Angeles, the Los Angeles County Bar Association. Referrals came from courts, police, and prosecutors. (project analysis) A 'neighborhood justice center' is best classed as a program or setting that delivers mediation (and sometimes conciliation or arbitration), not as a separate method. The three sponsorship models (citizen nonprofit, city agency, bar association) are direct analogues to options a Tribe has for housing an ADR office. *Free online. Methods: mediation.*

- [@macneil-1992-american-arbitration-law]. Macneil's history of American arbitration law (Oxford Univ. Press 1992, xvi + 266 pp. per catalog). It covers the 1920s reform movement that produced the 1925 FAA and the Court's later 'nationalization' of the Act. The catalog summary says arbitration can 'frequently be manipulated to maintain inequitable relationships'. (project analysis) This is the key secondary source for the 1925 legislative history. It also supports the commentary point that the FAA was designed for commercial parties of roughly equal bargaining power, which matters when a Tribal code decides how to treat consumer and employment arbitration clauses. *Not free online; pull from a library. Methods: arbitration. Confirmed in a catalog; full text not read.*

## Treatises, casebooks and practice books

- [@cole-et-al-mediation-law-policy-practice-2025]. The leading U.S. mediation-law treatise, updated annually. The Thomson Reuters store lists the 2025-2026 edition, by Craig A. McEwen, James R. Coben, Nadja M. Alexander, Nancy Hardin Rogers, Peter N. Thompson, and Sarah Rudolph Cole. It covers 'mediation law developments, cases, statutes, and the mediation process itself', identifies relevant cases and statutes in each state, and discusses confidentiality, conflicts of interest, and advertising. It is on Westlaw: Georgetown Law Library's ADR treatise guide links it to the Westlaw database identifier 'MEDIATION'. (project analysis) This should be the first stop for state-by-state comparisons when drafting Tribal confidentiality and privilege provisions. *Not free online; pull from Westlaw, the publisher. Methods: mediation. Confirmed in a catalog; full text not read.*

- [@domke-on-commercial-arbitration]. The standard U.S. commercial arbitration treatise (3d ed., currently the 2026-2027 annual edition per the Thomson Reuters store; authors listed as Gabriel M. Wilner, Larry Edmonson, and Martin Domke). Its coverage includes arbitration agreements, arbitrability, arbitrators, hearings, awards, and confirming and enforcing awards, including 'intrastate enforcement of award' and international enforcement. Georgetown's ADR treatise guide links it to Westlaw database 'DCMLARB' and also to Bloomberg Law. (project analysis) Use it for the award-confirmation, vacatur, and enforcement mechanics a Tribal arbitration chapter must address. *Not free online; pull from Westlaw, the publisher. Methods: arbitration. Confirmed in a catalog; full text not read.*

- [@macneil-speidel-stipanowich-1994-federal-arbitration-law]. A multi-volume loose-leaf FAA treatise (Michigan State University catalog: 5 volumes, Boston: Little, Brown, ©1994-, with contributions by G. Richard Shell). (project analysis) The most detailed treatment of FAA text and history before the modern mandatory-arbitration cases. It is useful when a Tribal code must decide whether to mirror FAA §§ 2-4 and 9-11 or depart from them. *Not free online; pull from a library. Methods: arbitration. Confirmed in a catalog; full text not read.*

- [@goldberg-sander-rogers-cole-2020-dispute-resolution]. The long-running ADR casebook co-authored by Frank Sander, the author of 'Varieties of Dispute Processing'. The Aspen page lists the Seventh Edition (publication date 2020-02-02, 704 pages, Aspen Casebook Series; ISBN 9781543801088, eBook 9781543849431) by Stephen B. Goldberg, Frank E.A. Sander, Nancy H. Rogers, and Sarah Rudolph Cole. (project analysis) The standard teaching map of U.S. processes, including hybrid processes and the design of dispute systems. 'Dispute systems design' is best treated in the project as a design discipline (like the multi-door courthouse), not as a method. *Not free online; pull from the publisher, a library. Methods: negotiation, mediation, arbitration, early neutral evaluation, minitrial, summary jury trial, ombuds. Confirmed in a catalog; full text not read.*

- [@ali-2023-restatement-us-law-international-commercial-investor-state-arbitration]. The ALI's first Restatement on international arbitration. The ALI Adviser project page lists the reporters as George A. Bermann, Jack J. Coe, Jr., Christopher Drahozal, and Catherine A. Rogers. A&O Shearman's blog post of July 24, 2023 reports that the ALI 'recently published the first ever Restatement of the U.S. Law of International Commercial and Investor-State Arbitration.' (project analysis) It is relevant only at the margins: if a Tribe or Tribal enterprise arbitrates with a foreign counterparty, U.S. courts will apply the New York Convention framework described here. Its Restatement format (black letter, comment, reporters' notes) is also a model for the project's own commentary. *Not free online; pull from Westlaw, the publisher. Methods: arbitration. Confirmed in a catalog; full text not read.*

- [@bush-folger-2004-promise-of-mediation]. The revised edition of the book that introduced 'transformative' mediation. The Wiley/Jossey-Bass page lists the Revised Edition, October 2004, ISBN 978-0-7879-7483-1 (books often cite it as 2005). (project analysis) Transformative mediation aims at party empowerment and mutual recognition rather than settlement. The project can record it as a style of mediation (an attribute) rather than a separate method. Its relational goals are sometimes compared with peacemaking, but the project keeps them distinct. *Not free online; pull from a library, the publisher. Methods: mediation. Confirmed in a catalog; full text not read.*

- [@moore-2014-mediation-process]. A standard practitioner text on the stages and strategies of mediation (4th ed., Jossey-Bass, April 2014, per the O'Reilly listing). (project analysis) Useful for the 'how' of mediation, such as stages, caucus, and impasse strategies, when writing commentary on a mediator's duties and the structure of sessions. It is not a source of law. *Not free online; pull from a library, the publisher. Methods: mediation. Confirmed in a catalog; full text not read.*

- [@fisher-ury-patton-2011-getting-to-yes]. The Harvard Negotiation Project's book on principled, interest-based negotiation. Penguin Random House lists the Penguin Books paperback (ISBN 9780143118756) and e-book (9781101539545) as published May 3, 2011, 240 pages, by Roger Fisher, William L. Ury, and Bruce Patton. Menkel-Meadow (2000) names its publication as one of the markers people use to date the ADR movement. (project analysis) Its interest-based framework underlies most U.S. mediation training, and that is worth flagging when Tribal communities adapt imported training. *Not free online; pull from a library, the publisher. Methods: negotiation. Confirmed in a catalog; full text not read.*

## Journals

- [@journal-ohio-state-journal-on-dispute-resolution]. Student-edited journal of the Moritz College of Law, established in 1985. Moritz describes it as 'the official law journal of the American Bar Association Section of Dispute Resolution.' It publishes article issues, a symposium issue, and an annual annotated bibliography issue that abstracts 'over 500 articles, books, and other resources on ADR'. Back issues are open on the OSU Knowledge Bank (kb.osu.edu). (project analysis) The annual bibliography is an efficient way to sweep ADR literature year by year for Tribal and Indigenous topics. *Free online; pull from HeinOnline, Westlaw.*

- [@journal-harvard-negotiation-law-review]. Harvard Law School journal. The Program on Negotiation's publications page says it provides 'a forum in which scholars from many disciplines can discuss negotiation as it relates to law and legal institutions'. Unlike Negotiation Journal, it is 'aimed specifically at lawyers and legal scholars', and its premier issue appeared in spring 1996. (project analysis) Useful for law-focused negotiation and ADR-design scholarship. *Free online; pull from HeinOnline, Westlaw.*

- [@journal-cardozo-journal-of-conflict-resolution]. Student-edited journal affiliated with Cardozo Law School's Kukin Program for Conflict Resolution. The journal describes itself as covering 'arbitration, negotiation, mediation, settlement, and restorative justice'. (project analysis) Its restorative-justice coverage makes it a likely place for scholarship near Indigenous justice, although the project treats peacemaking as its own track. *Free online; pull from HeinOnline, Westlaw.*

- [@journal-pepperdine-dispute-resolution-law-journal]. Journal of Pepperdine University's Caruso School of Law. It is open access on Digital Commons, ISSN 1536-3090, and its current issue is Vol. 26, Iss. 2 (2026). The journal states its aim is to 'facilitate an intellectual discourse on the rapidly evolving field of alternative dispute resolution.' (project analysis) The current issue includes an article on court-connected compulsory arbitration programs, which is relevant to court-annexed design choices. *Free online; pull from HeinOnline, Westlaw.*

- [@journal-of-dispute-resolution-missouri]. Student-edited journal of the University of Missouri School of Law, published 'in conjunction with the Center for the Study of Dispute Resolution', and 'established in 1984'. It is open access on the Missouri law scholarship repository. (project analysis) Among the earliest U.S. law journals devoted to ADR; compare the Ohio State journal (est. 1985). *Free online; pull from HeinOnline, Westlaw.*

- [@journal-negotiation-journal]. A quarterly peer-reviewed journal 'published by the Program on Negotiation at Harvard Law School'. It covers 'negotiation, mediation, other forms of alternative dispute resolution, and conflict resolution in general', including research reports, case studies, and teaching reports. (project analysis) Its interdisciplinary audience makes it a good source for empirical work on mediation practice and cross-cultural negotiation. *Free online; pull from HeinOnline, Westlaw.*

- [@journal-conflict-resolution-quarterly]. A Wiley journal sponsored by the Association for Conflict Resolution. A bookseller listing (Wildy & Sons) describes it as 'an academic publication ... sponsored by the Association for Conflict Resolution' with 4 issues a year, focused on 'the behavior, role and impact of third parties'. (project analysis) It is the journal of the organization that grew out of SPIDR, the Academy of Family Mediators, and CREnet, so it is a practitioner-oriented counterpart to the law reviews. *Not free online; pull from HeinOnline, Westlaw. Confirmed in a catalog; full text not read.*

- [@journal-aba-dispute-resolution-magazine]. The magazine of the ABA Section of Dispute Resolution. Section pages confirm the title, and the Section describes its publications as including 'a magazine, newsletter, books, and a podcast'. The Thomson Reuters author note for Mediation: Law, Policy & Practice mentions a co-author who co-chairs the magazine's Editorial Board. (project analysis) A practitioner magazine whose themed issues (e.g., Spring 2019, 'Family Matters') are useful for current practice trends; it is not a peer-reviewed journal. *Free online; pull from HeinOnline, Westlaw.*

- [@journal-nevada-law-journal-and-saltman-symposia]. The Nevada Law Journal is 'published by students of the William S. Boyd School of Law at the University of Nevada, Las Vegas' and publishes at least three issues a year. The Saltman Center for Conflict Resolution maintains a page collecting past NLJ symposium issues on conflict resolution: Vol. 3, Iss. 2 (Perspectives on Dispute Resolution in the Twenty-First Century); Vol. 8, Iss. 1 (Rethinking the Federal Arbitration Act); Vol. 10, Iss. 2 (Mindfulness, Emotions, and Ethics); Vol. 11, Iss. 2 (Conflict Resolution and the Economic Crisis, including Hollingshead on community mediation); and Vol. 18, Iss. 2 (re-inventing arbitration). (project analysis) No NLJ article specifically on Tribal ADR or peacemaking turned up in this pass. The Saltman symposia are relevant mainly for FAA critique and community-mediation sustainability. *Free online; pull from HeinOnline. Methods: arbitration, mediation.*

## ABA Section of Dispute Resolution and standards of conduct

- [@aba-section-of-dispute-resolution-ethics-resources]. The ABA Section of Dispute Resolution's ethics resource page. It lists 'ABA Model Rules and Standards Relevant to Attorneys and Neutrals' (Rules 2.4, 1.12, 3.3, 4.1 and 2.1 cmt. 5), the 2002 Model Rules amendments, the Model Standards of Conduct for Mediators (2005) with Reporter's Notes, the Code of Ethics for Arbitrators in Commercial Disputes, Ethics Codes for Settlement Negotiations (2002), and advisory opinions of the Section's Committee on Mediator Ethical Guidance. The Committee's opinions are available to members only. The Section's 'About' page describes itself as 'the largest dispute resolution community in the world' and says it publishes 'a magazine, newsletter, books, and a podcast.' (project analysis) This is the starting index for U.S. neutral-ethics materials. The Committee on Mediator Ethical Guidance opinions are a model for an advisory-opinion mechanism in a Tribal ADR code. *Free online. Methods: mediation, arbitration, negotiation.*

- [@aba-aaa-acr-2005-model-standards-of-conduct-for-mediators]. The August 2005 Model Standards, the principal U.S. mediator-ethics code, adopted jointly by the ABA (Section of Dispute Resolution), the AAA, and the Association for Conflict Resolution. They revise the 1994 Standards, which were developed by the AAA, the ABA Section, and the Society of Professionals in Dispute Resolution (SPIDR); the 1994 text was also opened, at uis.edu. Footnote 1 records the merger history: ACR 'is a merged organization of the Academy of Family Mediators, the Conflict Resolution Education Network and the Society of Professionals in Dispute Resolution (SPIDR).' Standard I defines self-determination as 'the act of coming to a voluntary, uncoerced decision in which each party makes free and informed choices as to process and outcome.' (project analysis) This is the default baseline a Tribal mediator code will be measured against. A Tribal code can adopt the structure (self-determination, impartiality, conflicts, competence, confidentiality, quality of process) while stating where community or kin participation changes the analysis. *Free online; pull from the publisher. Methods: mediation.*

- [@aaa-aba-2004-code-of-ethics-for-arbitrators]. The AAA/ABA Code of Ethics for Arbitrators in Commercial Disputes, 'Effective March 1, 2004'. It was first prepared in 1977 by a joint AAA-ABA committee and revised in 2003. It applies to commercial (not labor) arbitration. Its Note on Neutrality states the sponsors' view that 'it is preferable for all arbitrators including any party-appointed arbitrators to be neutral', and it lets parties in some domestic arbitrations opt for non-neutral party-appointed arbitrators. (project analysis) Relevant to Tribal commercial arbitration clauses and to any Tribal arbitrator roster. A Tribal code may adopt the Code by reference, or modify the disclosure rules for small communities where everyone is related or acquainted. Note: the ABA Section's ethics page lists this Code with the year 2002; the AAA's PDF gives an effective date of March 1, 2004. *Free online. Methods: arbitration.*

- [@aba-model-rule-2-4]. ABA Model Rule 2.4 defines when a lawyer serves as a third-party neutral: assisting two or more persons who are not the lawyer's clients to reach a resolution, for example as an arbitrator or mediator. Under 2.4(b) the lawyer-neutral must tell unrepresented parties that the lawyer does not represent them, and must explain the difference between a neutral's role and a representative's role when a party does not understand it. Comment [1] defines a third-party neutral as "a person, such as a mediator, arbitrator, conciliator or evaluator, who assists the parties, represented or unrepresented, in the resolution of a dispute or in the arrangement of a transaction." Comment [3] adds that, where appropriate, the lawyer should explain "the inapplicability of the attorney-client evidentiary privilege." Comment [4] sends conflicts, when a former neutral later represents a client in the same matter, to Rule 1.12. Comment [5] says a lawyer representing a client in ADR owes candor under Rule 3.3 before a tribunal, as in binding arbitration, and otherwise under Rule 4.1. (project analysis) Tribal codes often allow Tribal bar members, lay advocates, and non-lawyers to serve as neutrals. A model code can carry the 2.4(b) disclosure duty over to every neutral, not only lawyers. *Free online; pull from Westlaw. Methods: mediation, arbitration.*

- [@resolution-systems-institute-national-standards-court-connected-mediation]. RSI's library entry for the National Standards for Court-Connected Mediation Programs (RSI lists the date as Jan. 1, 1999; the UMA § 9 comment cites it as 'Center for Dispute Settlement, National Standards for Court-Connected Mediation Programs (1992)', see uniform-law-commission-mediation-act). The standards cover access, case selection and timing, mandatory attendance, mediator qualifications and ethics, confidentiality, the role of lawyers, 'inappropriate pressure to settle,' communications with the court, funding, liability, and enforceability of mediated agreements. (project analysis) For court-annexed Tribal programs, this is the closest program-level counterpart to the practitioner-level Model Standards of Conduct. Confirm the edition and any co-issuer (e.g. Institute of Judicial Administration) from the PDF title page. *Free online; pull from a library. Methods: mediation.*

## Federal

### The Federal Arbitration Act

- [@usc-9-2]. Section 2 is the core validity provision of the Federal Arbitration Act (FAA), derived from the Act of Feb. 12, 1925, ch. 213, § 2, 43 Stat. 883, and codified by the Act of July 30, 1947, ch. 392, 61 Stat. 670. It makes a written arbitration provision in a maritime transaction or a contract "evidencing a transaction involving commerce" "valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract or as otherwise provided in chapter 4"; the chapter 4 clause was added by Pub. L. 117-90, § 2(b)(1)(A) (2022) (LII credits and amendment note). (project analysis) Any Tribal ADR code provision on enforceability of arbitration agreements will be read against § 2, because federal and state courts asked to compel arbitration or enforce awards involving Tribal parties apply it to contracts involving commerce; the drafting should state clearly whether the Tribal code is meant to mirror § 2's equal-footing rule. *Free online; pull from Westlaw. Methods: arbitration.*

- [@usc-9-3-4]. Section 3 requires a federal court, once "satisfied that the issue involved in such suit or proceeding is referable to arbitration," to stay the trial on application of a party; § 4 lets a party aggrieved by another's refusal to arbitrate petition a district court for an order compelling arbitration, and requires a summary trial if the making of the agreement is in issue (both opened on LII; second page https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/9/4). Smith v. Spizzirri (2024) (see smith-v-spizzirri-2024) holds that the § 3 stay is mandatory when requested. (project analysis) These two sections are the procedural model most Tribal and state arbitration codes copy (stay of litigation plus an order compelling arbitration); a Tribal code can adopt the same pairing for its own courts while deciding separately who decides disputes over the existence of the agreement. *Free online; pull from Westlaw. Methods: arbitration.*

- [@usc-9-9-11]. Sections 9–11 set the post-award framework: § 9 provides that on application within one year the court "must grant" an order confirming the award "unless the award is vacated, modified, or corrected as prescribed in sections 10 and 11"; § 10(a) lists four grounds for vacatur (corruption/fraud/undue means, evident partiality, arbitrator misconduct, and exceeding powers); § 11 allows modification for evident miscalculation, awards on matters not submitted, and defects of form (pages for §§ 9 and 11 also opened on LII). Section 10(c) separately allows a nonparty adversely affected by an award issued under 5 U.S.C. § 580 (Administrative Dispute Resolution Act) to seek vacatur. Hall Street Associates v. Mattel (2008) treats the §§ 10–11 grounds as exclusive for expedited FAA review. (project analysis) A Tribal code's recognition-and-enforcement chapter will likely track these grounds; whether to add grounds (e.g., violation of Tribal public policy or custom) is a drafting choice the commentary should flag against the federal baseline. *Free online; pull from Westlaw. Methods: arbitration.*

- [@usc-9-201-301]. Chapter 2 of title 9 implements the 1958 New York Convention ("shall be enforced in United States courts in accordance with this chapter"), added by Pub. L. 91-368 (1970), 84 Stat. 692; LII notes the Convention entered into force for the United States on Dec. 29, 1970. Chapter 3 implements the 1975 Inter-American (Panama) Convention, added by Pub. L. 101-369 (1990), 104 Stat. 448, in force for the United States Oct. 27, 1990 (LII note at § 301, https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/9/301). (project analysis) Brief background only: relevant if a Tribal enterprise contracts with foreign parties, and as a reminder that recognition of awards across sovereign lines already has a treaty-based model that Tribal codes may borrow from. *Free online; pull from Westlaw. Methods: arbitration.*

- [@usc-9-401-402]. Chapter 4 of title 9, added by Pub. L. 117-90, § 2(a) (Mar. 3, 2022), 136 Stat. 26, lets a person alleging a sexual assault or sexual harassment dispute elect to void a predispute arbitration agreement or joint-action waiver; it applies to disputes or claims arising or accruing on or after Mar. 3, 2022 (Pub. L. 117-90, § 3, note under § 401). Notably for this project, the definitions and operative text expressly reference "Tribal" law: a sexual assault dispute includes conduct defined in 18 U.S.C. § 2246 "or similar applicable Tribal or State law," and § 402(a) covers a case "filed under Federal, Tribal, or State law." Section 402(b) reserves applicability and enforceability questions to a court, not an arbitrator, even if the agreement delegates them (opened § 401 at https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/9/401). (project analysis) A Tribal ADR code should include a parallel carve-out (or a cross-reference) so Tribal employers and Tribal courts do not enforce predispute arbitration of these claims contrary to the federal rule, and should consider whether Tribal law will define sexual harassment for purposes of § 401(4). *Free online; pull from Westlaw. Methods: arbitration.*

- [@usc-9-1-commerce-territory]. The FAA's definition of "commerce" expressly reaches commerce "in any Territory of the United States," along with commerce between territories, states and foreign nations. Read with 9 U.S.C. § 2, this is the textual basis for applying the FAA to arbitration agreements in the territories. (project analysis) Courts in the territories have read the FAA's reach in different ways. The V.I. Supreme Court applied it on an interstate-nexus theory without reaching the Territorial Clause question (Whyte v. Bockino, 2018), and Guam's Chapter 42A defers to the FAA where it applies. Keep this point brief in the book. The federal agent's 9 U.S.C. records should cover § 2. *Free online. Methods: arbitration.*

- [@southland-corp-v-keating-1984]. Southland, 465 U.S. 1 (decided Jan. 23, 1984), held that a California franchise-law provision requiring judicial resolution of claims conflicted with FAA § 2 and violated the Supremacy Clause, and that the FAA applies in state as well as federal courts. The Court stated that "[i]n enacting § 2 of the federal Act, Congress declared a national policy favoring arbitration and withdrew the power of the states to require a judicial forum for the resolution of claims which the contracting parties agreed to resolve by arbitration" (465 U.S. at 10, 104 S. Ct. at 858). (project analysis) Southland is the root of FAA preemption doctrine; Tribal code drafters need it to assess whether and how the FAA reaches Tribal courts, a question Southland itself (a state-court case) does not answer. *Free online; pull from Westlaw. Methods: arbitration.*

- [@att-mobility-v-concepcion-2011]. Concepcion (No. 09-893, decided Apr. 27, 2011) held that California's Discover Bank rule, which treated class waivers in consumer arbitration agreements as unconscionable, is preempted by the FAA. The Court read § 2's saving clause to permit invalidation by "generally applicable contract defenses, such as fraud, duress, or unconscionability," but not by defenses that apply only to arbitration (563 U.S. at 339, 131 S. Ct. at 1746). Although the saving clause preserves those defenses, "nothing in it suggests an intent to preserve state-law rules that stand as an obstacle to the accomplishment of the FAA's objectives" (id. at 343, 131 S. Ct. at 1748), and "[r]equiring the availability of classwide arbitration interferes with fundamental attributes of arbitration" (id. at 344, 131 S. Ct. at 1748). (project analysis) Important for drafting any Tribal consumer- or employment-protection limits on arbitration: rules that single out arbitration are the ones most exposed to an FAA preemption challenge in non-Tribal forums. *Free online; pull from Westlaw. Methods: arbitration.*

- [@hall-street-associates-v-mattel-2008]. Hall Street (No. 06-989, argued Nov. 7, 2007, decided Mar. 25, 2008) held that the FAA's §§ 10–11 grounds are exclusive for parties seeking expedited review under the FAA, so parties cannot contractually expand judicial review for legal error under the Act; the syllabus adds that the Court "decides nothing about other possible avenues for judicial enforcement of awards" (Held 2). The opinion holds "that §§ 10 and 11 respectively provide the FAA's exclusive grounds for expedited vacatur and modification" (552 U.S. at 584, 128 S. Ct. at 1403) but does "not purport to say that they exclude more searching review based on authority outside the statute as well," such as "state statutory or common law" (id. at 590, 128 S. Ct. at 1406). (project analysis) Relevant to whether a Tribal code should let parties opt into broader Tribal-court review of awards, a choice that federal law forecloses under the FAA but that a separate Tribal statute could permit for Tribal-court enforcement. *Free online; pull from Westlaw. Methods: arbitration.*

- [@epic-systems-v-lewis-2018]. Epic Systems (No. 16-285, decided together with Nos. 16-300 (Ernst & Young) and 16-307; argued Oct. 2, 2017, decided May 21, 2018) held that employment arbitration agreements requiring individualized proceedings must be enforced and that neither the FAA saving clause nor the National Labor Relations Act displaces that command. The opinion states that "Congress has instructed federal courts to enforce arbitration agreements according to their terms—including terms providing for individualized proceedings" (584 U.S. at 502, 138 S. Ct. at 1619). (project analysis) Directly relevant to Tribal employers (casinos, enterprises) that use employment arbitration, and to how a Tribal code treats collective-action waivers, now subject to the 2022 carve-out in 9 U.S.C. ch. 4 for sexual assault and harassment claims. *Free online; pull from Westlaw. Methods: arbitration.*

- [@smith-v-spizzirri-2024]. Smith v. Spizzirri (No. 22-1218, argued Apr. 22, 2024, decided May 16, 2024) holds that when a court finds a suit subject to arbitration and a party requests a stay, FAA § 3 requires a stay and the court may not dismiss. The opinion explains that "text, structure, and purpose all point to the same conclusion" (144 S. Ct. at 1176). The 601 U.S. 472 citation was seen in search results (Wikipedia, a Proskauer blog, openjurist), not on an official page; LII shows the slip opinion as "601 U.S. ___". (project analysis) Supports a Tribal code rule that the Tribal court retains the case on a stayed docket during arbitration, preserving its supervisory role for confirming or vacating the award. *Free online; pull from Westlaw. Methods: arbitration.*

### Federal courts: the Alternative Dispute Resolution Act of 1998 and court programs

- [@usc-28-651]. As amended by the Alternative Dispute Resolution Act of 1998, Pub. (project analysis) The § 651(a) definition ("other than an adjudication by a presiding judge") and the court-program mandate are a clean template for a Tribal court ADR chapter that sits alongside, rather than replacing, peacemaking (which the project treats as its own track). *Free online; pull from Westlaw. - [@usc-28-652]. Section 652(a) requires each district court, by local rule, to "require that litigants in all civil cases consider the use of an alternative dispute resolution process" and to offer at least one ADR process; courts may require ADR only for mediation, early neutral evaluation, and, with consent, arbitration. Section 652(b) allows exemptions in consultation with the bar and the U.S. Attorney, and § 652(d) directs courts to provide by local rule for confidentiality of ADR processes. - [@usc-28-654]. ### Uniform acts and adoption

- [@uniform-law-commission-mediation-act]. The official ULC text of the Uniform Mediation Act (UMA), approved at the ULC annual conference of August 10-17, 2001, amended in August 2003, and approved by the ABA on February 4, 2002 (title page). Its core is a privilege for 'mediation communications' (§ 4) with waiver, preclusion and exceptions (§§ 5-6), plus mediator disclosure duties (§ 9). The § 2(1) definition is the most widely copied statutory definition of mediation in the U.S., and the § 9 comment explains that the drafters chose not to set mediator qualifications. Scope (§ 3) reaches mediations required by statute, court or agency rule, or referred by a court, agency or arbitrator, and excludes collective-bargaining mediations, mediations 'conducted by a judge who might make a ruling on the case,' and some school and youth-correctional peer mediations. (project analysis) A Tribal code can adopt the UMA's privilege structure nearly word for word while leaving qualifications to Tribal law, which is the choice the drafters themselves made. The UMA's 'Person' definition covers 'government; governmental subdivision, agency, or instrumentality' but has no Tribe-specific definition, so a Tribal enactment would need its own 'tribal court' and 'proceeding' language. *Free online. Methods: mediation.*

- [@uniform-law-commission-mediation-act-enactment-status]. (project analysis) For a Tribal code, the RUAA is the most complete off-the-shelf model for court support of private arbitration. Its non-waivable sections (RUAA § 4) are a useful list of minimum fairness guarantees. *Free online. Methods: arbitration.*

- [@uniform-law-commission-arbitration-act-2000-enactment-status]. The Uniform Law Commission's enactment table for the Revised Uniform Arbitration Act (2000), with data "as of" November 21, 2026, lists 23 jurisdictions enacting the 2000 revision: Alaska, Arizona, Arkansas, Colorado, Connecticut, the District of Columbia, Florida, Hawaii, Kansas, Michigan, Minnesota, Nevada, New Jersey, New Mexico, North Carolina, North Dakota, Oklahoma, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Tennessee, Utah, Washington and West Virginia. A further column marks 29 jurisdictions as having enacted the prior 1956 version, several of which later adopted the revision. Puerto Rico and the U.S. Virgin Islands show no enactment. (project analysis) Puerto Rico's Ley 147-2024 follows the revised act in substance [@pr-ley-147-2024-ley-de-arbitraje] but is not counted here. *Free online. Methods: arbitration.*

- [@uniform-law-commission-revised-arbitration-act-summary]. A two-page ULC summary in the RUAA Enactment Kit. It states the reach of the original 1955 UAA and summarizes RUAA changes: default-rule structure, provisional remedies, mandatory disclosure by neutral arbitrators, judge-level immunity, discovery and summary disposition powers, punitive damages and fees 'if such an award is authorized by law in a civil action involving the same claim,' and consolidation. The summary gives no RUAA adoption count, and the RUAA community page shows enactments only in a non-text embedded map. (project analysis) The 49-jurisdiction figure is the only ULC-published count readable in text and refers to the original UAA, not the RUAA. *Free online. Methods: arbitration.*

- [@uniform-law-commission-collaborative-law-rules-act]. The ULC's combined Uniform Collaborative Law Rules/Act (UCLR/A), approved at the July 9-16, 2009 annual conference in Santa Fe and amended in 2010 (title page). The act can be enacted by court rule, by statute, or both. It requires a signed participation agreement, disqualifies the collaborative lawyers from later litigation, creates a privilege for collaborative law communications, and requires lawyers to screen for coercive or violent relationships. The ULC Summary in the Enactment Kit describes collaborative law as 'a form of limited scope representation (where an attorney is retained solely for the purpose of reaching a settlement, and expressly not for the purpose of litigation).' Nevada enacted it as NRS 38.400-38.575 (2011). (project analysis) Because the UCLR/A can be adopted as court rules, it is a ready model for a Tribal court that has rulemaking power but no legislature willing to act. *Free online. Methods: collaborative law.*

- [@uniform-law-commission-collaborative-law-act-enactment-status]. The Uniform Law Commission's enactment table for the Collaborative Law Act (2010), with data "as of" November 21, 2026, lists 28 enacting jurisdictions: Alabama, Arizona, Colorado, Connecticut, the District of Columbia, Florida, Hawaii, Illinois, Kentucky, Louisiana, Maryland, Michigan, Mississippi, Montana, Nevada, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New Mexico, North Carolina, North Dakota, Ohio, Oklahoma, Pennsylvania, Tennessee, Texas, Utah, Virginia and Washington. Its bill list shows Connecticut and Oklahoma enactments effective in 2025. *Free online. Methods: collaborative law.*

- [@uniform-law-commission-family-law-arbitration-act-2016]. The Uniform Family Law Arbitration Act (UFLAA), approved at the ULC annual conference in Stowe, Vermont, July 8-14, 2016. It governs arbitration of family-law disputes, including custody, with extra safeguards: arbitrator training in identifying domestic violence and child abuse (bracketed), and court review of child-related awards. Its § 2 definition of 'State' includes the bracketed option '[The term includes a federally recognized Indian tribe.]' (project analysis) This is a direct drafting model for how uniform acts can treat Tribes as states for recognition purposes. A Tribal code can also use the UFLAA's domestic-violence screening provisions in any family ADR chapter. *Free online. Methods: arbitration.*

- [@uniform-law-commission-family-law-arbitration-act-enactment-status]. The Uniform Law Commission's enactment table for the Family Law Arbitration Act (2016), with data "as of" November 21, 2026, lists 9 enacting jurisdictions: Arizona, the District of Columbia, Hawaii, Iowa, Montana, North Dakota, Pennsylvania, Utah and Washington, with bills introduced in Kansas and Massachusetts. Nevada shows no enactment. (project analysis) Few states have adopted it, so its optional treatment of a federally recognized Tribe as a state [@uniform-law-commission-family-law-arbitration-act-2016] has little enacted practice behind it yet. *Free online. Methods: arbitration.*

- [@uniform-law-commission-child-custody-jurisdiction-enforcement-act-104]. The UCCJEA (1997) is not an ADR act. It is included because § 104 is the leading uniform-law model for treating Tribes as states. Subsection (a) carves out proceedings governed by ICWA. Bracketed subsections (b) and (c) let a state treat a Tribe as a state for jurisdiction purposes and require recognition of Tribal custody determinations made in substantial conformity with the act. The comment notes 'a Tribe could adopt this Act as enabling legislation by simply replacing references to “this State” with “this Tribe.”' Nevada enacted both bracketed options (see nevada-revised-statutes-125a-215). (project analysis) This is the clearest reciprocity model for making Tribal mediated or arbitrated custody agreements, once reduced to Tribal-court orders, enforceable in state courts. *Free online.*

- [@uniform-law-commission-child-custody-jurisdiction-enforcement-act-enactment-status]. The Uniform Law Commission's enactment table for the Uniform Child Custody Jurisdiction and Enforcement Act (1997), with data "as of" November 21, 2026, lists 51 enacting jurisdictions: every state except Massachusetts (where it is marked "Introduced"), plus the District of Columbia and the U.S. Virgin Islands. Puerto Rico shows no enactment. The table does not say which enacting jurisdictions adopted the optional section 104 text treating Tribes as states; Nevada did [@nevada-revised-statutes-125a-215]. (project analysis) Because nearly every state has the act, section 104 is the most widely available route by which state courts treat Tribal custody orders as they would another state's. *Free online.*

### Nevada as a worked example

- [@nevada-revised-statutes-38-206]. Nevada's enactment of the Revised Uniform Arbitration Act (added by Stats. 2001, p. 1274). It applies to agreements to arbitrate made on or after October 1, 2001 (NRS 38.216). Nevada departs from the uniform text in NRS 38.219(1), which makes arbitration agreements enforceable 'except as otherwise provided in NRS 597.995' (Nevada's specific-authorization rule for arbitration clauses). NRS 38.241 lists the uniform vacatur grounds. (project analysis) This is the worked example of a state taking the ULC text with small local changes. A Tribal code can do the same, for example by adding Tribal-court-specific venue and sovereign-immunity provisions. *Free online. Methods: arbitration.*

- [@nevada-revised-statutes-38-250]. This is the statutory basis for Nevada's court-annexed ADR. NRS 38.250 (added 1991; most recently amended 2025) requires district-court civil damages actions arising in Nevada with no more than $100,000 per plaintiff in issue to go to nonbinding arbitration, unless the parties have agreed or are required to use another alternative method. Such agreements must be made at the time of the dispute, in writing, and knowingly and voluntarily, or they are void. NRS 38.253-38.255 direct the Supreme Court to adopt program rules and list case types that must be excluded. NRS 38.258 authorizes 'settlement conferences and other alternative methods of resolving disputes, including, without limitation, mediation and a short trial.' NRS 38.249, added in 2025, defines 'short trial.' (project analysis) This is a compact model of a legislature delegating ADR program design to the high court, which fits Tribal systems where the court drafts procedural rules. *Free online. Methods: nonbinding (court-annexed) arbitration, mediation, settlement conference, short trial.*

- [@nevada-revised-statutes-38-300]. This part makes mediation, or by agreement a referral to a dispute resolution program run by the state Real Estate Division, a condition before suit on claims about the governing documents and assessments of a common-interest community. Under NRS 38.330 the parties pick from a Division-maintained mediator list, mediation must finish within 60 days of the claim, and sessions are capped at 3 hours unless extended. NRS 38.350 tolls the statute of limitations. (project analysis) This is an example of a 'mediate before you sue' gate tied to an agency roster, which is relevant to Tribal housing-authority or land-assignment disputes. *Free online. Methods: mediation, arbitration.*

- [@nevada-revised-statutes-38-400]. Nevada's enactment of the Uniform Collaborative Law Act (added by Stats. 2011, p. 184). It includes the participation-agreement requirements, the disqualification rule and its low-income and government-party exceptions (NRS 38.515, 38.520), screening for coercive or violent relationships (NRS 38.540), and the privilege (NRS 38.550-38.560). (project analysis) This confirms that Nevada adopted the ULC collaborative-law text but not the ULC mediation text. *Free online. Methods: collaborative law.*

- [@nevada-revised-statutes-48-109]. Nevada's general mediation-confidentiality provision in its evidence code, added in 1991 and amended in 1993 (source note), well before the UMA. It treats a mediation session as settlement negotiations, bars admission and discovery of statements 'not otherwise discoverable or obtainable,' and shields the mediator from civil process. (project analysis) Its existence, together with the absence of any UMA text in NRS, supports the conclusion that Nevada has NOT adopted the Uniform Mediation Act. It is also a useful contrast for a Tribal drafter: a three-subsection rule versus the UMA's privilege-holder and exception structure. *Free online. Methods: mediation.*

- [@nevada-revised-statutes-3-475]. Nevada's mandatory custody and visitation mediation statutes. NRS 3.475 covers counties of 700,000 or more (Clark) and NRS 3.500 covers counties of 100,000 to 700,000 (Washoe), with smaller counties allowed to opt in. Each district court must set up the program by rule approved by the Supreme Court. Programs must let the court exclude cases for good cause, 'including' a history of child abuse or domestic violence. They must also set training, apprenticeship and continuing-education standards that 'must not be restricted to any particular professional or educational training,' bar the mediator from reporting anything except whether the dispute was resolved, and use sliding-scale fees. (project analysis) This is a very good statutory template for a Tribal family-mediation program. Note that the domestic-violence exclusion is discretionary ('authorize the court to exclude'), not mandatory. *Free online. Methods: mediation.*

- [@nevada-rules-governing-alternative-dispute-resolution]. The Nevada Supreme Court's consolidated ADR rules. They were adopted by order of December 22, 2004, effective March 1, 2005 (the cover says March 3, 2005), with amendments through March 12, 2026. Part A gives definitions and lists the forms of court-annexed ADR that districts with a county of 100,000 or more must offer: arbitration, mediation, settlement conference, and others contemplated by NRS 38.250. Part B (NAR) runs the mandatory, nonbinding Court Annexed Arbitration Program with trial de novo. Part C (NMR) creates a voluntary Court Annexed Mediation Program that parties may enter by stipulation instead of arbitration, with mediator qualifications, fee caps, and a confidentiality-and-immunity rule (NMR 11). (project analysis) Part A's definitions are clean, rule-ready texts that can be adapted directly into a Tribal ADR code. *Free online. Methods: nonbinding (court-annexed) arbitration, arbitration, mediation, settlement conference.*

- [@nevada-short-trial-rules]. The Short Trial Rules were adopted June 7, 2000 (effective July 6, 2000), readopted with amendments by order of December 22, 2004 (effective January 1, 2005), and amended through March 12, 2026. The program is mandatory in districts with mandatory court-annexed arbitration. A case enters it when a party requests trial de novo after arbitration, or by stipulation instead of arbitration. Juries have four members by default (six, or eight for good cause), each side gets 3.5 hours, and judgments are capped at $100,000 per plaintiff unless the parties stipulate otherwise. The statute lists the short trial among 'alternative methods of resolving disputes' (NRS 38.258), and NMR 9 sends unresolved mediations to the short trial program. (project analysis) It is an adjudicative, binding jury trial, not an advisory summary jury trial, so it is proposed as a new method ('short-trial'). *Free online. Methods: short trial.*

- [@nevada-foreclosure-mediation-rules]. Rules adopted by the Nevada Supreme Court under ADKT 435 (order of June 30, 2009, effective July 31, 2009), with the cover showing amendments through Oct. 31, 2025. They implement the owner-occupied residential foreclosure mediation required by NRS chapter 107 (NRS 107.086). The current Rule 1 limits the rules to defaults and documented hardships 'on or after December 2, 2016, and not sold prior to June 12, 2017.' District courts assign mediators, and the AOC keeps the roster. A second source, the Nevada AOC's 'Foreclosure Mediator Program Overview' page, confirms that the AOC 'is responsible for appointing foreclosure mediators' and that 'This list will be used by Nevada's District Courts to assign mediators.' RSI's Nevada page says 'The state launched a foreclosure mediation program in July 2009.' (project analysis) This is mediation tied to a statutory condition on non-judicial sale, with a mandatory document-exchange step. It is classed as 'mediation' rather than a new method; the special features are captured in topics. *Free online. Methods: mediation.*

- [@eighth-judicial-district-court-nevada-adr-office]. The Las Vegas (Eighth Judicial District) ADR office page describes three programs, arbitration, mediation and the Short Trial Program, and frames them as a 'multi-door' courthouse. It describes arbitration as 'an adversarial, typically non-binding procedure' and mediation as 'an informal and non-adversarial process.' The page carries a last-modified date of July 15, 2020 and links a Foreclosure Mediation sub-page that was not opened. (project analysis) This shows how the statewide rules are carried out in the largest district. A Tribal court could adopt a similar single ADR office. *Free online. Methods: nonbinding (court-annexed) arbitration, mediation, short trial.*

- [@second-judicial-district-court-nevada-custody-mediation]. The Reno (Second Judicial District) Family Services page for custody mediation. It says the program started at the court in 1991 and averages 48 mediations per month, that mediation discussions are confidential, and that an agreement reached is written up by the mediator. The page also links a Dependency Mediation program (not opened). It cites no statute, but NRS 3.500 requires such a program in Washoe County. (project analysis) This is a court-staffed (in-house mediator) model, in contrast with Clark County's roster model. *Free online. Methods: mediation.*

- [@resolution-systems-institute-court-adr-across-us-nevada]. RSI's 'Court ADR Across the U.S.' is a 50-state (plus D.C.) directory of statewide court-ADR statutes, rules, reports and contacts. It is the best free national survey of court-connected ADR. The Nevada entry lists NRS chapters 3, 38 and 107, the Rules Governing ADR, the Foreclosure Mediation Rules, and NRAP 16 (the Supreme Court Settlement Program, which 'utilizes mediation for civil appeals cases'). The page contains an editing error: its summary note says 'This is a summary of state court ADR in Nebraska.' (project analysis) Use it as a finding aid, not as authority. Its rule-revision dates (e.g. Rules Governing ADR 'Jan. 1, 2020') lag behind the LCB text. *Free online. Methods: mediation, nonbinding (court-annexed) arbitration, settlement conference.*

### Court-connected programs in other states

- [@michigan-court-rules-2-410-2-403]. Michigan's general court-ADR rule (MCR 2.410) makes all civil cases subject to ADR unless otherwise provided. It defines ADR to include settlement conferences, case evaluation (MCR 2.403), mediation (MCR 2.411), and domestic-relations and child-protection mediation. Each trial court using ADR must adopt a written ADR plan, and a party unable to pay must not be ordered into ADR when free or low-cost services are unavailable. Case evaluation (MCR 2.403) submits money-damage cases to a panel 'composed of 3 persons' who place a value on the case. It is a distinctive Michigan process, proposed here as a new method. (project analysis) The ADR-plan requirement and the indigent-access rule are both strong provisions to carry into a Tribal code. The plan may also refer cases to Community Dispute Resolution Program centers. *Free online. Methods: settlement conference, case evaluation, mediation.*

## Territories

### Puerto Rico

- [@pr-ley-147-2024-ley-de-arbitraje]. Puerto Rico's current general arbitration statute. It repeals Ley Núm. 376 de 8 de mayo de 1951 [Commercial Arbitration Act] and Ley 10-2012 [International Commercial Arbitration Act] (Exposición de Motivos; enacting clause). Chapters 1-3 follow the Revised Uniform Arbitration Act (called the "LUAR" in Spanish) and Chapter 4 follows the UNCITRAL Model Law. The chapters are Cap. 1 (Arts. 1.01-1.16), Cap. 2 "Arbitraje Ordinario" [ordinary arbitration] (Arts. 2.01-2.17), Cap. 3 "Proceso Arbitral Abreviado" [expedited arbitral process] (Arts. 3.01-3.18) and Cap. 4 "Arbitraje Comercial Internacional" [international commercial arbitration] (from Art. 4.01). Art. 2.03(a) makes a written agreement to arbitrate present or future disputes "válido, exigible e irrevocable" [valid, enforceable and irrevocable], except on grounds that void contracts. Art. 1.02 leaves other laws on arbitrability untouched. (project analysis) This is a rare U.S. example of one code that puts an RUAA-based domestic regime, an expedited track and a Model Law international chapter side by side. A Tribal code drafter could use that structure as a template. The OGP text is an unofficial compilation, so the exact wording should be checked against L.P.R.A. *Free online; pull from Westlaw, Lexis. Methods: arbitration.*

- [@mcconnell-valdes-2024-new-arbitration-act-puerto-rico]. An English-language client alert from a Puerto Rico law firm on Act 147-2024. It says the Act was signed August 9, 2024, was drafted "in accordance with the Revised Uniform Arbitration Act," repeals Act 376 of 1951 and Act 10-2012, and "will take effect on February 5, 2025." It also says the Act does not apply to arbitrations commenced, or rights accrued, before the effective date. (project analysis) This is a secondary source, useful mainly as an English summary and for the effective date, which the OGP excerpt read here did not state. No individual author is named. *Free online. Methods: arbitration.*

- [@pr-ley-376-1951-arbitraje-comercial]. Puerto Rico's arbitration statute from 1951 until Ley 147-2024 repealed it, codified at 32 L.P.R.A. §§ 3201-3229 (Title 32, Chapter 259). Section 3201 let two or more parties agree in writing to arbitrate an existing or future dispute, and made such an agreement "valid, requirable and irrevocable except for the grounds prescribed by law for the reversal of an agreement" (official English translation). Westlaw's Leyes de Puerto Rico Anotadas lists §§ 3201-3229 as "Derogadas" [repealed] by Ley 147 of August 9, 2024, art. 5.01, effective 180 days after August 9, 2024. Constructora Estelar v. Autoridad de Edificios Públicos, 183 D.P.R. 1 (2011), says the Act "regula el arbitraje comercial en Puerto Rico" [governs commercial arbitration in Puerto Rico] and was modeled on California and other state laws, New York procedure and the Federal Arbitration Act (183 D.P.R. at 31). (project analysis) It matters historically and for reading the large body of Puerto Rico arbitration case law decided under it. *Free online; pull from Westlaw, Lexis. Methods: arbitration. Details need checking.*

- [@pr-constructora-estelar-2011-tspr-139]. Opinion by Juez Asociado Estrella Martínez restating the Puerto Rico Supreme Court's doctrine that "en Puerto Rico existe una vigorosa política pública a favor del arbitraje" [Puerto Rico has a vigorous public policy in favor of arbitration] (183 D.P.R. 1, 30; slip op. p. 35). It also holds that doubts about arbitrability are resolved in favor of arbitration. The Court describes Ley 376-1951 (183 D.P.R. at 6, 30-31; slip op. pp. 2, 36). In footnote 50 (183 D.P.R. at 31) it notes that the FAA "aplica a contratos en el comercio interestatal" [applies to contracts in interstate commerce]. It does not hold that the FAA applies generally in Puerto Rico. (project analysis) This is a citable statement of Puerto Rico's pro-arbitration policy, which predates Ley 147-2024. *Free online; pull from Westlaw. Methods: arbitration.*

- [@pr-ley-19-1983-foros-informales]. The enabling statute for Puerto Rico's court-run community dispute forums. Art. 1 authorizes "programas o centros que sirvan como foros informales" [programs or centers serving as informal forums]. Art. 2 has the Tribunal Supremo adopt the operating rules, and Art. 7 funds the program through the Judicial Branch budget. Art. 3(e) requires voluntary participation. Art. 4 calls for informal, non-adversarial handling close to the community, with neutrals who cannot impose sanctions. Art. 5 makes case information privileged and confidential. Art. 4 was amended by Ley 165-2014. (project analysis) This is a compact model of a legislature authorizing the judiciary to build community-based informal forums, a design a Tribal code could adapt. *Free online; pull from Westlaw, Lexis. Methods: mediation, negotiation.*

- [@pr-tspr-1998-reglamento-metodos-alternos]. The Puerto Rico Supreme Court's ADR rules, effective January 1, 1999 (Regla 10.01). Regla 2.01 creates the Negociado de Métodos Alternos para la Solución de Conflictos [Bureau of Alternative Methods of Conflict Resolution], attached to the Office of the Chief Justice. Regla 2.02(k) has it coordinate the court centers' mediation centers. Regla 1.03 defines mediación [mediation], arbitraje [arbitration, binding or non-binding] and evaluación neutral del caso [neutral case evaluation]. Courts may refer cases on their own initiative (Regla 3.02(a)). For mediation, only the initial orientation session is compulsory (Reglas 3.04(b)(1), 3.05(a)), and Regla 7.01(b) leaves the choice to mediate with the parties. Regla 6.01 makes communications "confidencial y privilegiada" [confidential and privileged], with exceptions for child abuse and planned crimes. Regla 7.03 excludes certain criminal, civil-rights and public-interest cases. (project analysis) The mandatory-orientation and voluntary-mediation split is a useful middle path for a Tribal code. *Free online; pull from Westlaw. Methods: mediation, arbitration, nonbinding (court-annexed) arbitration, early neutral evaluation.*

- [@pr-poder-judicial-centros-de-mediacion-folleto]. A Judicial Branch public-education brochure, undated, describing the Centros de Mediación de Conflictos. It says they offer free, confidential, voluntary mediation for community, family, commercial, mortgage, interpersonal and labor disputes. Examples include debts, neighbor conflicts, landlord-tenant matters, child support and workplace harassment. Services can be requested online through the Tribunal Electrónico, in person, or by court referral. (project analysis) Online intake is the only basis for the online-dispute-resolution tag; the brochure does not describe online sessions. A related court document, 'Fui citado a CMC videoconferencia', appeared in search results but was not opened. Use this source for program description, not for legal rules. *Free online. Methods: mediation.*

- [@cruz-perez-2017-realidad-uso-mediacion]. An essay by a UPR-Cayey business professor arguing that mediation is underused in Puerto Rico despite Ley 19-1983, which "autorizó el establecimiento de foros informales para la solución de disputas" [authorized informal forums for dispute resolution] (p. 52). He blames low public and professional awareness, no uniform referral system, no follow-up on compliance with agreements, and public distrust. He recommends mandatory orientation, referral mechanisms, compliance monitoring and required ADR courses in law schools. (project analysis) It is useful for a commentary note on why authorizing ADR is not enough without referral and follow-through. Ceiba is a university journal, not a law review. *Free online. Methods: mediation.*

### Guam

- [@gca-7-ch-42a-guam-international-arbitration]. Guam's arbitration statute, enacted by P.L. 27-081:3 (Apr. 30, 2004). Section 42A101(b) says it "is based on the UNCITRAL Model Law and should be interpreted having regard to international comity." Under § 42A101(d), it applies to both international commercial arbitration and domestic arbitration, and § 42A101(g) defines domestic arbitration as one seated in Guam. Section 42A101(c) treats Guam as a "State" for the chapter's purposes. Some enforcement provisions (§§ 42A701-42A702) apply only where neither the FAA nor the New York Convention applies. (project analysis) Guam is unusual among U.S. jurisdictions in running domestic arbitration on the Model Law instead of the UAA or RUAA. That gives a Tribal drafter a tested territorial example of a Model-Law-based code. *Free online. Methods: arbitration.*

- [@gca-7-ch-42-civil-arbitration-repealed]. Guam's former general arbitration chapter. The Compiler's note reads "Entire chapter repealed by P.L. 27-081:2 (April 30, 2004)." The same 2004 law enacted Chapter 42A (international and domestic arbitration on the UNCITRAL Model Law), Chapter 42B (court-referred ADR), and Chapters 43A and 43B (mediation). The CNMI Supreme Court in PAC United v. Guam Concrete Builders, 2002 MP 15 ¶ 29, relied on former 7 GCA § 42101 as making arbitration agreements irrevocable and enforceable under Guam law. (project analysis) Contrary to the assumption in the task, the materials read here do not show that Guam enacted the Uniform Arbitration Act or the RUAA after 2004. *Free online. Methods: arbitration.*

- [@gca-7-ch-42b-court-referred-arbitration-adr]. Court-annexed arbitration for Superior Court civil actions, with custody cases excluded (§ 42B101(a)). By stipulation, any non-custody action may go to binding arbitration, mediation, early neutral evaluation or another method (§ 42B101(c)). A court-annexed award becomes final unless "a request for a de novo trial is filed within thirty days" (§ 42B103). A party who requests a trial de novo and does no better at trial pays specified costs and fees, absent substantial economic hardship (§ 42B104). An award not challenged becomes a judgment that "is not subject to appeal" (§ 42B105). Funding comes through Judicial Council fees (§ 42B102). (project analysis) This is a standard California-style judicial arbitration scheme and a ready model for non-binding court-annexed arbitration with cost-shifting. *Free online. Methods: arbitration, nonbinding (court-annexed) arbitration, mediation, early neutral evaluation.*

- [@gca-7-ch-42c-ending-forced-arbitration-2022]. Guam's local counterpart to the federal Ending Forced Arbitration of Sexual Assault and Sexual Harassment Act of 2021. At the election of the person alleging the conduct, "no predispute arbitration agreement or predispute joint-action waiver shall be valid or enforceable" for cases filed under federal or local law (§ 42C104). (project analysis) This is a territorial example of carving categories of disputes out of pre-dispute arbitration, a policy choice a Tribal ADR code will face. Whether this local rule survives FAA preemption where the FAA applies is a separate question not researched here. *Free online. Methods: arbitration.*

- [@gca-7-ch-43a-guam-mediation-chapter]. Guam's general mediation statute, in five articles. They cover agreements to mediate (which § 43A103 makes valid, irrevocable and enforceable), stays and tolling, evidence and non-disclosure (§§ 43A201-43A204), termination, costs and enforcement of settlements, and mediator ineligibility to serve as arbitrator, immunity and ethics or training standards (§§ 43A501-43A507). Section 43A102(a) defines mediation as a process in which neutrals facilitate communication between disputants "to assist them in reaching a mutually acceptable agreement." (project analysis) The text does not say it is based on the Uniform Mediation Act, and its structure (evidence rule, enforceable agreements to mediate) differs from the UMA's privilege model. Compare it with the UMA in commentary. *Free online. Methods: mediation.*

- [@gca-7-ch-43b-court-referred-mediation]. Authorizes the Supreme Court of Guam to "establish and promulgate rules and procedures for mediation" (§ 43B601(a)) and allows referral to mediation "Upon stipulation of the parties" (§ 43B601(b)). The Judicial Council may levy fees to fund the program (§ 43B602). The Compiler notes the program began under Supreme Court Promulgation Order 06-005-01 (July 20, 2006), Interim Rules for the Mediation Pilot Project for Civil Actions. Supreme Court rules go to the Legislature and are deemed approved unless it acts within 90 days plus one legislative day. (project analysis) The legislative-review mechanism for court ADR rules is a governance design point worth noting for Tribal courts and councils. *Free online. Methods: mediation.*

- [@gca-7-4301-alternate-dispute-resolution-office]. Creates within the Judicial Branch an Alternate Dispute Resolution Office "under the direction of the Chief Justice and the Judicial Council" (§ 4301). Section 4302 charges it to "facilitate dispute resolution without litigation" and to provide training and services for mediation, conciliation, arbitration and other means of settling disputes "among residents of Guam." Enacted in 1987 as former CCP §§ 205-206. (project analysis) This is a short statutory charter for a court ADR office, a structural piece a model Tribal code could copy almost word for word. *Free online. Methods: mediation, conciliation, arbitration.*

- [@gca-19-ch-7-guam-conciliation-law]. Gives the Superior Court "conciliation jurisdiction" over domestic-relations disputes (§§ 7101-7108), added to the Civil Code by P.L. 15-26 according to an unofficial mirror. Section 7111 (added by P.L. 24-239:31) bars the court from ordering parties into conciliation or mediation "when either party asserts that family violence has occurred." In protection-order matters it allows those services only if the victim requests them, a qualified professional screens the case, a mediator trained in family violence conducts it, and a support person may attend. (project analysis) This is a strong, citable statutory model for family-violence screening and opt-in safeguards. A Tribal ADR code covering domestic relations should address the same issue. *Free online. Methods: conciliation, mediation.*

- [@guam-superior-court-local-rules-cvr-16]. The Superior Court's civil case-management rules. CVR 16.2(a)(5) requires counsel at the meet-and-confer to discuss "the timing and appropriateness of mediation or other forms of alternative dispute resolution." CVR 16.6(a) allows any party to request a judicial settlement conference, which is a privileged proceeding "not reported or recorded." Under CVR 16.6(b), parties "may be ordered into mediation" or may stipulate to it. The CVR 16.x provisions were adopted by Promulgation Orders 06-006-01 and 06-006-02 (2007) and amended by 06-006-16 (2014). The compilation is dated Oct. 13, 2025. (project analysis) It shows how a court plugs ADR into scheduling orders without a standalone ADR code. *Free online. Methods: mediation, settlement conference.*

### U.S. Virgin Islands

- [@vi-super-ct-r-40-civil-mediation]. The Virgin Islands' original court-annexed civil mediation rule, adopted Oct. 14, 1994, effective Nov. 16, 1994, and amended Oct. 12, 1995. - [@vi-ocean-pest-v-rebelly-2026-vi-5]. Opinion by Chief Justice Hodge construing V.I. R. Civ. P. 90, the current Superior Court mediation rule, which "applies only to court-ordered mediation in civil cases pending in the Superior Court" (¶ 16). The Court held that a mediated agreement can be enforced under Rule 90(h)(3) only if it was "reduced to writing and signed by the parties and their counsel" under Rule 90(h)(2) (¶ 17). Because one plaintiff never signed, the enforcement order was vacated and the claims reinstated. The opinion also quotes Rule 90's privilege provisions (90(d)(8)-(9), ¶ 20) and its mandatory sanctions for failing to appear or participate in good faith (90(f)(2), ¶ 31). (project analysis) This is the best current primary source on V.I. court mediation, and it shows why a code should spell out the formalities for mediated settlements. Note that the court's web page for this opinion carries a mismatched URL slug ('palton_vs__v_i_government'). *Free online; pull from Westlaw. Methods: mediation.*

- [@vi-whyte-v-bockino-2018]. Opinion by Chief Justice Hodge, 69 V.I. 749 (2018 WL 4191523), affirming an order compelling arbitration of a store manager's wrongful-termination claims. Four holdings. (1) "Arbitration and award" in Rule 8(c)(1) "plainly means when arbitration has already taken place and an arbitrator has determined an award," so a defendant need not raise the right to arbitrate in its answer (at 756-57); the Court declined to follow Rhode Island's contrary reading (at 755-56). (2) The FAA governs "because there is an interstate nexus" (at 760, 768). The Court noted that 9 U.S.C. § 1 reaches commerce "in any Territory," so the FAA "may very well apply to the territory by way of the Territorial Clause," but it did not decide between that and the Commerce Clause (at 759-60). The nexus burden is "relatively low" (at 761); a Chicago notice address in the contract, and the manager's control over goods shipped to St. Croix, were enough, "as even the slightest nexus is sufficient" (at 762). (3) The arbitration clause survived the written contract's expiration through an implied continuation of employment and a survival clause (at 763-66). (4) No waiver: "Prejudice is the touchstone," and Pueblo's limited litigation conduct caused none (at 766-68). Footnote 5 quotes 24 V.I.C. § 74a, which allows employment arbitration only by written post-dispute agreement, and records Whyte's concession that the FAA would preempt it if the FAA applies through the Commerce Clause and an interstate nexus is found (at 757 n.5, citing Allen v. HOVENSA, 59 V.I. 430, 442 n.2 (2013)). The trial-court decision is Whyte v. Bockino, 69 V.I. 83 (Super. Ct. 2017) (Brady, J.). (project analysis) For the model code, the opinion shows a territorial high court applying the FAA without settling its constitutional basis, and it shows that a local rule restricting pre-dispute employment arbitration (§ 74a) gives way whenever the FAA reaches the contract. The earlier note that no V.I. arbitration statute was found is superseded: § 74a is one, though limited to employment. *Not free online; pull from Westlaw, Lexis. Methods: arbitration.*

- [@vi-code-24-74a-employment-arbitration]. As quoted in Whyte v. (a)). An employer "may not require an employee to arbitrate a dispute as a condition of employment" (subsec. (b)). The Whyte footnote records the employee's concession that the FAA would preempt the section where the FAA applies, citing Allen v. HOVENSA, L.L.C., 59 V.I. 430, 442 n.2 (V.I. 2013). (project analysis) This is a territorial statute that allows only post-dispute arbitration in employment, in the same spirit as the federal Ending Forced Arbitration Act's narrower carve-out. In practice the FAA displaces it whenever the contract shows an interstate nexus, which Whyte says is a low bar. - [@usdc-vi-alternative-dispute-resolution-program]. The program page of the federal district court for the Virgin Islands, which is distinct from the local Superior Court. ### Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands

- [@nmi-pac-united-v-guam-concrete-2002-mp-15]. The CNMI Supreme Court enforced an arbitration agreement even though "There is no specific law, statute or custom, in the CNMI governing arbitration" (¶ 15). Under the rule of decision in 7 CMC § 3401, it applied U.S. common law and Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws § 218 "In the absence of written or local customary law" (¶¶ 15-16). (project analysis) This is a useful model for how a court without an arbitration statute can enforce agreements through common law. - [@cmc-8-2904-carolinian-custom-family-land]. Codifies Carolinian (Refaluwasch) custom on family land. A customary trustee holds the land, and on the trustee's death title passes in a statutory order beginning with the oldest surviving sister (subsec. (a)(1)-(5)). All members share use rights (subsec. (b)). Absent family agreement, the trustee may not sell, lease, mortgage or partition (subsec. (c)). The family can vary the rules by consent: "Unless the family consents or agrees otherwise." If no one qualifies and the family members with use rights cannot agree on a trustee, the court selects one according to "the customs of the Carolinian people" (subsec. (a)(6)). (project analysis) This is not an ADR process. It is a statutory hierarchy that puts family consensus first and leaves custom-guided court decision as the fallback, which is useful for a model code's interface between custom and courts. The statute uses the English term 'Carolinian'; Refaluwasch is the community's own name. *Free online.*

- [@ristroph-2008-survival-customary-law-nmi]. ### American Samoa

- [@asca-43-0302-certificate-irreconcilable-dispute]. A statutory pre-filing process. Before "any action relating to controversies over communal land or matai titles may be commenced," each party must file a certificate signed by the Secretary of Samoan Affairs or deputy. The certificate must state that the parties appeared personally, "without an attorney or counsel," on at least two occasions before the Secretary and two designees, and that an attempt was made to resolve the controversy (subsec. (a)(1)). Each party must receive at least 20 days' notice per appearance (subsec. (a)(2)). The certificate records the times of each appearance and the findings and conclusions, "including a statement of the reason why the controversy could not be resolved" (subsec. (a)(3)-(4)). Subsection (b), added in 1973, says the certificate "may not be required" before a temporary restraining order to prevent irreparable damage. The section comes from PL 12-59 (1972) and PL 13-39 (1973). The Bar's case notes include Tavai P. Kaleopa v. Nia-Marie & Company (1980), holding that the requirement "is procedural and not jurisdictional." The same text is reproduced in Hunkin v. Galea'i, 8 A.S.R.3d 260 (Land & Titles Div. 2004). (project analysis) This is a documented-in-law example of a mandatory customary-authority step before court, and the closest territorial analogue to a Tribal code requiring a traditional process before Tribal court. The Secretary is appointed "from among the leading registered matais" (Rev. Const. Am. Samoa art. IV, § 4). *Not free online; pull from Westlaw. Methods: Office of Samoan Affairs pre-filing hearing.*

- [@as-hunkin-v-galeai-2004]. It describes the process as "an opportunity to meet, discuss, debate, and seek consensus," criticizes parties who treat it "as more of an obstacle to litigation," and recognizes an affirmative duty on the Secretary to facilitate resolution. (project analysis) Courts here enforce a customary pre-filing step strictly, which is directly relevant to drafting the consequences of skipping a required traditional step in a Tribal code. *Free online; pull from Westlaw. ## Where Tribal and other systems meet

### Full faith and credit and comity

- [@usc-28-1738]. The general statute that implements the Full Faith and Credit Clause. Its text names States, Territories, and Possessions; it does not mention Indian tribes. Wilson v. Marchington, 127 F.3d 805 (9th Cir. 1997), held that § 1738 does not reach Tribal judgments, so recognition in federal court rests on comity. (project analysis) Most Tribal ADR outcomes, and the Tribal court judgments that confirm them, will therefore be recognized outside the Tribe under comity, state statutes, or court rules, not under § 1738. The exceptions are the subject-specific statutes (25 U.S.C. § 1911(d), 18 U.S.C. § 2265, 28 U.S.C. § 1738B). *Free online; pull from Westlaw.*

- [@usc-25-1911]. Section 101 of the Indian Child Welfare Act of 1978 (Pub. L. 95-608, 92 Stat. 3071). Subsection (d) requires the United States, every State, every territory or possession, and every Indian tribe to give full faith and credit to Tribal 'public acts, records, and judicial proceedings' in Indian child custody proceedings. Wilson v. Marchington treats § 1911(d) as one of the few express congressional extensions of full faith and credit to Tribes, and reads Congress's choice to name Tribes here as evidence that 28 U.S.C. § 1738 does not reach them. (project analysis) For a model Tribal ADR code, this means a mediated or peacemaking outcome that a Tribal court adopts in an ICWA-covered custody matter is entitled to full faith and credit. An ADR outcome in most other kinds of matters is not; it depends on comity or on a specific statute. *Free online; pull from Westlaw.*

- [@usc-18-2265]. The Violence Against Women Act full-faith-and-credit provision. Under subsection (a), a protection order issued consistently with subsection (b) by a State, Indian tribe, or territory must be given full faith and credit and enforced by the courts of every other State, Tribe, and territory. Subsection (e) affirms that a Tribal court has 'full civil jurisdiction to issue and enforce protection orders involving any person' in matters arising in the Tribe's Indian country. (project analysis) A Tribal ADR code should not send protection-order matters to mediation in a way that delays or replaces an enforceable order. Any agreement reached in ADR that is meant to travel to other jurisdictions should be entered as a court order that meets § 2265(b). *Free online; pull from Westlaw.*

- [@usc-28-1738b]. The Full Faith and Credit for Child Support Orders Act. Subsection (a) requires each State's appropriate authorities to enforce, according to its terms, a child support order that a court of another State made consistently with the section. Subsection (b)(9) defines 'State' to include 'Indian country (as defined in section 1151 of title 18)', so Tribal child support orders are covered. (project analysis) A child support agreement reached in Tribal mediation or peacemaking should be entered as an order of a Tribal court, or of another Tribal tribunal with authority to make support orders, so that it qualifies under § 1738B. *Free online; pull from Westlaw.*

- [@hilton-v-guyot-1895]. The foundational U.S. Supreme Court decision on comity and the recognition of foreign-nation judgments (opinion of Justice Gray). Wilson v. Marchington adopts Hilton's definition of comity, quoting 159 U.S. at 163-64, and its due-process elements as the 'guiding principles' for recognizing Tribal judgments, while rejecting Hilton's reciprocity rule. (project analysis) A model Tribal code may want to include a reciprocal recognition provision (comity for state and foreign judgments that meet stated standards) to encourage recognition of Tribal outcomes in return, even though Wilson does not require reciprocity. *Free online; pull from Westlaw.*

- [@wilson-v-marchington-1997]. Ninth Circuit (Wright, Reinhardt, Thomas, JJ.; opinion by Judge Thomas) holding that 28 U.S.C. § 1738 does not extend full faith and credit to Tribal judgments (127 F.3d at 808-09). Federal-court recognition of Tribal judgments rests on comity. The court adapts Hilton v. Guyot and Restatement (Third) of Foreign Relations Law § 482 into two mandatory grounds for refusing recognition (no personal and subject-matter jurisdiction; no due process) and four discretionary ones (fraud, a conflicting judgment, inconsistency with a contractual choice of forum, public policy) (id. at 810). It also rejects a reciprocity requirement (id. at 811-12) and holds that federal law governs recognition (id. at 813). The court adds that comity 'does not require that a tribe utilize judicial procedures identical to those used in the United States Courts' (id. at 811). (project analysis) This is the leading template a state or federal court will use when asked to recognize a Tribal court judgment that confirms an ADR or peacemaking outcome, so a model code should build in jurisdictional findings, notice, and an opportunity to be heard. *Free online; pull from Westlaw.*

- [@ali-2022-restatement-law-of-american-indians]. The ALI's Restatement of federal Indian law. The ALI Adviser says the Official Text 'was published in 2022' in six chapters: Federal-Tribal Relations, Tribal Authority, State-Tribal Relations, Tribal Economic Development, Indian Country Criminal Jurisdiction, and Natural Resources. Reporter: Matthew L.M. Fletcher; Associate Reporters: Wenona T. Singel and Kaighn Smith, Jr. The ALI Adviser index also shows § 34 (civil regulatory and adjudicatory authority over nonmembers) and § 35. (project analysis) The black-letter section on recognition of Tribal-court judgments in state and federal court was NOT identified here. Confirmed in a catalog; full text not read.*

Jones, then director of the Northern Plains Tribal Institute and a Tribal judge, surveys Tribal-state and Tribal-federal court relations a year after Wilson v. Marchington. The article covers Tribal-state judicial forums as an educational tool (at 466), the history of Tribal courts (at 468), full faith and credit or comity between Tribal and state courts (at 477), jurisdictional agreements under the Indian Child Welfare Act and for abstention, domestic violence and child support (at 484), and the Tribal court exhaustion rule (at 499). Confirmed in a catalog; full text not read.*

The (c)(2) judge-to-judge resolution of recognition problems is an informal intercourt dispute-resolution step. *Free online.*

- [@michigan-court-rule-2-615]. Michigan's rule (text current as of the official compilation 'Updated November 18, 2026'; adoption date not shown in the compilation). Tribal-court judgments and other judicial acts are recognized with the same effect as those of Michigan courts of record. Recognition applies only if the Tribe enacts a binding measure obligating its court to enforce Michigan judgments and sends it to the State Court Administrative Office. Qualifying Tribal acts are 'presumed to be valid,' and the objecting party must show lack of jurisdiction, fraud, lack of fair notice or hearing, public-policy repugnance, or non-finality. (project analysis) This reciprocity-by-filing design is directly usable in a Tribal code. The Tribe enacts a mirror provision and files it, so Tribal judgments, including judgments confirming arbitral awards or entering mediated agreements, become presumptively enforceable in state court. *Free online.*

- [@california-rules-of-court-10-60]. Rule 10.60, adopted effective October 25, 2013, makes California's Tribal Court–State Court Forum a Judicial Council advisory body. Its focus is proceedings where state and Tribal jurisdiction overlap. It is directed to make recommendations on recognition of orders across jurisdictional lines and to share 'local rules of court, protocols, standing orders, and other agreements.' The Judicial Council comment recognizes that 'Traditional tribal justice practices are essential to the maintenance of the culture and identity of tribes' and limits Council oversight to the forum, not any Tribe or Tribal court. The Forum's own page (https://courts.ca.gov/3065.htm) says it was created in May 2010 after a December 2009 meeting and describes it as 'the coming together of tribal court and state court leaders as equal partners to address areas of mutual concern.' (project analysis) This is the most formalized Tribal-state forum model, and it produced the Tribal Court Civil Money Judgment Act. *Free online.*

- [@california-tribal-court-civil-money-judgment-act-sb-406-2014]. The chaptered text of SB 406 (Stats. 2014, ch. 243, approved August 22, 2014). It added Code of Civil Procedure title 11, chapter 3 ('Tribal Court Civil Money Judgment Act'), which governs how superior courts recognize and enter 'tribal court money judgments of any federally recognized Indian tribe.' It also sets an objection window and mandatory and discretionary grounds for refusal. As chaptered, CCP § 1742 sunset the title on January 1, 2018. The Act was still in force in 2023, when AB 1139 amended § 1731 to cover Tribal tax judgments, so a later act must have changed the sunset (not identified). (project analysis) This is a statutory alternative to a court rule. A Tribal drafter should note the 'impartial tribunals or procedures' ground for refusal, which a Tribal ADR code's due-process provisions should be written to satisfy. *Free online; pull from Westlaw.*

- [@iowa-code-626d-5]. Iowa's statutory recognition rule for Tribal-court civil judgments (Iowa Code ch. 626D, 'Recognition and Enforcement of Tribal Court Civil Judgments'; 2007 Acts ch. 192). Unobjected Tribal judgments are enforced like Iowa judgments. Recognition must be refused for lack of jurisdiction or due process, and may be refused on equitable grounds, including extrinsic fraud, a conflicting judgment, a contractual forum choice, non-reciprocity, and public-policy repugnance. (project analysis) It is included as a short, statute-form model that sits between Michigan's court-rule reciprocity and Washington's broad recognition rule. *Free online.*

- [@nevada-supreme-court-state-and-tribal-courts-in-nevada]. A public-education page from the Nevada judiciary. It says Tribal courts operate under the sovereignty of '28 federally recognized tribes' and that protective orders issued by Tribal courts are recognized in state courts. It describes joint education programs (the National Judicial College's National Tribal Judicial Center and the Nevada Court Improvement Program) but no formal Tribal-state court forum. A second document, the Court Improvement Program's 'Tribal Jurisdictions & Resources: Nevada' (undated PDF: https://nvcourts.gov/__data/assets/pdf_file/0031/48496/Nevada_Tribal_Jurisdictions_and_Services_Resource_1.pdf), states 'There are 18 active Tribal Courts in Nevada' and describes the Inter-Tribal Council of Nevada (ITCN) as offering 'trial and appellate court services for member tribes.' (project analysis) Nevada appears to lack the formal Tribal-state court forum found in California, Michigan, New Mexico or Washington. That gap is relevant for the book's Nevada worked example. *Free online.*

- [@nevada-revised-statutes-233a-260]. Part of NRS chapter 233A ('Indian Affairs'). The 2023 Legislature created the Department of Native American Affairs (NRS 233A.1003) and placed the Nevada Indian Commission (created 1965) within it (NRS 233A.1005). NRS 233A.260 (added 2019, amended 2023) requires a policy promoting 'positive government-to-government relations,' Tribal liaisons in agencies that deal with Tribes regularly, and a defined path for resolving issues a Tribe raises about an agency 'policy, agreement or program.' NRS 233A.270 adds an annual state-Tribal summit with the Governor. NRS 233A.003 declares that nothing in the chapter 'is intended to infringe upon the sovereignty of Indian tribes.' (project analysis) This is a statutory, non-court state-Tribal issue-resolution channel, a governmental process that fits none of the listed ADR methods (see new_methods). *Free online. Methods: negotiation, government-to-government issue resolution.*

### Arbitration clauses, sovereign immunity and gaming compacts

- [@c-and-l-enterprises-v-citizen-band-potawatomi-2001]. C & L Enterprises, 532 U.S. 411 (decided Apr. 30, 2001), held that "by the clear import of the arbitration clause, the Tribe is amenable to a state-court suit to enforce an arbitral award" (532 U.S. at 414, 121 S. Ct. at 1592). The building was commercial and the land "off-reservation, nontrust property" (id.). The Court restated that "to relinquish its immunity, a tribe's waiver must be 'clear'" (id. at 418, 121 S. Ct. at 1594, citing Oklahoma Tax Comm'n v. Citizen Band Potawatomi, 498 U.S. 505, 509 (1991)). It found the waiver in the Tribe's own form contract: an arbitration clause, American Arbitration Association rules under which an award "may be entered in any federal or state court having jurisdiction thereof" (id. at 419), and a choice of Oklahoma law, which had adopted the Uniform Arbitration Act (id. at 419-20). (project analysis) This is the key intersection case for the project: a model Tribal ADR code and its commentary should tell Tribal contracting officers that agreeing to arbitration plus a court-enforcement clause can itself be a waiver, and should offer model clauses that state the scope of any waiver and the enforcing forum (e.g., Tribal court) expressly. *Free online; pull from Westlaw. Methods: arbitration.*

- [@usc-25-2710]. Section 2710(d)(3)(C) lists the subjects a Tribal-State class III gaming compact 'may include', among them '(v) remedies for breach of contract'. Many compacts use this to set out dispute-resolution clauses. Section 2710(d)(7) gives federal district courts jurisdiction over a Tribe's suit claiming that a State failed to negotiate in good faith. If the court orders a compact and the parties still do not agree, each side must 'submit to a mediator appointed by the court a proposed compact that represents their last best offer', and the mediator 'shall select from the two proposed compacts'. That last step is a final-offer selection process, not mediation in the facilitative sense. (project analysis) Seminole Tribe v. Florida, 517 U.S. 44 (1996), held that Congress could not authorize Tribes to sue States under the Indian Commerce Clause, so the (d)(7) remedy cannot be enforced against a State that refuses to consent. That is one reason compact dispute clauses carry so much weight. *Free online; pull from Westlaw. Methods: negotiation, final-offer selection.*

- [@seminole-tribe-v-florida-1996]. The syllabus states the holding that 'The Eleventh Amendment prevents Congress from authorizing suits by Indian tribes against States to enforce legislation enacted pursuant to the Indian Commerce Clause.' The case arose under the good-faith-negotiation remedy of IGRA, 25 U.S.C. § 2710(d)(7). (project analysis) Because the federal court-ordered process (which ends in a mediator choosing one side's last best offer) cannot be forced on a State that has not consented, consent-based dispute provisions written into Tribal-state compacts and other intergovernmental agreements matter more. *Free online; pull from Westlaw.*

- [@nevada-te-moak-tribe-gaming-compact-2014]. A Nevada Class III gaming compact approved by Interior on October 17, 2014 (approval letter bound with the compact on the BIA Office of Indian Gaming site). Article IX gives the Tribe jurisdiction over all patron disputes, with optional state technical help on gaming devices. Article X contains a limited mutual waiver of immunity for compact disputes in federal court (venue in Washoe County), limited to declaratory and injunctive relief with no money damages. Article XIII sets a notice, 90-day cure, and 30-day meet-and-confer sequence, and keeps open 'mediation or arbitration' by mutual written agreement. (project analysis) Compared with California compacts, Nevada's is light: negotiation and then federal court, with ADR only by later agreement. Article X's condition reads oddly ('provided that the parties have not first attempted to resolve the dispute'), and Trevor should check the signed original. *Free online. Methods: negotiation, mediation, arbitration.*

- [@california-ione-band-of-miwok-gaming-compact]. A recent California compact, posted by the California Gambling Control Commission as 'Ione_Compact_2020'. The execution date was not extracted, and the preamble mentions trust acquisition in March 2020. Section 13.0 sets out a tiered dispute-resolution clause 'In recognition of the government-to-government relationship': written notice, a written response within 15 days, a meet-and-confer within 30 days, then optional (never mandatory) arbitration before a JAMS arbitrator under JAMS Comprehensive Rules with the Optional Arbitration Appeal Procedure, then federal or state court. Section 13.3 keeps 'mediation' open by mutual agreement, and § 13.1(f) bars either side from raising the other's failure to exhaust its own administrative remedies. Section 10.0 requires a patron-dispute process: written complaint, Tribal Gaming Agency review, then Tribal court or a Tribal Claims Commission. (project analysis) This is the most detailed intergovernmental ADR clause found in this research and a ready template for a Tribe's intergovernmental-agreements chapter. *Free online. Methods: negotiation, arbitration, mediation.*

### Federal-tribal contracts and Indian probate

- [@usc-25-5329-c-model-agreement-disputes]. The statutory model self-determination contract in 25 U.S.C. § 5329(c), § 1(b)(12), defines "third-party mediation" and lets the Secretary and the Tribal contractor jointly submit disputes to third-party mediation, "to the adjudicatory body of the Contractor, including the tribal court of the Contractor," to the contractor's own mediation processes, or to ADRA processes; the Secretary is bound by those decisions except one that "significantly conflicts with the interests of Indians or the United States." (project analysis) This is the clearest federal statutory recognition that federal-Tribal contract disputes may be resolved in a Tribe's own court or mediation process; a model Tribal ADR code should provide a ready forum and procedure that a Tribe can name under clause (B)(ii)–(iii). *Free online; pull from Westlaw. Methods: mediation.*

- [@usc-25-5331]. ISDEAA § 110 (added by Pub. L. 100-472, § 206(a), Oct. 5, 1988) gives federal district courts original jurisdiction over claims against the Secretary under the Act and money-damages claims under self-determination contracts (concurrent with the Court of Federal Claims), bars the Secretary from revising a contract without the Tribal organization's consent, and applies the Contract Disputes Act ("Chapter 71 of title 41") to self-determination contracts (§ 5331(a)–(d)). (project analysis) Provides the litigation backdrop against which the model-agreement ADR options (25 U.S.C. § 5329(c)) and the 25 C.F.R. § 900.217 alternatives operate. *Free online; pull from Westlaw.*

- [@cfr-25-900-217]. In the joint Interior/HHS ISDEAA regulations (25 C.F.R. part 900, subpart N, post-award contract disputes), § 900.217 answers "No" to whether a Contract Disputes Act claim is the only option: the awarding official "should consider using informal discussions between the parties, assisted by individuals who have not substantially participated in the matter," and the parties may use ADR under the ADRA "or the options listed in section 108(1)(b)(12) of the Indian Self-Determination Act" (evidently the model-agreement disputes clause, 25 U.S.C. § 5329(c) § 1(b)(12)). Section 900.216 lists the CDA, CBCA rules, and EAJA as also applicable (61 Fed. Reg. 32501 (1996), as amended 75 Fed. Reg. 31701 (2010)). Text read through the eCFR versioner API (current as of 2026-11-19). (project analysis) Shows the regulatory pathway for Tribes to invoke ADR (including Tribal forums) in federal contract disputes; the model code should give Tribal negotiators a ready process to point to. *Free online; pull from Westlaw. Methods: negotiation, mediation.*

- [@cfr-43-30-150]. In Interior's Indian probate rules (43 C.F.R. part 30, subpart F, "Consolidation and Settlement Agreements"), § 30.150 allows a probate judge to approve a settlement agreement among interested parties resolving any issue if all parties are advised of material facts, understand the agreement's effect, and settlement is in their best interest; the judge then issues an order distributing the estate per the agreement. Section 30.152 permits waiver of trust-property valuation in such agreements. Text read through the eCFR versioner API (current as of 2026-11-19). (project analysis) Not a formal ADR program, but an example of Interior probate judges approving negotiated family settlements over trust land; a Tribal code's probate or family-mediation chapter could interact with it. No dedicated OHA ADR program was found in 43 C.F.R. part 4, subpart A. *Free online; pull from Westlaw. Methods: negotiation.*

### Peacemaking and tribal custom (context, not ADR)

- [@narf-ipi-home]. *Not free online.*

- [@porter-1997-strengthening-tribal-sovereignty-through-peacemaking]. Robert B. Porter (Seneca Nation; at the time Director of the Tribal Law and Government Center at the University of Kansas) argues that adversarial Anglo-American litigation erodes Indigenous societies and that peacemaking strengthens Tribal sovereignty. He illustrates the argument with the Seneca Nation's internal conflict of 1994-95. Part III.A contrasts peacemaking with litigation along six axes. In footnote 126 he notes the rise of 'alternative dispute resolution ("ADR") in the form of mediation, arbitration, and mini-trials', which in his view 'suggests the limitations of litigation even within American society'. (project analysis) Porter compares peacemaking with litigation and treats ADR as a reform within Anglo-American practice. That supports the project's choice to treat peacemaking as a separate track rather than a kind of ADR. *Free online; pull from HeinOnline. Methods: peacemaking.*

- [@yazzie-1994-life-comes-from-it]. An article by Robert Yazzie (then Chief Justice of the Navajo Nation) on Navajo justice concepts, published in the New Mexico Law Review's 1994 Tribal-law issue (Vol. 24, Iss. 2). The same issue includes Valencia-Weber's 'Tribal Courts: Custom and Innovative Law', Alex Tallchief Skibine on deference to Tribal courts' jurisdictional determinations, and Christine Zuni Cruz on the Southwest Intertribal Court of Appeals. (project analysis) Widely cited for the distinction between Navajo peacemaking and adversarial or Anglo processes. The article text was not opened here, so no characterization of its argument is recorded. Trevor should read it before the commentary relies on it. *Free online; pull from HeinOnline. Methods: peacemaking. Confirmed in a catalog; full text not read.*

- [@valencia-weber-1994-tribal-courts-custom-innovative-law]. Gloria Valencia-Weber's study of custom and innovation in Tribal courts, 24 N.M. L. Rev. 225 (1994), in the same Tribal-law issue as Yazzie. (project analysis) Relevant to how a Tribal ADR code can let neutrals draw on custom and tradition, and to how outside courts applying Wilson-style comity should treat Tribal procedures that differ from Anglo-American ones. The full text was not read here. *Free online; pull from HeinOnline. Methods: customary dispute resolution. Confirmed in a catalog; full text not read.*
