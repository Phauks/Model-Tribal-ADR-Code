# Key cases in federal Indian law: a digest

This digest summarizes 65 court decisions that shape how
[[native-nations|Native nations]] resolve disputes, how far their
[[tribal-court|Tribal courts]] and other forums reach, and whether other
courts respect and enforce the results. It has 54 decisions of the
Supreme Court of the United States and 11 decisions of federal courts of appeals
and state supreme and appellate courts, chosen for their bearing on
arbitration, comity and enforcement. The annotated timeline
(`content/essays/i-legal-history-timeline.md`) places each case among the
statutes of its era.

## How to read an entry

- **Citation line.** The official citation, the court, the author of the
  opinion and the vote, then the nations involved, named as each nation or
  the Bureau of Indian Affairs list writes its name today. Some nations use
  another name for themselves; that is noted where known, and each name is
  to be confirmed with the nation before publication.
- **Question and Holding.** A short summary in the project's words. Holdings
  use the Court's own legal categories, such as "non-Indian" and
  "nonmember," because the rule depends on them.
- **In the court's words.** Quotations checked word for word against the
  official U.S. Reports scans, the Supreme Court's own opinions or, for other
  courts, the court's own site or a public copy. A pinpoint page follows each
  quotation. Where only an unofficial copy was available, the entry says the
  page is not verified.
- **Later treatment.** Later decisions or statutes that changed or applied
  the case, each with its source. A line marked "Lead to check" was not
  verified and is on the Westlaw list.
- **Why it matters here.** The project's own analysis of what the case means
  for a model code. It is not a statement of the court and not legal advice.
- **Topics.** Tags used by the index below and, later, by the website's
  filters.

No case here has been checked in a citator yet. Each source record carries a
`citator` field to be filled after a KeyCite check; the list of checks is in
`research/pull-lists/2026-11-21-legal-history-westlaw.md`. Older opinions use
language about Native people that is offensive today; quotations are chosen
for their legal content and are reproduced as written (project choice).

## Cases most relevant to dispute resolution (project analysis)

- **Legitimacy of Tribal forums, including nonjudicial ones.** Santa Clara
  Pueblo v. Martinez, Williams v. Lee, Fisher v. District Court and United
  States v. Wheeler.
- **Consent and jurisdiction over people who are not citizens of the
  nation.** Montana v. United States, Strate v. A-1 Contractors, Nevada v.
  Hicks, Atkinson Trading Co. v. Shirley and Plains Commerce Bank. Written
  consent to a Tribal forum is the most reliable basis.
- **Arbitration and immunity.** C & L Enterprises, Kiowa, Bay Mills,
  Ninigret, Val-U, Sokaogon Gaming and Smith v. Hopland Band.
- **Recognition and enforcement.** Wilson v. Marchington, Bird v. Glacier
  Electric, AT&T Corp. v. Coeur d'Alene Tribe, John v. Baker and the two
  Teague decisions; and, for finality, Iowa Mutual.

## Treaty era (1492 to 1871)

### Johnson v. M'Intosh (1823)

*21 U.S. (8 Wheat.) 543 (1823)* [@johnson-v-mintosh]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Marshall, C.J.; unanimous (no separate opinions).
Nations: Peoria Tribe of Indians of Oklahoma (successor to the Illinois Confederacy and the Piankashaw, the sellers in the 1773 and 1775 deeds).

**Question.** Can a title to land bought directly from Native nations by private purchasers before the Revolution be recognized in United States courts over a later federal patent?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that, under the "discovery" doctrine, the discovering European sovereign (and then the United States) acquired the exclusive right to extinguish Native title by purchase or conquest, so the Illinois and Piankeshaw could convey only their right of occupancy and only to that sovereign. The private purchasers' title could not be sustained against the federal grantee.

**In the court's words.**

> “discovery gave title to the government by whose subjects, or by whose authority, it was made, against all other European governments, which title might be consummated by possession” [@johnson-v-mintosh, p. 573]

> “They were admitted to be the rightful occupants of the soil, with a legal as well as just claim to retain possession of it, and to use it according to their own discretion; but their rights to complete sovereignty, as independent nations, were necessarily diminished” [@johnson-v-mintosh, p. 574]

**Later treatment.**
- Still cited as the root of the "discovery" doctrine and of Native title as a right of occupancy; it has not been overruled. Its reasoning was relied on for the holding in Tee-Hit-Ton (1955) that unrecognized Native title is not compensable property. [@tee-hit-ton-indians-v-united-states]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Foundational to how U.S. law describes Native land title and the limits it places on nations' power to sell land; useful background for any commentary on property-related disputes and on why federal law, not state law, governs Native title.

*Topics:* land-and-treaty-rights, status-and-recognition.

## Removal (1830 to about 1850)

### Cherokee Nation v. Georgia (1831)

*30 U.S. (5 Pet.) 1 (1831)* [@cherokee-nation-v-georgia]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Marshall, C.J.; 4-2 (Johnson and Baldwin, JJ., concurring separately; Thompson, J., joined by Story, J., dissenting).
Nations: Cherokee Nation; Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians; United Keetoowah Band of Cherokee Indians in Oklahoma.

**Question.** Is the Cherokee Nation a "foreign State" that may bring an original action in the Supreme Court against Georgia?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that a Native nation within the United States is not a "foreign state" under Article III, so the Court lacked original jurisdiction over the Cherokee Nation's suit to stop Georgia from enforcing its laws in Cherokee territory. The opinion described Native nations as "domestic dependent nations" whose relation to the United States "resembles that of a ward to his guardian."

**In the court's words.**

> “They may, more correctly, perhaps, be denominated domestic dependent nations” [@cherokee-nation-v-georgia, p. 17]

> “Their relation to the United States resembles that of a ward to his guardian” [@cherokee-nation-v-georgia, p. 17]

> “an Indian tribe or nation within the United States is not a foreign state in the sense of the constitution, and cannot maintain an action in the courts of the United States” [@cherokee-nation-v-georgia, p. 20]

**Later treatment.**
- The "domestic dependent nations" language is quoted throughout modern decisions, e.g., Michigan v. Bay Mills Indian Community (2014). [@michigan-v-bay-mills-indian-community]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Source of the "domestic dependent nations" and trust ("ward") framing that still shapes federal-court access for Native nations and the trust relationship; relevant to commentary on which forums are open to nations as litigants.

*Topics:* status-and-recognition, jurisdiction, sovereign-immunity.

### Worcester v. Georgia (1832)

*31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 515 (1832)* [@worcester-v-georgia]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Marshall, C.J.; Marshall, C.J., for the Court; McLean, J., concurring; Baldwin, J., dissenting.
Nations: Cherokee Nation; Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians; United Keetoowah Band of Cherokee Indians in Oklahoma.

**Question.** May Georgia apply its criminal laws to a non-Indian missionary living in the Cherokee Nation under federal license?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that the Cherokee Nation is a distinct political community in which state law has no force, that relations with it are vested exclusively in the federal government under the Constitution, treaties and federal statutes, and that Georgia's statute and Worcester's conviction were void.

**In the court's words.**

> “The Indian nations had always been considered as distinct, independent political communities, retaining their original natural rights, as the undisputed possessors of the soil, from time immemorial” [@worcester-v-georgia, p. 559]

> “A weak state, in order to provide for its safety, may place itself under the protection of one more powerful, without stripping itself of the right of government, and ceasing to be a state” [@worcester-v-georgia, p. 561]

> “The Cherokee nation, then, is a distinct community occupying its own territory, with boundaries accurately described, in which the laws of Georgia can have no force” [@worcester-v-georgia, p. 561]

**Later treatment.**
- Williams v. Lee (1959) calls it one of Marshall's "most courageous and eloquent opinions" while noting that later cases modified its rule against any state jurisdiction in Indian country. [@williams-v-lee]
- Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta (2022) states that the Worcester view that Indian country was separate from the State was later abandoned; the dissent disputes this reading. [@oklahoma-v-castro-huerta]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** The core statement of inherent Native sovereignty and of the federal-Tribal relationship excluding state law; the starting point for any model code provision asserting a nation's territorial jurisdiction and its own dispute-resolution forums.

*Topics:* jurisdiction, self-governance, status-and-recognition, land-and-treaty-rights.

## Reservations and allotment (1871 to 1934)

### Ex parte Crow Dog (1883)

*109 U.S. 556 (1883)* [@ex-parte-crow-dog]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Matthews, J.; unanimous.
Nations: Rosebud Sioux Tribe (Sicangu Lakota Oyate); Crow Dog and Spotted Tail were Brulé (Sicangu) Lakota.

**Question.** Did a federal territorial court have jurisdiction to try and sentence a Lakota man for killing another Lakota man within the Great Sioux Reservation?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that neither the general federal criminal statutes nor the 1868 treaty and 1877 agreement extended federal jurisdiction to a crime by one Indian against another in Indian country, which remained subject to the nation's own law and processes; the writ of habeas corpus issued.

**In the court's words.**

> “that of self-government, the regulation by themselves of their own domestic affairs” [@ex-parte-crow-dog, p. 568]

> “It tries them, not by their peers, nor by the customs of their people, nor the law of their land, but by superiors of a different race” [@ex-parte-crow-dog, p. 571]

**Later treatment.**
- Congress responded with section 9 of the Indian Appropriation Act of March 3, 1885 (the Major Crimes Act), which United States v. Kagama (1886) upheld and described as following the Crow Dog decision. [@united-states-v-kagama]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Recognizes a nation's own (in that case restorative, compensation-based) process for resolving even a homicide as law the federal courts must respect absent clear congressional action; a key historical anchor for commentary on traditional and restorative dispute resolution.

*Topics:* criminal-jurisdiction, self-governance, dispute-resolution, tribal-courts.

### Elk v. Wilkins (1884)

*112 U.S. 94 (1884)* [@elk-v-wilkins]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Gray, J.; 7-2 (Harlan, J., joined by Woods, J., dissenting).

**Question.** Was a Native man born in Tribal relations who later moved away and lived among non-Indians a U.S. citizen under the Fourteenth Amendment entitled to vote?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that Native people born members of a Tribe were not "subject to the jurisdiction" of the United States within the Citizenship Clause, and that leaving the Tribe did not make Elk a citizen without naturalization or other action by Congress.

**In the court's words.**

> “Indians born within the territorial limits of the United States, members of, and owing immediate allegiance to, one of the Indian tribes (an alien, though dependent, power), although in a geographical sense born in the United States, are no more "born in the United States and subject to the jurisdiction thereof," within the meaning of the first section of the Fourteenth Amendment, than the children 'of subjects of any foreign government born within the domain of that government” [@elk-v-wilkins, p. 102]

**Later treatment.**
- Congress later conferred citizenship by statute in the Indian Citizenship Act of 1924 [@indian-citizenship-act-1924]; the current codification (8 U.S.C. § 1401(b)) is not verified here.

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Background for the dual political status of citizens of Native nations, which affects who may be parties before Tribal forums and how membership-based jurisdiction is framed.

*Topics:* status-and-recognition.

### United States v. Kagama (1886)

*118 U.S. 375 (1886)* [@united-states-v-kagama]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Miller, J.; unanimous.
Nations: Hoopa Valley Tribe (crime alleged on the Hoopa Valley Reservation).

**Question.** Does Congress have power to make crimes by one Indian against another within a reservation federal offenses (the Major Crimes Act)?

**Holding.** Yes. The Court rejected the Commerce Clause as the source of power but upheld the Major Crimes Act as an exercise of federal power arising from the Native nations' dependence on, and the duty of protection owed by, the federal government; state courts were not the proper forum.

**In the court's words.**

> “These Indian tribes are the wards of the nation. They are communities dependent on the United States” [@united-states-v-kagama, p. 383-384]

> “Because of the local ill feeling, the people of the States where they are found are often their deadliest enemies. From their very weakness and helplessness, so largely due to the course of dealing of the Federal Government with them and the treaties in which it has been promised, there arises the duty of protection, and with it the power” [@united-states-v-kagama, p. 384]

**Later treatment.**
- Haaland v. Brackeen (2023) reviews the sources of congressional power over Indian affairs and does not rely on the free-standing "protection" rationale in the form Kagama stated it. [@haaland-v-brackeen]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** The doctrinal root of federal "plenary power" and of federal criminal jurisdiction in Indian country, which limits how far a nation's own criminal or restorative forums can reach for major crimes.

*Topics:* criminal-jurisdiction, status-and-recognition.

### Talton v. Mayes (1896)

*163 U.S. 376 (1896)* [@talton-v-mayes]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by White, J.; 8-1 (Harlan, J., dissenting).
Nations: Cherokee Nation.

**Question.** Does the Fifth Amendment's grand jury requirement apply to a murder prosecution under Cherokee Nation law in Cherokee courts?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that the Cherokee Nation's powers of local self-government existed before the Constitution and are not limited by the Fifth Amendment, so a Cherokee grand jury of five persons did not violate the Constitution.

**In the court's words.**

> “as the powers of local self government enjoyed by the Cherokee nation existed prior to the Constitution, they are not operated upon by the Fifth Amendment” [@talton-v-mayes, p. 384]

**Later treatment.**
- Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez (1978) describes Talton as the source of the rule that the Bill of Rights does not constrain Tribes and notes that Congress responded with the Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968. [@santa-clara-pueblo-v-martinez]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Establishes that Tribal courts and processes are not bound by the federal Bill of Rights as such, which is why the Indian Civil Rights Act and each nation's own law, not the Constitution, set procedural protections in Tribal forums.

*Topics:* tribal-courts, self-governance, criminal-jurisdiction.

### Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock (1903)

*187 U.S. 553 (1903)* [@lone-wolf-v-hitchcock]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by White, J.; unanimous in judgment (Harlan, J., concurring in the result).
Nations: Kiowa Tribe; Comanche Nation, Oklahoma; Apache Tribe of Oklahoma.

**Question.** May Congress allot and open reservation land in breach of the 1867 Medicine Lodge Treaty's requirement that three-fourths of adult men consent to any cession?

**Holding.** Yes. The Court held that Congress has plenary authority over Tribal relations, including power to abrogate treaty provisions, that the exercise of that power is political and not reviewable by the courts, and that the 1900 allotment act was presumed to be a good-faith exercise of guardianship.

**In the court's words.**

> “Plenary authority over the tribal relations of the Indians has been exercised by Congress from the beginning, and the power has always been deemed a political one, not subject to be controlled by the judicial department of the government” [@lone-wolf-v-hitchcock, p. 565]

> “When, therefore, treaties were entered into between the United States and a tribe of Indians it was never doubted that the power to abrogate existed in Congress” [@lone-wolf-v-hitchcock, p. 566]

**Later treatment.**
- Lead to check, not verified in this research: Its view that congressional Indian-affairs judgments are unreviewable political questions was later limited (e.g., United States v. Sioux Nation, 448 U.S. 371 (1980), and Delaware Tribal Business Committee v. Weeks, 430 U.S. 73 (1977)); those opinions were not opened.
- Haaland v. Brackeen (2023) still cites Lone Wolf for the breadth of congressional power, and separate opinions in that case criticize it. [@haaland-v-brackeen]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** The high-water mark of "plenary power"; commentary on treaty-based dispute provisions and on the stability of agreements with the United States should account for Congress's asserted power to abrogate treaties.

*Topics:* land-and-treaty-rights, status-and-recognition.

### United States v. Winans (1905)

*198 U.S. 371 (1905)* [@united-states-v-winans]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by McKenna, J.; 8-1 (White, J., dissenting).
Nations: Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakama Nation.

**Question.** Does the Yakama Nation's 1855 treaty right to take fish at "usual and accustomed places" survive private ownership of riverbank land?

**Holding.** Yes. The Court held that the treaty reserved the fishing right as a servitude that burdens later private titles, so private owners could not exclude Yakama fishers from the usual and accustomed places; treaties are construed as Native signatories understood them.

**In the court's words.**

> “the treaty was not a grant of rights to the Indians, but a grant of rights from them” [@united-states-v-winans, p. 381]

> “a reservation of those not granted” [@united-states-v-winans, p. 381]

**Later treatment.**
- Lead to check, not verified in this research: The reserved-rights principle is applied in later treaty cases; Herrera v. Wyoming (2019) is a recent example (not opened).

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Source of the reserved-rights doctrine: rights not expressly ceded are retained by the nation. Commentary on inherent authority to create dispute-resolution forums can use it as an interpretive anchor.

*Topics:* land-and-treaty-rights.

### Winters v. United States (1908)

*207 U.S. 564 (1908)* [@winters-v-united-states]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by McKenna, J.; 8-1 (Brewer, J., dissenting).
Nations: Fort Belknap Indian Community (Gros Ventre/Aaniiih and Assiniboine/Nakoda).

**Question.** Did the agreement creating the Fort Belknap Reservation impliedly reserve water from the Milk River for the reservation, as against later upstream settlers?

**Holding.** Yes. The Court held that the agreement impliedly reserved the water needed to make the reservation lands valuable, that the federal government had power to reserve the water and exempt it from state appropriation, and that the reservation's rights prevailed over later appropriators.

**In the court's words.**

> “command of all their beneficial use” [@winters-v-united-states, p. 576]

> “The power of the Government to reserve the waters and exempt them from appropriation under the state laws is not denied, and could not be” [@winters-v-united-states, p. 577]

**Later treatment.**
- Arizona v. Navajo Nation (2023) reaffirms that reservations carry implied water rights under Winters but holds that the 1868 Navajo treaty imposed no affirmative federal duty to secure water. [@arizona-v-navajo-nation]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Basis of reserved water rights; water disputes are a likely subject for intergovernmental ADR (water settlements, adjudications), so the code's commentary on intergovernmental agreements should cite it.

*Topics:* land-and-treaty-rights.

### United States v. Sandoval (1913)

*231 U.S. 28 (1913)* [@united-states-v-sandoval]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Van Devanter, J.; unanimous.
Nations: Pueblo of Santa Clara; Ohkay Owingeh (formerly San Juan Pueblo).

**Question.** May Congress prohibit bringing liquor into Pueblo lands in New Mexico, even though the Pueblos held fee title?

**Holding.** Yes. The Court held that the Pueblos are dependent Indian communities under federal guardianship and that Congress could bring them and their lands within federal Indian liquor laws, but that Congress may not arbitrarily label a group an Indian Tribe.

**In the court's words.**

> “the power and the duty of exercising a fostering care and protection over all dependent Indian communities within its borders” [@united-states-v-sandoval, p. 46]

> “it is not meant by this that Congress may bring a community or body of people within the range of this power by arbitrarily calling them an Indian tribe” [@united-states-v-sandoval, p. 46]

**Later treatment.**
- The "dependent Indian communities" concept was codified in 18 U.S.C. § 1151(b) and construed in Alaska v. Native Village of Venetie Tribal Government (1998). [@alaska-v-native-village-of-venetie-tribal-government]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Defines the federal relationship with the Pueblos and the "dependent Indian community" category of Indian country, which matters for the territorial reach of a nation's forums. The opinion contains openly racist descriptions of Pueblo people; any quotation in the book should be chosen and framed with care.

*Topics:* status-and-recognition, jurisdiction.

## Termination (1946 to 1968)

### Tee-Hit-Ton Indians v. United States (1955)

*348 U.S. 272 (1955)* [@tee-hit-ton-indians-v-united-states]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Reed, J.; 6-3 (Douglas, J., joined by Warren, C.J., and Frankfurter, J., dissenting).
Nations: Tee-Hit-Ton clan of the Tlingit (Stikine Tlingit, Wrangell area); the Wrangell Cooperative Association is the federally recognized tribe there.

**Question.** Is the taking of timber from aboriginal Tlingit land that Congress never recognized a compensable taking under the Fifth Amendment?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that original Native title not recognized by Congress is a right of occupancy that the United States may extinguish without compensation under the Fifth Amendment.

**In the court's words.**

> “Indian occupation of land without government recognition of ownership creates no rights against taking or extinction by the United States protected by the Fifth Amendment or any other principle of law” [@tee-hit-ton-indians-v-united-states, p. 285]

**Later treatment.**
- Aboriginal claims in Alaska were later settled by Congress in the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act of 1971 [@alaska-native-claims-settlement-act-1971]; Venetie (1998) describes that Act [@alaska-v-native-village-of-venetie-tribal-government].

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Explains why Alaska Native land claims were settled by statute rather than litigation, which shapes the distinctive Alaska setting (few reservations, the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act corporations) in which Alaska Native Tribal forums operate.

*Topics:* land-and-treaty-rights, status-and-recognition.

### Williams v. Lee (1959)

*358 U.S. 217 (1959)* [@williams-v-lee]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Black, J.; unanimous.
Nations: Navajo Nation.

**Question.** May an Arizona state court hear a non-Indian trader's suit to collect a debt from Navajo citizens for goods sold on the Navajo Reservation?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that state-court jurisdiction would infringe on the right of reservation Indians to make their own laws and be ruled by them and would undermine the authority of the Navajo courts, so the claim belonged in the Navajo courts.

**In the court's words.**

> “Essentially, absent governing Acts of Congress, the question has always been whether the state action infringed on the right of reservation Indians to make their own laws and be ruled by them” [@williams-v-lee, p. 220]

> “There can be no doubt that to allow the exercise of state jurisdiction here would undermine the authority of the tribal courts over Reservation affairs and hence would infringe on the right of the Indians to govern themselves” [@williams-v-lee, p. 223]

**Later treatment.**
- The "infringement" test is applied in Fisher v. District Court (1976) and in White Mountain Apache Tribe v. Bracker (1980), which treats it as one of two independent barriers to state authority. [@white-mountain-apache-tribe-v-bracker]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** The leading case protecting Tribal forums from state-court encroachment over on-reservation claims against citizens of the nation; central to any code provision on exclusive or primary Tribal jurisdiction over contract and debt disputes.

*Topics:* tribal-courts, civil-jurisdiction, jurisdiction, self-governance.

### Native American Church v. Navajo Tribal Council (1959)

*272 F.2d 131 (10th Cir. 1959)* [@native-american-church-v-navajo-tribal-council]. United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit; opinion by Huxman, J. (panel: Murrah, C.J., Phillips and Huxman, JJ.); 3-0.
Nations: Navajo Nation.

**Question.** Do federal courts have jurisdiction to hear a First Amendment challenge to a Navajo Tribal Council ordinance prohibiting peyote on the reservation?

**Holding.** No. The court held that the First Amendment does not bind Native nations because no constitutional provision or act of Congress makes it applicable to them, so the federal courts lacked jurisdiction over the challenge to the Navajo ordinance; the dismissal was affirmed.

**In the court's words.**

> “Indian tribes are not states. They have a status higher than that of states.” (272 F.2d 131, ¶ 11 of the law.resource.org text (F.2d pinpoint not verified; likely 134)) [@native-american-church-v-navajo-tribal-council]

> “No provision in the Constitution makes the First Amendment applicable to Indian nations nor is there any law of Congress doing so.” (272 F.2d 131, ¶ 11 of the law.resource.org text (F.2d pinpoint not verified)) [@native-american-church-v-navajo-tribal-council]

**Later treatment.** Congress later imposed free-exercise and other protections on Tribal governments by statute in the Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968, 25 U.S.C. § 1302 [@indian-civil-rights-act-1968]; Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez (1978) describes that Act as responding to the Talton line of cases [@santa-clara-pueblo-v-martinez].

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Confirms that federal constitutional limits do not apply directly to Tribal governments, so procedural guarantees in Tribal forums come from the Indian Civil Rights Act and each nation's own law; a model code should state its own due-process guarantees.

*Topics:* tribal-courts, self-governance, jurisdiction.

## Self-determination (1968 to 1987)

### Menominee Tribe of Indians v. United States (1968)

*391 U.S. 404 (1968)* [@menominee-tribe-v-united-states]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Douglas, J.; 6-2 (Stewart, J., joined by Black, J., dissenting; Marshall, J., not participating).
Nations: Menominee Indian Tribe of Wisconsin.

**Question.** Did the Menominee Termination Act of 1954 silently abrogate the Menominee's treaty hunting and fishing rights?

**Holding.** No. Reading the Termination Act together with Public Law 280, the Court held that the treaty hunting and fishing rights survived termination because Congress did not clearly abrogate them, and it declined to infer abrogation from ambiguous language.

**In the court's words.**

> “We decline to construe the Termination Act as a backhanded way of abrogating the hunting and fishing rights of these Indians” [@menominee-tribe-v-united-states, p. 412]

**Later treatment.**
- Congress restored federal recognition to the Menominee Indian Tribe of Wisconsin in the Menominee Restoration Act of 1973. [@menominee-restoration-act-1973]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** A clear-statement rule protecting treaty rights from implied abrogation; supports interpretive provisions in the model code requiring clear language before a nation's rights or immunities are treated as surrendered.

*Topics:* land-and-treaty-rights, status-and-recognition.

### McClanahan v. State Tax Commission of Arizona (1973)

*411 U.S. 164 (1973)* [@mcclanahan-v-arizona-state-tax-commission]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Marshall, J.; 9-0.
Nations: Navajo Nation.

**Question.** May Arizona impose its personal income tax on a Navajo citizen whose income came wholly from reservation sources?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that the state tax was preempted by the Navajo treaty and federal statutes, read against the backdrop of Navajo sovereignty, so Arizona had no authority to tax a reservation Navajo's on-reservation income.

**In the court's words.**

> “The Indian sovereignty doctrine is relevant, then, not because it provides a definitive resolution of the issues in this suit, but because it provides a backdrop against which the applicable treaties and federal statutes must be read” [@mcclanahan-v-arizona-state-tax-commission, p. 172]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** States the modern "backdrop" approach to sovereignty and preemption, which commentary can use to explain how Tribal, state and federal authority are sorted in disputes involving reservation activity.

*Topics:* jurisdiction, self-governance.

### Morton v. Mancari (1974)

*417 U.S. 535 (1974)* [@morton-v-mancari]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Blackmun, J.; 9-0.

**Question.** Does the Bureau of Indian Affairs' statutory employment preference for Indians violate the Equal Employment Opportunity Act of 1972 or due process?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that the 1972 Act did not implicitly repeal the preference and that the preference is political rather than racial, being granted to members of federally recognized Tribes; it survives because it is rationally tied to Congress's unique obligation toward Native nations.

**In the court's words.**

> “The preference, as applied, is granted to Indians not as a discrete racial group, but, rather, as members of quasi-sovereign tribal entities” [@morton-v-mancari, p. 554]

> “As long as the special treatment can be tied rationally to the fulfillment of Congress' unique obligation toward the Indians, such legislative judgments will not be disturbed” [@morton-v-mancari, p. 555]

**Later treatment.**
- Rice v. Cayetano (2000) distinguished Mancari in holding Hawaiʻi's ancestry-based voting limit unconstitutional. [@rice-v-cayetano]
- Haaland v. Brackeen (2023) did not reach the equal-protection challenge to the Indian Child Welfare Act, finding no standing. [@haaland-v-brackeen]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** The political-classification principle supports membership-based eligibility rules (for example, for panels of elders, peacemakers or Tribal-court bar admission) in a model code.

*Topics:* status-and-recognition, self-governance.

### Fisher v. District Court (1976)

*424 U.S. 382 (1976)* [@fisher-v-district-court]. Supreme Court of the United States; per curiam (no dissent noted).
Nations: Northern Cheyenne Tribe.

**Question.** Does a Montana state court have jurisdiction over an adoption proceeding in which all parties are Northern Cheyenne citizens residing on the reservation?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that the Northern Cheyenne Tribal Court's jurisdiction, established by Tribal ordinance under the Indian Reorganization Act, was exclusive, that state jurisdiction would interfere with Tribal self-government, and that denying the Indian plaintiffs a state forum was not impermissible racial discrimination.

**In the court's words.**

> “State-court jurisdiction plainly would interfere with the powers of self-government conferred upon the Northern Cheyenne Tribe and exercised through the Tribal Court” [@fisher-v-district-court, p. 387]

> “The exclusive jurisdiction of the Tribal Court does not derive from the race of the plaintiff but rather from the quasi-sovereign status of the Northern Cheyenne Tribe under federal law” [@fisher-v-district-court, p. 390]

**Later treatment.**
- Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians v. Holyfield (1989) treats exclusive Tribal jurisdiction over Indian child custody matters as codified by the Indian Child Welfare Act. [@mississippi-band-of-choctaw-indians-v-holyfield]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Shows that a nation's own ordinance allocating jurisdiction to its forum can displace state courts; directly relevant to drafting jurisdiction clauses for family and community dispute-resolution forums.

*Topics:* tribal-courts, civil-jurisdiction, child-welfare, self-governance.

### Bryan v. Itasca County (1976)

*426 U.S. 373 (1976)* [@bryan-v-itasca-county]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Brennan, J.; 9-0.
Nations: Minnesota Chippewa Tribe (Leech Lake Band of Ojibwe).

**Question.** Does Public Law 280 authorize a State to impose a personal property tax on a Native person's mobile home on trust land within a reservation?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that Public Law 280's civil provision gave States jurisdiction over private civil litigation involving reservation Indians in state court, not general civil regulatory or taxing power over reservations.

**In the court's words.**

> “nothing in its legislative history remotely suggests that Congress meant the Act's extension of civil jurisdiction to the States should result in the undermining or destruction of such tribal governments as did exist” [@bryan-v-itasca-county, p. 388]

> “are to be liberally construed, doubtful expressions being resolved in favor of the Indians” [@bryan-v-itasca-county, p. 392]

**Later treatment.**
- California v. Cabazon Band (1987) builds on Bryan's reading of Public Law 280 to draw the criminal/prohibitory versus civil/regulatory line. [@california-v-cabazon-band-of-mission-indians]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Essential for nations in Public Law 280 States (including Alaska and California): state courts may hear private civil suits, but Tribal governments and their forums are not displaced. A model code should explain concurrent jurisdiction in Public Law 280 settings.

*Topics:* civil-jurisdiction, jurisdiction, self-governance.

### Oliphant v. Suquamish Indian Tribe (1978)

*435 U.S. 191 (1978)* [@oliphant-v-suquamish-indian-tribe]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Rehnquist, J.; 6-2 (Marshall, J., joined by Burger, C.J., dissenting; Brennan, J., not participating).
Nations: Suquamish Tribe.

**Question.** Do Tribal courts have inherent criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that, absent authorization by Congress, Native nations lack inherent criminal jurisdiction to try and punish non-Indians, reasoning that such power is inconsistent with their dependent status.

**In the court's words.**

> “Indian tribes do not have inherent jurisdiction to try and to punish non-Indians” [@oliphant-v-suquamish-indian-tribe, p. 212]

> “Indian tribes are prohibited from exercising both those powers of autonomous states that are expressly terminated by Congress and those powers "inconsistent with their status"” [@oliphant-v-suquamish-indian-tribe, p. 208]

**Later treatment.**
- Congress partly restored jurisdiction over non-Indians: the Violence Against Women Reauthorization Act of 2013 amended the Indian Civil Rights Act to authorize "special domestic violence criminal jurisdiction" (25 U.S.C. § 1304), as described in United States v. Bryant (2016) [@united-states-v-bryant] and recorded in [@violence-against-women-reauthorization-act-2013-title-ix]. The 2022 reauthorization expanded the covered crimes [@violence-against-women-act-reauthorization-act-2022-title-viii].

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Defines the main limit on Tribal criminal forums; restorative or diversion programs involving non-Indians generally need consent or a statutory basis, which the model code should address.

*Topics:* criminal-jurisdiction, tribal-courts, jurisdiction.

### United States v. Wheeler (1978)

*435 U.S. 313 (1978)* [@united-states-v-wheeler]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Stewart, J.; 8-0 (Brennan, J., not participating).
Nations: Navajo Nation.

**Question.** Does the Double Jeopardy Clause bar a federal prosecution of a Navajo citizen after his conviction in Navajo Tribal court for an offense arising from the same incident?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that the Navajo Nation's power to punish its own members is an aspect of retained, inherent sovereignty, not delegated federal power, so Tribal and federal prosecutions are by separate sovereigns.

**In the court's words.**

> “The sovereignty that the Indian tribes retain is of a unique and limited character. It exists only at the sufferance of Congress and is subject to complete defeasance” [@united-states-v-wheeler, p. 323]

> “the power to punish offenses against tribal law committed by Tribe members, which was part of the Navajos' primeval sovereignty, has never been taken away from them” [@united-states-v-wheeler, p. 328]

**Later treatment.**
- United States v. Lara (2004) extends the separate-sovereign analysis to Tribal prosecutions of nonmember Indians after the 1990 "Duro fix"; Denezpi v. United States (2022) addresses Courts of Indian Offenses. [@united-states-v-lara]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Confirms that Tribal justice systems exercise inherent, not delegated, authority; this is the legal basis for a nation's own courts and peacemaking forums.

*Topics:* criminal-jurisdiction, tribal-courts, self-governance.

### Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez (1978)

*436 U.S. 49 (1978)* [@santa-clara-pueblo-v-martinez]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Marshall, J.; 7-1 (White, J., dissenting; Rehnquist, J., joining all but Part III; Blackmun, J., not participating).
Nations: Pueblo of Santa Clara.

**Question.** Does the Indian Civil Rights Act authorize a federal civil action against a Pueblo and its officer to challenge a membership ordinance as sex discrimination?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that the Pueblo's sovereign immunity bars suit against it under the Indian Civil Rights Act, and that the Indian Civil Rights Act creates no implied federal cause of action against Tribal officers beyond habeas corpus; the Indian Civil Rights Act rights are to be enforced in Tribal forums, including nonjudicial ones.

**In the court's words.**

> “As separate sovereigns pre-existing the Constitution, tribes have historically been regarded as unconstrained by those constitutional provisions framed specifically as limitations on federal or state authority” [@santa-clara-pueblo-v-martinez, p. 56]

> “Tribal courts have repeatedly been recognized as appropriate forums for the exclusive adjudication of disputes affecting important personal and property interests of both Indians and non-Indians” [@santa-clara-pueblo-v-martinez, p. 65]

> “Nonjudicial tribal institutions have also been recognized as competent law-applying bodies” [@santa-clara-pueblo-v-martinez, p. 66]

> “A tribe's right to define its own membership for tribal purposes has long been recognized as central to its existence as an independent political community” [@santa-clara-pueblo-v-martinez, p. 72 n.32]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** The most important Supreme Court authority for Tribal ADR: it recognizes Tribal courts and nonjudicial Tribal institutions as competent forums for enforcing civil rights and resolving disputes, and leaves the Indian Civil Rights Act enforcement (except habeas) to them. A model ADR code should cite it for the legitimacy of non-court forums.

*Topics:* tribal-courts, dispute-resolution, sovereign-immunity, self-governance, status-and-recognition.

### White Mountain Apache Tribe v. Bracker (1980)

*448 U.S. 136 (1980)* [@white-mountain-apache-tribe-v-bracker]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Marshall, J.; 6-3 (Stevens, J., joined by Stewart and Rehnquist, JJ., dissenting; Powell, J., concurring).
Nations: White Mountain Apache Tribe.

**Question.** May Arizona impose motor carrier and fuel taxes on a non-Indian logging company working exclusively for a Tribal enterprise on the reservation?

**Holding.** No. The Court held the state taxes preempted by comprehensive federal regulation of Tribal timber harvesting, applying a particularized inquiry into the federal, Tribal and state interests at stake.

**In the court's words.**

> “The two barriers are independent because either, standing alone, can be a sufficient basis for holding state law inapplicable to activity undertaken on the reservation or by tribal members” [@white-mountain-apache-tribe-v-bracker, p. 143]

> “particularized inquiry into the nature of the state, federal, and tribal interests at stake” [@white-mountain-apache-tribe-v-bracker, p. 145]

**Later treatment.**
- Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta (2022) applied Bracker balancing to state criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians in Indian country. [@oklahoma-v-castro-huerta]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Supplies the balancing test for state authority over non-Indians on reservations; relevant to choice-of-law and forum provisions in contracts between nations and non-Indian businesses.

*Topics:* jurisdiction, civil-jurisdiction.

### Montana v. United States (1981)

*450 U.S. 544 (1981)* [@montana-v-united-states]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Stewart, J.; 6-3 (Blackmun, J., joined by Brennan and Marshall, JJ., dissenting in part; Stevens, J., concurring).
Nations: Crow Tribe of Montana (Apsáalooke Nation).

**Question.** May the Crow Tribe regulate hunting and fishing by nonmembers on land within the reservation owned in fee by non-Indians?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that the bed of the Big Horn River passed to Montana at statehood and that, as a general rule, the inherent sovereign powers of a nation do not extend to nonmember activities on non-Indian fee land, subject to two exceptions: consensual relationships with the Tribe or its members, and conduct threatening or directly affecting the Tribe's political integrity, economic security, or health or welfare.

**In the court's words.**

> “exercise of tribal power beyond what is necessary to protect tribal self-government or to control internal relations is inconsistent with the dependent status of the tribes, and so cannot survive without express congressional delegation” [@montana-v-united-states, p. 564]

> “A tribe may regulate, through taxation, licensing, or other means, the activities of nonmembers who enter consensual relationships with the tribe or its members, through commercial dealing, contracts, leases, or other arrangements” [@montana-v-united-states, p. 565]

> “A tribe may also retain inherent power to exercise civil authority over the conduct of non-Indians on fee lands within its reservation when that conduct threatens or has some direct effect on the political integrity, the economic security, or the health or welfare of the tribe” [@montana-v-united-states, p. 566]

**Later treatment.**
- Narrowed and applied in Strate (1997), Atkinson (2001), Hicks (2001) and Plains Commerce Bank (2008); United States v. Cooley (2021) applied the second exception to uphold Tribal police authority. [@united-states-v-cooley]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** The governing test for Tribal civil jurisdiction over nonmembers. Its consensual-relationship exception is why express consent clauses (forum selection, arbitration, choice of Tribal law) in contracts with nonmembers are central to a Tribal ADR code.

*Topics:* civil-jurisdiction, jurisdiction, tribal-courts.

### Merrion v. Jicarilla Apache Tribe (1982)

*455 U.S. 130 (1982)* [@merrion-v-jicarilla-apache-tribe]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Marshall, J.; 6-3 (Stevens, J., joined by Burger, C.J., and Rehnquist, J., dissenting).
Nations: Jicarilla Apache Nation.

**Question.** May the Jicarilla Apache Nation impose a severance tax on oil and gas extracted by non-Indian lessees from Tribal lands?

**Holding.** Yes. The Court held that the power to tax is an inherent attribute of Tribal sovereignty, not merely a landlord's contract right, and that the Nation did not surrender it by entering leases that were silent on taxation.

**In the court's words.**

> “The power to tax is an essential attribute of Indian sovereignty because it is a necessary instrument of self-government and territorial management” [@merrion-v-jicarilla-apache-tribe, p. 137]

> “To presume that a sovereign forever waives the right to exercise one of its sovereign powers unless it expressly reserves the right to exercise that power in a commercial agreement turns the concept of sovereignty on its head” [@merrion-v-jicarilla-apache-tribe, p. 148]

**Later treatment.**
- Atkinson Trading Co. v. Shirley (2001) limited Merrion to taxation on Tribal land and applied Montana to nonmember activity on fee land. [@atkinson-trading-company-v-shirley]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Teaches that commercial agreements do not silently waive sovereign powers; a drafting point for any dispute clause that a nation signs (reserve powers expressly; waive only expressly).

*Topics:* self-governance, civil-jurisdiction.

### National Farmers Union Insurance Cos. v. Crow Tribe of Indians (1985)

*471 U.S. 845 (1985)* [@national-farmers-union-insurance-companies-v-crow-tribe]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Stevens, J.; 9-0.
Nations: Crow Tribe of Montana (Apsáalooke Nation).

**Question.** May a non-Indian party go directly to federal court to challenge a Tribal court's civil jurisdiction, and must it first exhaust Tribal court remedies?

**Holding.** The Court held that whether a Tribal court has exceeded its jurisdiction is a federal question under 28 U.S.C. § 1331, but that the question should ordinarily be decided first by the Tribal court itself, so Tribal remedies must be exhausted before a federal court considers it.

**In the court's words.**

> “Congress is committed to a policy of supporting tribal self-government and self-determination” [@national-farmers-union-insurance-companies-v-crow-tribe, p. 856]

> “Exhaustion of tribal court remedies, moreover, will encourage tribal courts to explain to the parties the precise basis for accepting jurisdiction, and will also provide other courts with the benefit of their expertise in such matters in the event of further judicial review” [@national-farmers-union-insurance-companies-v-crow-tribe, p. 857]

**Later treatment.**
- Strate v. A-1 Contractors (1997) and Nevada v. Hicks (2001) recognize exceptions where Tribal jurisdiction is plainly lacking. [@strate-v-a-1-contractors]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Creates the Tribal-exhaustion doctrine: Tribal forums get the first opportunity to decide their own jurisdiction. A model code should provide a clear, prompt procedure for jurisdictional rulings so exhaustion works in practice.

*Topics:* exhaustion, tribal-courts, civil-jurisdiction, jurisdiction.

### Iowa Mutual Insurance Co. v. LaPlante (1987)

*480 U.S. 9 (1987)* [@iowa-mutual-insurance-company-v-laplante]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Marshall, J.; 8-1 (Stevens, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part).
Nations: Blackfeet Nation (Blackfeet Tribe of the Blackfeet Indian Reservation of Montana).

**Question.** Does the Tribal-exhaustion requirement apply when federal jurisdiction rests on diversity of citizenship?

**Holding.** Yes. The Court held that federal policy supporting Tribal self-government requires exhaustion of Tribal remedies in diversity cases too, that civil jurisdiction over non-Indian activities on reservation lands presumptively lies in Tribal courts, and that, unless the Tribal court lacked jurisdiction, its resolution of issues may not be relitigated in federal court.

**In the court's words.**

> “Tribal authority over the activities of non-Indians on reservation lands is an important part of tribal sovereignty” [@iowa-mutual-insurance-company-v-laplante, p. 18]

> “Civil jurisdiction over such activities presumptively lies in the tribal courts unless affirmatively limited by a specific treaty provision or federal statute” [@iowa-mutual-insurance-company-v-laplante, p. 18]

> “Unless a federal court determines that the Tribal Court lacked jurisdiction, however, proper deference to the tribal court system precludes relitigation of issues raised by the LaPlantes' bad-faith claim and resolved in the Tribal Courts” [@iowa-mutual-insurance-company-v-laplante, p. 19]

**Later treatment.**
- The "presumptively lies" language was narrowed for nonmembers on non-Indian land in Strate v. A-1 Contractors (1997). [@strate-v-a-1-contractors]
- Relied on in AT&T Corp. v. Coeur d'Alene Tribe (9th Cir. 2002) for the rule that federal courts may not relitigate issues a Tribal court with jurisdiction has decided. [@att-corporation-v-coeur-dalene-tribe]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Supports finality of Tribal-forum decisions: once a Tribal forum with jurisdiction decides issues, federal courts defer. This is a foundation for code provisions on the preclusive effect of Tribal ADR outcomes.

*Topics:* exhaustion, tribal-courts, comity, enforcement, civil-jurisdiction.

### California v. Cabazon Band of Mission Indians (1987)

*480 U.S. 202 (1987)* [@california-v-cabazon-band-of-mission-indians]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by White, J.; 6-3 (Stevens, J., joined by O'Connor and Scalia, JJ., dissenting).
Nations: Cabazon Band of Cahuilla Indians; Morongo Band of Mission Indians.

**Question.** May California and Riverside County enforce their bingo and card-game laws on the Cabazon and Morongo reservations?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that California's gaming laws were civil/regulatory rather than criminal/prohibitory, so Public Law 280 did not authorize their enforcement, and that state regulation was preempted given the strong federal and Tribal interests in Tribal gaming as a source of revenue.

**In the court's words.**

> “if the intent of a state law is generally to prohibit certain conduct, it falls within Pub. L. 280's grant of criminal jurisdiction, but if the state law generally permits the conduct at issue, subject to regulation, it must be classified as civil/regulatory” [@california-v-cabazon-band-of-mission-indians, p. 209]

**Later treatment.**
- Congress adopted the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act (1988) in response to Cabazon, as described in Michigan v. Bay Mills Indian Community (2014). [@michigan-v-bay-mills-indian-community]
- Ysleta del Sur Pueblo v. Texas (2022) applied Cabazon's prohibitory/regulatory distinction to the Restoration Act. [@ysleta-del-sur-pueblo-v-texas]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** The origin of the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act and of Tribal-state compacting, whose dispute-resolution clauses are a major real-world example of intergovernmental ADR.

*Topics:* gaming, jurisdiction, civil-jurisdiction.

## Modern era (1988 to the present)

### Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians v. Holyfield (1989)

*490 U.S. 30 (1989)* [@mississippi-band-of-choctaw-indians-v-holyfield]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Brennan, J.; 6-3 (Stevens, J., joined by Rehnquist, C.J., and Kennedy, J., dissenting).
Nations: Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians.

**Question.** Under the Indian Child Welfare Act, were twin children born off the reservation to parents domiciled on it "domiciled" on the reservation, so that the Tribal court had exclusive jurisdiction over their adoption?

**Holding.** Yes. The Court held that "domicile" under the Indian Child Welfare Act is a matter of federal law, that the children took their mother's reservation domicile, and that the Tribe's exclusive jurisdiction under 25 U.S.C. § 1911(a) could not be defeated by the parents' deliberate off-reservation birth and consent to adoption.

**In the court's words.**

> “Tribal jurisdiction under § 1911(a) was not meant to be defeated by the actions of individual members of the tribe, for Congress was concerned not solely about the interests of Indian children and families, but also about the impact on the tribes themselves of the large numbers of Indian children adopted by non-Indians” [@mississippi-band-of-choctaw-indians-v-holyfield, p. 49]

**Later treatment.**
- Haaland v. Brackeen (2023) rejected constitutional challenges to the Indian Child Welfare Act on the merits or for lack of standing. [@haaland-v-brackeen]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Shows that a nation's jurisdiction over family matters protects collective as well as individual interests, which supports Tribal family-group conferencing and other family ADR provisions.

*Topics:* child-welfare, tribal-courts, jurisdiction.

### Duro v. Reina (1990)

*495 U.S. 676 (1990)* [@duro-v-reina]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Kennedy, J.; 7-2 (Brennan, J., joined by Marshall, J., dissenting).
Nations: Salt River Pima-Maricopa Indian Community; Torres Martinez Desert Cahuilla Indians (Duro's nation).

**Question.** May a Native nation exercise criminal jurisdiction over an Indian who is not one of its members?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that a Tribe's retained sovereignty does not include authority to impose criminal sanctions on a nonmember Indian.

**In the court's words.**

> “the retained sovereignty of the tribe as a political and social organization to govern its own affairs does not include the authority to impose criminal sanctions against a citizen outside its own membership” [@duro-v-reina, p. 679]

**Later treatment.**
- Superseded by statute: soon after Duro, Congress amended the Indian Civil Rights Act (25 U.S.C. § 1301(2)) to recognize inherent Tribal power to prosecute all Indians; United States v. Lara (2004) upheld that "Duro fix." [@united-states-v-lara]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Historical example of Congress restoring Tribal jurisdiction after an adverse decision; relevant to how a code defines who is subject to a nation's criminal or restorative forums.

*Topics:* criminal-jurisdiction, tribal-courts.

### Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Citizen Band Potawatomi Indian Tribe of Oklahoma (1991)

*498 U.S. 505 (1991)* [@oklahoma-tax-commission-v-citizen-band-potawatomi-indian-tribe]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Rehnquist, C.J.; 9-0 (Stevens, J., concurring).
Nations: Citizen Potawatomi Nation.

**Question.** Did the Nation waive its sovereign immunity from the State's counterclaim to collect cigarette taxes by suing to enjoin the tax assessment?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that Tribal sovereign immunity barred the State's counterclaim absent clear waiver or congressional abrogation, but that the State could tax sales to nonmembers and had other remedies, including agreements with the Tribe.

**In the court's words.**

> “Suits against Indian tribes are thus barred by sovereign immunity absent a clear waiver by the tribe or congressional abrogation” [@oklahoma-tax-commission-v-citizen-band-potawatomi-indian-tribe, p. 509]

> “There is no doubt that sovereign immunity bars the State from pursuing the most efficient remedy, but we are not persuaded that it lacks any adequate alternatives” [@oklahoma-tax-commission-v-citizen-band-potawatomi-indian-tribe, p. 514]

> “States may also enter into agreements with the tribes to adopt a mutually satisfactory regime for the collection of this sort of tax” [@oklahoma-tax-commission-v-citizen-band-potawatomi-indian-tribe, p. 514]

**Later treatment.**
- Reaffirmed in Kiowa (1998) and Bay Mills (2014). [@michigan-v-bay-mills-indian-community]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Sets the "clear waiver" standard that any ADR clause must meet, and expressly points to negotiated Tribal-state agreements as the alternative to litigation, which supports intergovernmental ADR provisions.

*Topics:* sovereign-immunity, dispute-resolution.

### Sokaogon Gaming Enterprise Corp. v. Tushie-Montgomery Associates, Inc. (1996)

*86 F.3d 656 (7th Cir. 1996)* [@sokaogon-gaming-enterprise-corporation-v-tushie-montgomery-associates]. United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit; opinion by Posner, C.J.; not verified.
Nations: Sokaogon Chippewa Community (Mole Lake Band of Lake Superior Chippewa).

**Question.** Did a Tribal gaming enterprise waive its sovereign immunity by agreeing to an arbitration clause in an architect's contract?

**Holding.** Reported holding, not yet checked against the official reporter: yes; the court held that the enterprise waived its immunity in the arbitration clause and reversed the partial summary judgment for the Tribe. The First Circuit in Ninigret cites it as finding waiver at 86 F.3d at 659-60.

**In the court's words.**

> “It waived it in the arbitration clause.” (86 F.3d at 661 (page from an unofficial online copy; not verified)) [@sokaogon-gaming-enterprise-corporation-v-tushie-montgomery-associates]

**Later treatment.** Cited in Ninigret Development Corp. (1st Cir. 2000) as finding waiver, 86 F.3d at 659-60. [@ninigret-development-corporation-v-narragansett-indian-wetuomuck-housing-authority]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** An early, influential holding (by Judge Posner) that an ordinary arbitration clause can waive Tribal immunity; useful background for C & L Enterprises.

### Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Florida (1996)

*517 U.S. 44 (1996)* [@seminole-tribe-v-florida-1996]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Rehnquist, C.J.; 5-4 (Stevens, J., dissenting; Souter, J., joined by Ginsburg and Breyer, JJ., dissenting).
Nations: Seminole Tribe of Florida.

**Question.** May Congress, under the Indian Commerce Clause, authorize a Tribe to sue a State in federal court to compel good-faith negotiation of a gaming compact under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that the Eleventh Amendment bars Congress from abrogating state sovereign immunity under its Article I powers, including the Indian Commerce Clause, overruled Pennsylvania v. Union Gas, and held that Ex parte Young could not be used to enforce the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act's remedial scheme against the Governor.

**In the court's words.**

> “Even when the Constitution vests in Congress complete lawmaking authority over a particular area, the Eleventh Amendment prevents congressional authorization of suits by private parties against unconsenting States” [@seminole-tribe-v-florida-1996, p. 72]

> “Article I cannot be used to circumvent the constitutional limitations placed upon federal jurisdiction” [@seminole-tribe-v-florida-1996, p. 73]

**Later treatment.**
- The opinion itself overrules Pennsylvania v. Union Gas Co. (1989). [@seminole-tribe-v-florida-1996]
- Lead to check, not verified in this research: after Seminole, the Interior Department adopted "Secretarial procedures" for compacts (25 C.F.R. part 291).

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Removes the federal-court remedy against a State that will not negotiate a gaming compact in good faith, which increases the importance of negotiated dispute-resolution and waiver clauses in Tribal-state compacts.

*Topics:* gaming, sovereign-immunity, dispute-resolution.

### Strate v. A-1 Contractors (1997)

*520 U.S. 438 (1997)* [@strate-v-a-1-contractors]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Ginsburg, J.; 9-0.
Nations: Mandan, Hidatsa and Arikara Nation (Three Affiliated Tribes of the Fort Berthold Reservation).

**Question.** Does a Tribal court have jurisdiction over a tort suit between nonmembers arising from a highway accident on a state right-of-way within the reservation?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that a Tribe's adjudicative jurisdiction over nonmembers does not exceed its legislative jurisdiction, that the state highway right-of-way is equivalent to non-Indian fee land for Montana purposes, and that neither Montana exception applied; exhaustion was not required because Tribal jurisdiction was plainly lacking.

**In the court's words.**

> “As to nonmembers, we hold, a tribe's adjudicative jurisdiction does not exceed its legislative jurisdiction” [@strate-v-a-1-contractors, p. 453]

> “Opening the Tribal Court for her optional use is not necessary to protect tribal self-government” [@strate-v-a-1-contractors, p. 459]

**Later treatment.**
- Applied in Nevada v. Hicks (2001) and Plains Commerce Bank (2008). [@nevada-v-hicks]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Limits Tribal adjudication of disputes between nonmembers absent consent; a Tribal ADR code should provide for express written consent to Tribal forums by nonmembers to fit the Montana consensual-relationship exception.

*Topics:* civil-jurisdiction, tribal-courts, exhaustion, jurisdiction.

### Wilson v. Marchington (1997)

*127 F.3d 805 (9th Cir. 1997)* [@wilson-v-marchington-1997]. United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit; opinion by Thomas, J. (panel: Wright, Reinhardt and Thomas, JJ.); 3-0 (no separate opinion shown in the text read).
Nations: Blackfeet Nation (Blackfeet Tribe of the Blackfeet Indian Reservation of Montana).

**Question.** Must a federal court give full faith and credit to a Blackfeet Tribal Court tort judgment, and if not, when should it recognize the judgment under comity?

**Holding.** The court held that comity, not full faith and credit, governs federal recognition of Tribal judgments; federal courts must refuse recognition if the Tribal court lacked personal or subject-matter jurisdiction or denied due process, and may decline on listed equitable grounds. Because, after Strate, the Tribal court lacked jurisdiction over the highway accident between a member and a nonmember, the judgment could not be recognized; the district court's enforcement judgment was reversed.

**In the court's words.**

> “We conclude that the principles of comity, not full faith and credit, govern whether a district court should recognize and enforce a tribal court judgment.” (127 F.3d 805 (opening paragraph; F.3d page not verified)) [@wilson-v-marchington-1997]

> “federal courts must neither recognize nor enforce tribal judgments if: (1) the tribal court did not have both personal and subject matter jurisdiction; or (2) the defendant was not afforded due process of law.” (127 F.3d at 810 (page as cited in AT&T Corp. v. Coeur d'Alene Tribe, citing "127 F.3d 805, 810," and in Bird v. Glacier Electric, citing "127 F.3d at 810")) [@wilson-v-marchington-1997]

> “the existence of both personal and subject matter jurisdiction is a necessary predicate for federal court recognition and enforcement of a tribal judgment” (127 F.3d at 811 (as quoted in AT&T Corp. v. Coeur d'Alene Tribe)) [@wilson-v-marchington-1997]

> “Extending comity to tribal judgments is not an invitation for the federal courts to exercise unnecessary judicial paternalism in derogation of tribal self-governance.” (127 F.3d 805 (F.3d page not verified; ¶ 34 of the law.resource.org text)) [@wilson-v-marchington-1997]

**Later treatment.** Applied in Bird v. Glacier Electric Cooperative (9th Cir. 2001) [@bird-v-glacier-electric-cooperative] and AT&T Corp. v. Coeur d'Alene Tribe (9th Cir. 2002) [@att-corporation-v-coeur-dalene-tribe].

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** The leading federal framework for recognizing Tribal judgments; the equitable grounds include inconsistency with the parties' contractual choice of forum, so Tribal ADR outcomes and forum clauses should be drafted with these factors in mind.

*Topics:* comity, enforcement, tribal-courts, civil-jurisdiction.

### Alaska v. Native Village of Venetie Tribal Government (1998)

*522 U.S. 520 (1998)* [@alaska-v-native-village-of-venetie-tribal-government]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Thomas, J.; 9-0.
Nations: Native Village of Venetie Tribal Government.

**Question.** Are lands conveyed to Alaska Native village corporations under the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act "Indian country" as a "dependent Indian community" under 18 U.S.C. § 1151(b)?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that a dependent Indian community requires federal set-aside and federal superintendence, and that the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act lands meet neither because the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act revoked reservations and conveyed land to state-chartered corporations; Venetie therefore could not tax a contractor on those lands as Indian country.

**In the court's words.**

> “Equally clearly, ANCSA ended federal superintendence over the Tribe's lands” [@alaska-v-native-village-of-venetie-tribal-government, p. 533]

> “Whether the concept of Indian country should be modified is a question entirely for Congress” [@alaska-v-native-village-of-venetie-tribal-government, p. 534]

**Later treatment.**
- The Alaska Supreme Court in John v. Baker (1999) held that Alaska Native Tribes retain non-territorial jurisdiction over members' domestic disputes despite Venetie. [@john-v-baker]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Central for Alaska Native nations: territorial (Indian country) jurisdiction is largely unavailable on the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act lands, so Alaska Tribal forums rely on membership-based jurisdiction and consent, which the model code must accommodate.

*Topics:* jurisdiction, status-and-recognition, civil-jurisdiction.

### Kiowa Tribe of Oklahoma v. Manufacturing Technologies, Inc. (1998)

*523 U.S. 751 (1998)* [@kiowa-tribe-v-manufacturing-technologies]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Kennedy, J.; 6-3 (Stevens, J., joined by Thomas and Ginsburg, JJ., dissenting).
Nations: Kiowa Tribe.

**Question.** Does Tribal sovereign immunity bar a state-court suit on a promissory note signed for off-reservation commercial activity?

**Holding.** Yes. The Court held that Tribes enjoy immunity from suits on contracts, whether governmental or commercial and whether made on or off a reservation, unless Congress has authorized the suit or the Tribe has waived immunity; it left any narrowing of the doctrine to Congress.

**In the court's words.**

> “As a matter of federal law, an Indian tribe is subject to suit only where Congress has authorized the suit or the tribe has waived its immunity” [@kiowa-tribe-v-manufacturing-technologies, p. 754]

> “Tribes enjoy immunity from suits on contracts, whether those contracts involve governmental or commercial activities and whether they were made on or off a reservation” [@kiowa-tribe-v-manufacturing-technologies, p. 760]

> “There are reasons to doubt the wisdom of perpetuating the doctrine” [@kiowa-tribe-v-manufacturing-technologies, p. 758]

**Later treatment.**
- Reaffirmed in Michigan v. Bay Mills Indian Community (2014). [@michigan-v-bay-mills-indian-community]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Because immunity covers commercial contracts, the only path to binding ADR with a nation is an express waiver; this makes waiver drafting a core subject of the model code.

*Topics:* sovereign-immunity, enforcement.

### Val-U Construction Co. v. Rosebud Sioux Tribe (1998)

*146 F.3d 573 (8th Cir. 1998)* [@val-u-construction-company-v-rosebud-sioux-tribe]. United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit; opinion by Waters, District Judge, sitting by designation (panel: Beam and Heaney, JJ., and Waters, D.J.); 3-0 (no separate opinion shown).
Nations: Rosebud Sioux Tribe (Sicangu Lakota Oyate).

**Question.** Is an arbitration award entered against a Tribe that refused to participate in the arbitration valid and preclusive, given the Tribe's sovereign-immunity objection?

**Holding.** Yes. Relying on its earlier ruling in the same dispute that the contract's arbitration clause waived the Tribe's immunity, the court held that the award was valid, that the Tribe's contract claims were barred by the award because it chose not to take part, and it affirmed summary judgment for the contractor (and the denial of prejudgment interest).

**In the court's words.**

> “We conclude that the Tribe's breach of contract claims are barred by the arbitration award. The Tribe was given a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issues at the arbitration hearing, but chose not to participate in the hearing, knowing full well that an award would be entered against it by the arbitrator.” (146 F.3d 573 (F.3d page not verified; ¶ 48 of the law.resource.org text)) [@val-u-construction-company-v-rosebud-sioux-tribe]

**Later treatment.** Cited by the First Circuit in Ninigret (2000) as finding waiver at 146 F.3d at 577-78. [@ninigret-development-corporation-v-narragansett-indian-wetuomuck-housing-authority]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** A nation that has agreed to arbitrate may be bound by an award even if it refuses to participate; a model code should advise on participation and on clear limits in waiver clauses.

*Topics:* dispute-resolution, sovereign-immunity, enforcement.

### John v. Baker (1999)

*982 P.2d 738 (Alaska 1999)* [@john-v-baker]. Supreme Court of Alaska; opinion by Fabe, J.; 3-2 (Matthews, C.J., joined by Compton, J., dissenting).
Nations: Northway Village.

**Question.** Does an Alaska Native village without Indian country retain inherent authority to adjudicate a custody dispute between its members, and must Alaska courts respect the resulting Tribal decision?

**Holding.** Yes. The court held that Alaska Native Tribes, as sovereign nations, retain inherent, membership-based (non-territorial) authority to resolve domestic disputes between their members, and that Alaska courts should, as a general rule, respect Tribal court decisions under the comity doctrine, subject to exceptions for lack of jurisdiction, denial of due process, and public policy. It reversed and remanded for a comity determination.

**In the court's words.**

> “We hold that Alaska Native tribes, by virtue of their inherent powers as sovereign nations, do possess that authority.” (982 P.2d 738 (P.2d page not verified)) [@john-v-baker]

> “We therefore hold that, as a general rule, our courts should respect tribal court decisions under the comity doctrine.” (982 P.2d 738 (P.2d page not verified)) [@john-v-baker]

**Later treatment.** Not checked. Leads (unverified): John v. Baker II (Alaska 2001) on the comity remand, and State v. Central Council of Tlingit & Haida Indian Tribes of Alaska (Alaska 2016) on Tribal child-support jurisdiction. (leads only; not verified in this research)

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** The leading authority that Alaska Native Tribal forums operate on membership-based jurisdiction even without Indian country, and that state courts extend comity to them; essential for any Alaska chapter of the model code.

*Topics:* comity, tribal-courts, jurisdiction, self-governance, dispute-resolution, child-welfare.

### Rice v. Cayetano (2000)

*528 U.S. 495 (2000)* [@rice-v-cayetano]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Kennedy, J.; 7-2 (Stevens, J., dissenting, joined by Ginsburg, J., as to Part II; Ginsburg, J., dissenting; Breyer, J., joined by Souter, J., concurring in the result).
Nations: Native Hawaiian people (Kānaka Maoli); no federally recognized Native Hawaiian governing entity exists.

**Question.** May Hawaiʻi limit voting for trustees of the Office of Hawaiian Affairs to "Hawaiians" defined by ancestry?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that the ancestry-based voter qualification was a racial classification that violated the Fifteenth Amendment, distinguishing Morton v. Mancari because the Office of Hawaiian Affairs election is a state election.

**In the court's words.**

> “Ancestry can be a proxy for race. It is that proxy here” [@rice-v-cayetano, p. 514]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** For Native Hawaiian institutions, this limits ancestry-based eligibility in state-run bodies; any Hawaiian dispute-resolution body (for example, hoʻoponopono-based programs) should consider whether it is state, private or community-based.

*Topics:* status-and-recognition, self-governance.

### Ninigret Development Corp. v. Narragansett Indian Wetuomuck Housing Authority (2000)

*207 F.3d 21 (1st Cir. 2000)* [@ninigret-development-corporation-v-narragansett-indian-wetuomuck-housing-authority]. United States Court of Appeals for the First Circuit; opinion by Selya, J. (panel: Torruella, C.J., Selya and Lipez, JJ.); 3-0 (no separate opinion shown).
Nations: Narragansett Indian Tribe.

**Question.** Did a Tribal housing authority waive immunity through a contract clause sending disputes to the Tribal Council and then to a Tribal Court-appointed arbitration board, and must the contractor exhaust Tribal remedies?

**Holding.** The court held that the contract's explicit, mandatory dispute-resolution clause (read with the Tribal "sue and be sued" ordinance) waived the housing authority's immunity, but that the Tribal exhaustion doctrine applied, so the Tribal forum must first interpret the forum-selection clause and its own jurisdiction; it vacated and remanded.

**In the court's words.**

> “we believe that explicit language broadly relegating dispute resolution to arbitration constitutes a waiver of tribal sovereign immunity, whereas language that is ambiguous rather than definite, cryptic rather than explicit, or precatory rather than mandatory, usually will not achieve that end.” (207 F.3d 21 (F.3d page not verified; ¶ 26 of the law.resource.org text)) [@ninigret-development-corporation-v-narragansett-indian-wetuomuck-housing-authority]

> “The tribal exhaustion doctrine is not jurisdictional in nature, but, rather, is a product of comity and related considerations.” (207 F.3d 21 (F.3d page not verified; ¶ 28)) [@ninigret-development-corporation-v-narragansett-indian-wetuomuck-housing-authority]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** A real tiered clause (Tribal Council, then a Tribal Court-appointed arbitration board, then federal court only by written agreement) and a clear test for when arbitration language waives immunity; an excellent model for drafting multi-step Tribal ADR clauses.

*Topics:* dispute-resolution, sovereign-immunity, exhaustion, tribal-courts, comity.

### Teague v. Bad River Band of the Lake Superior Tribe of Chippewa Indians (2000)

*2000 WI 79, 236 Wis. 2d 384, 612 N.W.2d 709 (2000)* [@teague-v-bad-river-band-2000]. Supreme Court of Wisconsin; opinion by Sykes, J.; No concurring or dissenting opinions listed on the court's caption page.
Nations: Bad River Band of the Lake Superior Tribe of Chippewa Indians.

**Question.** May a Wisconsin court deny full faith and credit to a Tribal court judgment under the state "prior action pending" rule because the same dispute was filed first in state court?

**Holding.** No. The court held that the prior-action-pending rule does not apply to a Tribal court, a court of an independent sovereign, but that comity required the state and Tribal courts, aware of each other's parallel proceedings, to confer to allocate jurisdiction; it reversed and remanded for such a judicial conference.

**In the court's words.**

> “We now hold that the prior action pending rule of Syver does not apply to these circumstances because an Indian tribal court is a court of an independent sovereign.” (2000 WI 79, ¶ 2) [@teague-v-bad-river-band-2000]

> “However, under the circumstances of this case, principles of comity required that the state and tribal courts confer for purposes of allocating jurisdiction between them, in order to avoid both the race to judgment and inconsistent results that occurred here.” (2000 WI 79, ¶ 2) [@teague-v-bad-river-band-2000]

**Later treatment.** Followed by Teague v. Bad River Band, 2003 WI 118 (the same dispute after remand). [@teague-v-bad-river-band-2003]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** A model of court-to-court communication (judicial conferencing) to resolve concurrent Tribal-state jurisdiction; relevant to code provisions on cooperation and transfer between Tribal and state forums.

*Topics:* comity, tribal-courts, enforcement, jurisdiction.

### C & L Enterprises, Inc. v. Citizen Band Potawatomi Indian Tribe of Oklahoma (2001)

*532 U.S. 411 (2001)* [@c-and-l-enterprises-v-citizen-band-potawatomi-2001]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Ginsburg, J.; 9-0.
Nations: Citizen Potawatomi Nation.

**Question.** Did the Nation waive its sovereign immunity from suit to enforce an arbitration award by agreeing to a standard-form construction contract with an arbitration clause and an Oklahoma choice-of-law clause?

**Holding.** Yes. The Court held that by agreeing to arbitrate all contract disputes, to have judgment entered on the award in any court having jurisdiction, and to have Oklahoma law govern (which makes Oklahoma courts competent to enforce arbitration agreements), the Nation clearly waived immunity from a state-court suit to enforce the award. It noted that the Nation itself proposed and prepared the contract.

**In the court's words.**

> “We hold that, by the clear import of the arbitration clause, the Tribe is amenable to a state-court suit to enforce an arbitral award in favor of contractor C & L” [@c-and-l-enterprises-v-citizen-band-potawatomi-2001, p. 414]

> “the contract specifically authorizes judicial enforcement of the resolution arrived at through arbitration” [@c-and-l-enterprises-v-citizen-band-potawatomi-2001, p. 422]

> “The Tribe proposed and prepared the contract; C & L foisted no form on a quiescent Tribe” [@c-and-l-enterprises-v-citizen-band-potawatomi-2001, p. 423]

**Later treatment.**
- Cited in Michigan v. Bay Mills Indian Community (2014) for the rule that a Tribe may waive immunity by contract. [@michigan-v-bay-mills-indian-community]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** The key Supreme Court case for Tribal ADR drafting: an arbitration clause plus enforcement and choice-of-law language can waive immunity for enforcement of awards. A model code should offer clauses that state exactly which forum may confirm or enforce an award, what relief is available, and what is not waived.

*Topics:* dispute-resolution, sovereign-immunity, enforcement.

### Atkinson Trading Co. v. Shirley (2001)

*532 U.S. 645 (2001)* [@atkinson-trading-company-v-shirley]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Rehnquist, C.J.; 9-0 (Souter, J., joined by Kennedy and Thomas, JJ., concurring).
Nations: Navajo Nation.

**Question.** May the Navajo Nation impose a hotel occupancy tax on nonmember guests of a hotel on non-Indian fee land within the reservation?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that Montana's general rule governs Tribal taxation of nonmember activity on non-Indian fee land, that the guests' receipt of Tribal services did not create a consensual relationship, and that the second Montana exception did not apply.

**In the court's words.**

> “An Indian tribe's sovereign power to tax-whatever its derivation-reaches no further than tribal land” [@atkinson-trading-company-v-shirley, p. 653]

> “a nonmember's actual or potential receipt of tribal police, fire, and medical services does not create the requisite connection” [@atkinson-trading-company-v-shirley, p. 655]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Shows that services alone do not create consent; ADR provisions binding nonmembers should rest on express agreements.

*Topics:* civil-jurisdiction, jurisdiction.

### Nevada v. Hicks (2001)

*533 U.S. 353 (2001)* [@nevada-v-hicks]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Scalia, J.; 9-0 in judgment (Souter, J., joined by Kennedy and Thomas, JJ., concurring; Ginsburg, J., concurring; O'Connor, J., joined by Stevens and Breyer, JJ., concurring in part and in the judgment; Stevens, J., joined by Breyer, J., concurring in the judgment).
Nations: Paiute-Shoshone Tribe of the Fallon Reservation and Colony.

**Question.** Does a Tribal court have jurisdiction over a member's civil-rights and tort claims against state game wardens for executing a search warrant on his trust land on the reservation?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that the Tribe lacked regulatory (and therefore adjudicative) authority over state officers enforcing state law for off-reservation crimes, that Tribal courts are not courts of general jurisdiction able to hear 42 U.S.C. § 1983 claims, and that exhaustion was not required.

**In the court's words.**

> “State sovereignty does not end at a reservation's border” [@nevada-v-hicks, p. 361]

> “Respondents' contention that tribal courts are courts of "general jurisdiction" is also quite wrong” [@nevada-v-hicks, p. 367]

**Later treatment.**
- Plains Commerce Bank (2008) relies on Hicks in limiting Tribal adjudicative authority over nonmembers. [@plains-commerce-bank-v-long-family-land-and-cattle-company]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Limits Tribal-forum jurisdiction over state officials and federal claims; intergovernmental (Tribal-state) disputes may need negotiated forums, such as cross-deputization or cooperative agreements with dispute clauses.

*Topics:* tribal-courts, civil-jurisdiction, exhaustion, jurisdiction.

### Bird v. Glacier Electric Cooperative, Inc. (2001)

*255 F.3d 1136 (9th Cir. 2001)* [@bird-v-glacier-electric-cooperative]. United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit; opinion by Gould, J. (panel: Reavley, O'Scannlain and Gould, JJ.); 3-0 (no separate opinion shown in the text read).
Nations: Blackfeet Nation (Blackfeet Tribe of the Blackfeet Indian Reservation of Montana).

**Question.** May a federal court recognize a Blackfeet Tribal Court jury verdict against a non-member-owned cooperative when the plaintiff's closing argument appealed to racial and ethnic prejudice?

**Holding.** No. Applying Wilson v. Marchington, the court held that the closing argument's appeals to racial prejudice offended fundamental fairness and denied due process, so the Tribal judgment was not entitled to comity; it reversed with instructions to enter judgment denying recognition.

**In the court's words.**

> “We hold that Glacier Construction's appeal to racial prejudice in closing argument in its civil case in tribal court offended fundamental fairness and violated due process owed the Co-op.” (255 F.3d 1136 (F.3d page not verified; ¶ 83 of the law.resource.org text)) [@bird-v-glacier-electric-cooperative]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Shows the due-process ground for refusing recognition in practice; a model code's rules of procedure and conduct for advocates and neutrals (including bias safeguards) protect the enforceability of Tribal outcomes.

*Topics:* comity, enforcement, tribal-courts.

### Smith v. Hopland Band of Pomo Indians (2002)

### AT&T Corp. v. Coeur d'Alene Tribe (2002)

*295 F.3d 899 (9th Cir. 2002)* [@att-corporation-v-coeur-dalene-tribe]. United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit; opinion by B. Fletcher, J. (panel: Browning, B. Fletcher and Gould, JJ.); 2-1 in part (Gould, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part).
Nations: Coeur d'Alene Tribe.

**Question.** Must a federal court enforce a Coeur d'Alene Tribal Court judgment ordering AT&T to provide toll-free service for the Tribe's national lottery?

**Holding.** The court reaffirmed that federal courts must recognize Tribal judgments under comity unless the Tribal court lacked jurisdiction or denied due process, and may not relitigate issues a Tribal court with jurisdiction decided. But it held the Tribal Court lacked jurisdiction because the Federal Communications Act claim had to be brought before the FCC or in federal district court; it vacated the district court's ruling that the lottery violated the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act because AT&T was not the proper party to raise that question.

**In the court's words.**

> “As a general rule, federal courts must recognize and enforce tribal court judgments under principles of comity.” (295 F.3d 899 (F.3d page not verified; ¶ 19 of the law.resource.org text)) [@att-corporation-v-coeur-dalene-tribe]

> “federal courts may not readjudicate questions — whether of federal, state or tribal law — already resolved in tribal court absent a finding that the tribal court lacked jurisdiction or that its judgment be denied comity for some other valid reason.” (295 F.3d 899 (F.3d page not verified; ¶ 21)) [@att-corporation-v-coeur-dalene-tribe]

**Later treatment.** The opinion amends an earlier opinion reported at 283 F.3d 1156 (filed March 19, 2002); cite the amended opinion [@att-corporation-v-coeur-dalene-tribe].

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Combines a strong statement of comity with a reminder that subject-matter limits (here, a federal statute's exclusive forum provisions) can defeat recognition; a model code should identify claims that Tribal forums cannot finally resolve.

*Topics:* comity, enforcement, tribal-courts, gaming, civil-jurisdiction.

### Teague v. Bad River Band of the Lake Superior Tribe of Chippewa Indians (2003)

*2003 WI 118, 265 Wis. 2d 64, 665 N.W.2d 899 (2003)* [@teague-v-bad-river-band-2003]. Supreme Court of Wisconsin; opinion by Crooks, J. (lead opinion); Abrahamson, C.J., concurring, joined by Bablitch, Bradley and Sykes, JJ.; 5-2 in the judgment (Wilcox, J., and Prosser, J., dissenting).
Nations: Bad River Band of the Lake Superior Tribe of Chippewa Indians.

**Question.** After the parallel state and Tribal cases both reached judgment, must Wisconsin give full faith and credit to the earlier Tribal-court judgment voiding the employment contracts?

**Holding.** The court reversed the order refusing to reopen the circuit court's judgment and remanded for dismissal of the state action. The lead opinion would give full faith and credit to the first-in-time Tribal judgment under Wis. Stat. § 806.245; the four-justice concurrence reached the same result through comity, stressing that courts exercising concurrent jurisdiction should stop, consult, and decide which court proceeds.

**In the court's words.**

> “I would hold that the circuit court was required by Wis. Stat. § 806.245 to give full faith and credit to the tribal court judgment declaring the employment contracts at issue void and, thus, unenforceable.” (2003 WI 118, ¶ 2 (lead opinion of Crooks, J.)) [@teague-v-bad-river-band-2003]

> “Rather, each court should stop its proceedings, consult with the other, and as a matter of comity decide which court should proceed.” (2003 WI 118, ¶ 58 (Abrahamson, C.J., concurring)) [@teague-v-bad-river-band-2003]

**Later treatment.** Not checked. Lead: Wisconsin later adopted a rule on discretionary transfer of cases to Tribal court (Wis. Stat. § 801.54); confirm whether it was a response to Teague. (leads only; not verified in this research)

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Illustrates the costs of uncoordinated parallel proceedings and the fractured approaches (full faith and credit vs. comity) state courts take; supports code provisions for early coordination with state courts and for recognition of Tribal judgments.

*Topics:* comity, enforcement, tribal-courts, jurisdiction.

### United States v. Lara (2004)

*541 U.S. 193 (2004)* [@united-states-v-lara]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Breyer, J.; 7-2 (Souter, J., joined by Scalia, J., dissenting; Stevens, J., concurring; Kennedy, J., and Thomas, J., concurring in the judgment).
Nations: Spirit Lake Tribe; Turtle Mountain Band of Chippewa Indians of North Dakota (Lara's nation).

**Question.** After a Tribal prosecution of a nonmember Indian under the 1990 the Indian Civil Rights Act amendment, does the Double Jeopardy Clause bar a federal prosecution for the same conduct?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that Congress has constitutional power to relax restrictions on the Tribes' inherent criminal jurisdiction over nonmember Indians, so the Spirit Lake prosecution was an exercise of inherent Tribal power and the later federal prosecution was by a separate sovereign.

**In the court's words.**

> “the Constitution authorizes Congress to permit tribes, as an exercise of their inherent tribal authority, to prosecute nonmember Indians” [@united-states-v-lara, p. 210]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Confirms Congress can restore (recognize) inherent jurisdiction; supports code commentary on the scope of Tribal criminal and restorative forums over nonmember Indians.

*Topics:* criminal-jurisdiction, tribal-courts, self-governance.

### Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co. (2008)

*554 U.S. 316 (2008)* [@plains-commerce-bank-v-long-family-land-and-cattle-company]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Roberts, C.J.; 5-4 (Ginsburg, J., joined by Stevens, Souter and Breyer, JJ., concurring in part, concurring in the judgment in part, and dissenting in part).
Nations: Cheyenne River Sioux Tribe.

**Question.** Does a Tribal court have jurisdiction over a discrimination claim by Tribal-member-owned company against a non-Indian bank concerning the bank's sale of fee land it owned on the reservation?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that Montana does not permit Tribal regulation of the sale of non-Indian fee land, so the Tribal court lacked jurisdiction over the discrimination claim; it also held the bank had standing to challenge Tribal-court jurisdiction.

**In the court's words.**

> “Montana and its progeny permit tribal regulation of nonmember conduct inside the reservation that implicates the tribe’s sovereign interests” [@plains-commerce-bank-v-long-family-land-and-cattle-company, p. 332]

> “The Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes” [@plains-commerce-bank-v-long-family-land-and-cattle-company, p. 337]

**Later treatment.**
- United States v. Bryant (2016) cites Plains Commerce Bank for the point that the Sixth Amendment does not apply in Tribal-court proceedings. [@united-states-v-bryant]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Even where a nonmember has consensual dealings with members, Tribal jurisdiction must relate to those dealings and to Tribal sovereign interests; ADR consent clauses should describe the disputes covered precisely.

*Topics:* civil-jurisdiction, tribal-courts, jurisdiction.

### Michigan v. Bay Mills Indian Community (2014)

*572 U.S. 782 (2014)* [@michigan-v-bay-mills-indian-community]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Kagan, J.; 5-4 (Sotomayor, J., concurring; Scalia, J., dissenting; Thomas, J., joined by Scalia, Ginsburg and Alito, JJ., dissenting; Ginsburg, J., dissenting).
Nations: Bay Mills Indian Community.

**Question.** Does Tribal sovereign immunity bar a State's suit to enjoin a Tribe's operation of a casino outside Indian lands?

**Holding.** Yes. The Court held that the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act abrogates immunity only for suits to enjoin class III gaming on Indian lands, that the suit concerned gaming off Indian lands, and, reaffirming Kiowa, that Tribal immunity extends to off-reservation commercial activity unless Congress decides otherwise; the State has other remedies, including bargaining for a waiver.

**In the court's words.**

> “Among the core aspects of sovereignty that tribes possess—subject, again, to congressional action—is the “common-law immunity from suit traditionally enjoyed by sovereign powers”” [@michigan-v-bay-mills-indian-community, p. 788]

> “the State need only bargain for a waiver of immunity” [@michigan-v-bay-mills-indian-community, p. 796]

> “it is fundamentally Congress’s job, not ours, to determine whether or how to limit tribal immunity” [@michigan-v-bay-mills-indian-community, p. 800]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Reaffirms broad Tribal immunity and expressly points to negotiated waivers in compacts and contracts as the route to remedies; this directly supports model waiver and dispute clauses for intergovernmental agreements.

*Topics:* sovereign-immunity, gaming, dispute-resolution, enforcement.

### United States v. Bryant (2016)

*579 U.S. 140 (2016)* [@united-states-v-bryant]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Ginsburg, J.; 9-0 (Thomas, J., concurring).
Nations: Northern Cheyenne Tribe.

**Question.** May uncounseled Tribal-court misdemeanor convictions that complied with the Indian Civil Rights Act serve as predicates for the federal habitual domestic-assault offense, 18 U.S.C. § 117(a)?

**Holding.** Yes. The Court held that because the Sixth Amendment does not apply in Tribal courts and Bryant's convictions complied with the Indian Civil Rights Act, they were valid when entered and their use as predicates does not violate the Constitution.

**In the court's words.**

> “Because Bryant's tribal-court convictions occurred in proceedings that complied with ICRA and were therefore valid when entered, use of those convictions as predicate offenses in a § 117(a) prosecution does not violate the Constitution” [@united-states-v-bryant, p. 157]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Federal law gives effect to Tribal-court judgments that comply with the Indian Civil Rights Act; supports code commentary that the Indian Civil Rights Act-compliant Tribal procedures produce judgments other systems will respect.

*Topics:* tribal-courts, criminal-jurisdiction, comity, enforcement.

### Dollar General Corp. v. Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians (2016)

*579 U.S. 545 (2016)* [@dollar-general-corporation-v-mississippi-band-of-choctaw-indians]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by per curiam; 4-4 (affirmed by an equally divided Court).
Nations: Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians.

**Question.** Does a Tribal court have jurisdiction over tort claims by a member against a nonmember business that entered a consensual relationship with the Tribe (a youth employment program) under Montana's first exception?

**Holding.** The Court affirmed the Fifth Circuit's judgment (746 F.3d 167) upholding Tribal-court jurisdiction by an equally divided vote, without opinion; the affirmance has no precedential value beyond the case.

**In the court's words.**

**Later treatment.**
- Lead to check, not verified in this research: The Fifth Circuit decision below, Dolgencorp, Inc. v. Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians, 746 F.3d 167 (5th Cir. 2014), remains binding in that circuit (not opened).

### Lewis v. Clarke (2017)

*581 U.S. 155 (2017)* [@lewis-v-clarke]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Sotomayor, J.; 8-0 (Thomas, J., and Ginsburg, J., concurring in the judgment; Gorsuch, J., not participating).
Nations: Mohegan Tribe of Indians of Connecticut.

**Question.** Does Tribal sovereign immunity bar a negligence suit against a Tribal gaming authority employee sued in his individual capacity for driving on the job?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that in an individual-capacity suit the employee, not the Tribe, is the real party in interest, so Tribal immunity does not apply, and that the Tribe's indemnification of the employee does not extend immunity to him.

**In the court's words.**

> “in a suit brought against a tribal employee in his individual capacity, the employee, not the tribe, is the real party in interest and the tribe's sovereign immunity is not implicated” [@lewis-v-clarke, p. 158]

> “We hold that an indemnification provision cannot, as a matter of law, extend sovereign immunity to individual employees who would otherwise not fall under its protective cloak” [@lewis-v-clarke, p. 164-165]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Tribal immunity does not shield employees sued personally; a Tribal claims or ADR process for injuries involving Tribal enterprises (for example, casino tort claims procedures) can channel such claims.

*Topics:* sovereign-immunity, dispute-resolution.

### McGirt v. Oklahoma (2020)

*591 U.S. 894 (2020)* [@mcgirt-v-oklahoma]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Gorsuch, J.; 5-4 (Roberts, C.J., joined by Alito and Kavanaugh, JJ., and by Thomas, J., except as to n.9, dissenting; Thomas, J., dissenting).
Nations: The Muscogee (Creek) Nation; The Seminole Nation of Oklahoma (McGirt's nation).

**Question.** Does land reserved for the Muscogee (Creek) Nation since the 19th century remain "Indian country" for purposes of the Major Crimes Act?

**Holding.** Yes. The Court held that Congress never disestablished the Creek Reservation, so it remains Indian country under the Major Crimes Act, and Oklahoma lacked jurisdiction to prosecute McGirt, a Native person, for crimes committed there.

**In the court's words.**

> “Today we are asked whether the land these treaties promised remains an Indian reservation for purposes of federal criminal law” [@mcgirt-v-oklahoma, p. 897-898]

> “To determine whether a tribe continues to hold a reservation, there is only one place we may look: the Acts of Congress” [@mcgirt-v-oklahoma, p. 903]

> “Because Congress has not said otherwise, we hold the government to its word” [@mcgirt-v-oklahoma, p. 898]

**Later treatment.**
- Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta (2022) held that States have concurrent jurisdiction over crimes by non-Indians against Indians in Indian country, limiting the practical reach of McGirt. [@oklahoma-v-castro-huerta]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Reservation status determines the territorial scope of a nation's forums; after McGirt, eastern Oklahoma nations expanded court systems and intergovernmental agreements, a practical context for intergovernmental ADR.

*Topics:* land-and-treaty-rights, criminal-jurisdiction, jurisdiction.

### United States v. Cooley (2021)

*593 U.S. 345 (2021)* [@united-states-v-cooley]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Breyer, J.; 9-0 (Alito, J., concurring).
Nations: Crow Tribe of Montana (Apsáalooke Nation).

**Question.** May a Tribal police officer temporarily detain and search a non-Indian on a public right-of-way within a reservation for apparent violations of state or federal law?

**Holding.** Yes. The Court held that under Montana's second exception a Tribe retains inherent authority to detain and search non-Indians on such rights-of-way for a reasonable time when the officer has reason to believe the person may commit or has committed a crime, and it vacated the Ninth Circuit judgment affirming suppression and remanded.

**In the court's words.**

> “To deny a tribal police officer authority to search and detain for a reasonable time any person he or she believes may commit or has committed a crime would make it difficult for tribes to protect themselves against ongoing threats” [@united-states-v-cooley, p. 350-351]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** First modern Supreme Court application of Montana's second exception in favor of Tribal authority; supports code provisions on Tribal police and peacekeeping powers over all persons pending referral.

*Topics:* criminal-jurisdiction, jurisdiction, self-governance.

### Yellen v. Confederated Tribes of the Chehalis Reservation (2021)

*594 U.S. 338 (2021)* [@yellen-v-confederated-tribes-of-chehalis-reservation]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Sotomayor, J.; 6-3 (Gorsuch, J., joined by Thomas and Kagan, JJ., dissenting).
Nations: Confederated Tribes of the Chehalis Reservation; Alaska Native regional and village corporations (ANCs) as parties.

**Question.** Are Alaska Native Corporations "Indian tribes" under ISDA, and so eligible for CARES Act Title V funds?

**Holding.** Yes. The Court held that Alaska Native corporations fall within ISDA's definition of "Indian tribe" whether or not they are federally recognized Tribes, and so qualify for CARES Act Title V funding.

**In the court's words.**

> “Under the plain meaning of ISDA, ANCs are Indian tribes, regardless of whether they are also federally recognized tribes” [@yellen-v-confederated-tribes-of-chehalis-reservation, p. 351]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Clarifies the distinct legal status of Alaska Native corporations versus federally recognized Tribes; an Alaska-specific chapter of the model code should distinguish Tribal forums from ANC corporate dispute processes.

*Topics:* status-and-recognition.

### Denezpi v. United States (2022)

*596 U.S. 591 (2022)* [@denezpi-v-united-states]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Barrett, J.; 6-3 (Gorsuch, J., dissenting, joined by Sotomayor and Kagan, JJ., as to Parts I and III).
Nations: Navajo Nation (Denezpi's nation); Ute Mountain Ute Tribe (offense occurred on its reservation).

**Question.** Does the Double Jeopardy Clause bar a federal prosecution after a conviction in a Court of Indian Offenses for a Tribal-law offense arising from the same act?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that the Clause bars successive prosecutions only for the same offense, and because the Court of Indian Offenses prosecution was for a Tribal-ordinance offense and the federal prosecution was for a federal offense, the second prosecution did not violate the Clause, whatever the source of that court's prosecutorial power.

**In the court's words.**

> “Because Denezpi's second prosecution did not place him in jeopardy again “for the same offence,” that prosecution did not violate the Double Jeopardy Clause” [@denezpi-v-united-states, p. 599]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Concerns Courts of Indian Offenses, which still serve some nations [@cfr-25-part-11-courts-of-indian-offenses]; relevant if the model code addresses a nation's move from a Court of Indian Offenses to its own forums.

*Topics:* criminal-jurisdiction, tribal-courts.

### Ysleta del Sur Pueblo v. Texas (2022)

*596 U.S. 685 (2022)* [@ysleta-del-sur-pueblo-v-texas]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Gorsuch, J.; 5-4 (Roberts, C.J., joined by Thomas, Alito and Kavanaugh, JJ., dissenting).
Nations: Ysleta del Sur Pueblo.

**Question.** Does the 1987 Restoration Act subject all of the Pueblo's gaming to Texas gaming regulations, or only bar games that Texas prohibits outright?

**Holding.** The Court held that the Restoration Act bans on the Pueblo's lands only gaming activities that Texas prohibits, not those it merely regulates, adopting the prohibitory/regulatory distinction from California v. Cabazon Band.

**In the court's words.**

> “The Restoration Act bans as a matter of federal law on tribal lands only those gaming activities also banned in Texas” [@ysleta-del-sur-pueblo-v-texas, p. 708]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Shows how statute-specific gaming regimes for particular nations work, which matters for compacts and dispute clauses for nations outside the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act's ordinary framework.

*Topics:* gaming, jurisdiction.

### Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta (2022)

*597 U.S. 629 (2022)* [@oklahoma-v-castro-huerta]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Kavanaugh, J.; 5-4 (Gorsuch, J., joined by Breyer, Sotomayor and Kagan, JJ., dissenting).
Nations: Cherokee Nation (crime occurred within its reservation; victim was a Native child).

**Question.** Does the federal government have exclusive jurisdiction to prosecute crimes committed by non-Indians against Indians in Indian country?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that the federal government and the State have concurrent jurisdiction over such crimes, reasoning that Indian country is part of the State and that neither the General Crimes Act, Public Law 280, nor Bracker balancing preempts state jurisdiction.

**In the court's words.**

> “Indian country is part of the State, not separate from the State” [@oklahoma-v-castro-huerta, p. 636]

> “the Federal Government and the State have concurrent jurisdiction to prosecute crimes committed by non-Indians against Indians in Indian country” [@oklahoma-v-castro-huerta, p. 633]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Increases state criminal authority in Indian country, making Tribal-state cooperative agreements (and their dispute clauses) more important.

*Topics:* criminal-jurisdiction, jurisdiction.

### Haaland v. Brackeen (2023)

*599 U.S. 255 (2023)* [@haaland-v-brackeen]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Barrett, J.; 7-2 (Thomas, J., and Alito, J., dissenting; Gorsuch, J., concurring, joined by Sotomayor and Jackson, JJ., as to Parts I and III; Kavanaugh, J., concurring).
Nations: Cherokee Nation; Oneida Nation; Quinault Indian Nation; Morongo Band of Mission Indians; Navajo Nation.

**Question.** Is the Indian Child Welfare Act within Congress's power and consistent with the anticommandeering principle and equal protection?

**Holding.** The Court rejected the Article I and anticommandeering challenges to the Indian Child Welfare Act on the merits and held that the individual petitioners and Texas lacked standing to raise the equal-protection and nondelegation challenges.

**In the court's words.**

> “Our cases leave little doubt that Congress’s power in this field is muscular, superseding both tribal and state authority” [@haaland-v-brackeen, p. 273]

> “But the bottom line is that we reject all of petitioners’ challenges to the statute, some on the merits and others for lack of standing” [@haaland-v-brackeen, p. 264]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Upholds the Indian Child Welfare Act, including the role of Tribal courts and Tribal placement preferences; relevant to family dispute-resolution and child-welfare provisions in the model code.

*Topics:* child-welfare, status-and-recognition, jurisdiction.

### Lac du Flambeau Band of Lake Superior Chippewa Indians v. Coughlin (2023)

*599 U.S. 382 (2023)* [@lac-du-flambeau-band-v-coughlin]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Jackson, J.; 8-1 (Gorsuch, J., dissenting; Thomas, J., concurring in the judgment).
Nations: Lac du Flambeau Band of Lake Superior Chippewa Indians.

**Question.** Does the Bankruptcy Code abrogate Tribal sovereign immunity?

**Holding.** Yes. The Court held that the Bankruptcy Code's abrogation for "governmental units," including any "other foreign or domestic government," unequivocally covers federally recognized Tribes, so a debtor could pursue the Band's lending business for violating the automatic stay.

**In the court's words.**

> “The Code unequivocally abrogates the sovereign immunity of all governments, categorically. Tribes are indisputably governments. Therefore, § 106(a) unmistakably abrogates their sovereign immunity too” [@lac-du-flambeau-band-v-coughlin, p. 393]

> “Federally recognized tribes undeniably fit that description” [@lac-du-flambeau-band-v-coughlin, p. 388]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** A congressional abrogation that applies to Tribal enterprises in bankruptcy; a model code on commercial ADR should flag statutes like this where immunity does not apply regardless of contract language.

*Topics:* sovereign-immunity, enforcement.

### Arizona v. Navajo Nation (2023)

*599 U.S. 555 (2023)* [@arizona-v-navajo-nation]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Kavanaugh, J.; 5-4 (Gorsuch, J., joined by Sotomayor, Kagan and Jackson, JJ., dissenting; Thomas, J., concurring).
Nations: Navajo Nation.

**Question.** Does the 1868 Treaty with the Navajos require the United States to take affirmative steps to assess and secure water for the Navajo Nation?

**Holding.** No. The Court held that the treaty reserved water rights under Winters but imposed no affirmative duty on the United States to assess the Nation's water needs or build infrastructure, so the Nation's breach-of-trust claim failed.

**In the court's words.**

> “In light of the treaty’s text and history, we conclude that the treaty does not require the United States to take those affirmative steps” [@arizona-v-navajo-nation, p. 559]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** Limits judicial enforcement of trust duties unless specific duties are written into treaties or statutes; nations negotiating settlements and agreements should write enforceable duties and dispute processes into the text.

*Topics:* land-and-treaty-rights.

### Becerra v. San Carlos Apache Tribe (2024)

*602 U.S. 222 (2024)* [@becerra-v-san-carlos-apache-tribe]. Supreme Court of the United States; opinion by Roberts, C.J.; 5-4 (Kavanaugh, J., joined by Thomas, Alito and Barrett, JJ., dissenting).
Nations: San Carlos Apache Tribe; Northern Arapaho Tribe (companion case).

**Question.** Must the Indian Health Service pay contract support costs that a Tribe incurs when it collects and spends third-party "program income" under an the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act self-determination contract?

**Holding.** Yes. The Court held that the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act requires the Indian Health Service to pay contract support costs a Tribe incurs in spending program income to further the federal programs transferred to it under a self-determination contract.

**In the court's words.**

> “Because a self-determination contract requires a tribe to spend program income to further the programs transferred to it in the contract, these provisions require IHS to pay contract support costs when a tribe does so, just as IHS must pay contract support costs to support a tribe’s spending of the Secretarial amount” [@becerra-v-san-carlos-apache-tribe, p. 233]

**Why it matters here (project analysis).** The only Indian-law merits decision of October Terms 2023-2025. It arose from contract disputes under the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act, whose Contract Disputes Act procedures are a federal ADR model the commentary can compare with Tribal ADR.

*Topics:* self-governance, dispute-resolution.
