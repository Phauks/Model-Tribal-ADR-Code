# Hawaiʻi, Puerto Rico, American Samoa, the Northern Mariana Islands and Guam

Native Hawaiians, the CHamoru people of Guam and the Northern Mariana Islands, the Refaluwasch (Carolinian) people and Samoans in American Samoa are not Tribes, and Tribal status terms are not used for them here ([terminology guide](../../docs/terminology.md)). Federal law draws the same line from its own side. The federal acknowledgment rules define "Indigenous" as "native to the continental United States," and "Continental United States" as "the contiguous 48 states and Alaska" [@cfr-25-83-1]. So the history below runs on a different track from the Tribal one: kingdoms and chiefly orders, cessions, statutes for each island government, and courts that apply custom.

## Hawaiʻi

**1840. A Kingdom with courts.** The Hawaiian Kingdom's 1840 Constitution "created a judicial system, including a supreme court, consisting of the king, the kuhina nui (prime minister), and four others appointed by the house of representatives" [@mackenzie-native-hawaiian-law-treatise-2015, ch. 1, § IV]. Sally Engle Merry's history traces how the Kingdom's courts took up Anglo-American law over the following decades [@merry-colonizing-hawaii-2000], and Jonathan Kay Kamakawiwoʻole Osorio's history follows the Kingdom's constitutional changes to 1887 [@osorio-dismembering-lahui-2002].

**1893 to 1898. Overthrow and annexation.** In 1993 Congress apologized "to Native Hawaiians on behalf of the people of the United States for the overthrow of the Kingdom of Hawaii on January 17, 1893 with the participation of agents and citizens of the United States." The same resolution says that "Nothing in this Joint Resolution is intended to serve as a settlement of any claims against the United States" [@apology-resolution-1993, secs. 1, 3].

**1978. State constitutional amendments.** The 1978 convention created the Office of Hawaiian Affairs and added a protection of "all rights, customarily and traditionally exercised for subsistence, cultural and religious purposes and possessed by ahupua'a tenants who are descendants of native Hawaiians who inhabited the Hawaiian Islands prior to 1778" [@hawaii-constitution, art. XII, secs. 5, 7].

**2011 and 2016. Recognition by the State, a path offered by Interior.** Hawaiʻi's Act 195 states that "The Native Hawaiian people are hereby recognized as the only indigenous, aboriginal, maoli people of Hawaii" [@hawaii-revised-statutes-10h-1]. In 2016 the Interior Department adopted procedures for reestablishing a formal government-to-government relationship with a Native Hawaiian government, if the community forms one and asks [@interior-native-hawaiian-final-rule-2016; @cfr-43-part-50]. Neither step makes Native Hawaiians a Tribe, and many Kānaka Maoli reject the federal-recognition model itself [@kauanui-paradoxes-of-hawaiian-sovereignty-2018].

**Hoʻoponopono.** The treatise describes hoʻoponopono as "the Native Hawaiian method of restoring and maintaining good relationships among family members and with the spiritual realm." It sets it beside mediation and draws the difference plainly: "The purpose of mediation is to come to an agreement, while the purpose of hoʻoponopono is to restore a relationship." It also reports the view that "it may not be appropriate to incorporate elements of a process intended for family use into the commercial context of Western-style mediation" [@mackenzie-native-hawaiian-law-treatise-2015, ch. 18, §§ II, II.B]. The treatise names Mary Kawena Pukui as "the main source of contemporary knowledge" of the practice, through *Nānā I Ke Kumu* [@mackenzie-native-hawaiian-law-treatise-2015, ch. 18, § II.B]. It is a family and spiritual practice. It is named here as history; its steps are not described, and it is not offered as a model for anyone else's process.

## American Samoa

**1900 and 1904. Cessions by the chiefs.** The chiefs of Tutuila ceded their islands on terms that "The Chiefs of the towns will be entitled to retain their individual control of the separate towns," subject to federal law and supervision [@instrument-of-cession-tutuila-1900, art. 3]. The Manuʻa instrument provided that "the rights of the Chiefs in each village and of all people concerning their property according to their custom shall be recognized" [@instrument-of-cession-manua-1904]. Congress ratified both in 1929, "as of April 10, 1900, and July 16, 1904," dates that differ from those printed on the instruments [@usc-48-1661, (a)].

**American Samoa today.** American Samoa's law preserves "The customs of the Samoan people not in conflict with the laws of American Samoa or the laws of the United States" and the village, county and district councils of chiefs [@asca-1-0202-preservation-of-samoan-customs]. The High Court's Land and Titles Division has "exclusive jurisdiction" over matai titles and land controversies [@asca-title-3-judiciary-land-titles-village-courts, § 3.0208(b)]. Before such a case may be filed, each party must appear in person, "without an attorney or counsel," at least twice before the Secretary of Samoan Affairs, who certifies that the dispute could not be resolved [@asca-43-0302-certificate-irreconcilable-dispute, (a)].

**2015 and 2021. Citizenship and land.** Two federal courts of appeals have declined to extend birthright citizenship to American Samoa. Both weighed what citizenship imposed by courts could mean for American Samoa's land rules and way of life. The D.C. Circuit, in *Tuaua v. United States*, pointed to "American Samoa’s traditional, racially-based land alienation rules" [@tuaua-v-united-states-2015, p. 310]. The Tenth Circuit, in *Fitisemanu v. United States*, noted that "There are also racial restrictions on land ownership requiring landowners to be at least 50% American Samoan" [@fitisemanu-v-united-states-2021, p. 866].

**Commonwealth law on custom.** Commonwealth law makes the common law the rule of decision "in the absence of written law or local customary law to the contrary" [@cmc-7-3401-applicability-of-common-law]. It also codifies Carolinian family-land custom. When the family cannot agree on a trustee, a court chooses one "in accordance with the customs of the Carolinian people" [@cmc-8-2904-carolinian-custom-family-land, (a)(6)]. No statute setting up a customary dispute body was found.
