# Federal Indian Law and Native Nations: A Timeline

The statutes, regulations, executive acts and court decisions in this timeline shaped the law that applies to [[native-nations|Native nations]], their citizens and their lands today, and how far other governments respect Tribal law and Tribal courts. They run by era, from before contact to the present, and each era after contact has a chapter of its own (listed in the table below). The leading court decisions sit in their place in each era, in boxes that give the question, the holding, the court's own words and what later courts made of them. Three chapters follow the eras. [Tribal Courts](/foundations/legal-history-timeline-08-tribal-courts) traces the development of [[tribal-court|Tribal courts]], including [[peacemaking]]. [Hawaiʻi, Puerto Rico, American Samoa, the Northern Mariana Islands and Guam](/foundations/legal-history-timeline-09-hawaii-puerto-rico-american-samoa-mariana-islands-guam) covers those places. [How States Recognize Tribal Judgments](/foundations/legal-history-timeline-10-state-recognition-of-tribal-judgments) sets out the state rules that decide whether a Tribal judgment is enforced off the [[reservation]]. An index of the cases by topic is at the back of the book.

## How to Read the Entries

### Years, Cases and Analysis

Each entry opens with its year. A court decision of lasting importance has a box of its own. The box heading gives the full citation. Beneath it are the nations involved, named as the Bureau of Indian Affairs list or the nation itself writes its name today. For a decision of the Supreme Court of the United States, the box also names the Court as it sat (the Marshall Court, the Warren Court and so on), gives the vote and lists each justice's part. These come from Oyez's case data or, where Oyez has none, from the official report [@oyez-supreme-court-case-data]. The box then states the question and the holding. Under "Quotes" it gives the court's own words, and under "Treatment" it gives later decisions or statutes that applied, limited, distinguished or declined to extend the case.

Boxes labeled "Analysis" give a short reading of an entry's place in the law. They are interpretation, not statements of the sources and not legal advice. A holding stated here is a starting point for research, not a statement of current law.

### Sources and Words

Each entry cites its sources in the notes at the end of the chapter. Quotations were checked word for word against the official reports, the court's own publication or the statute as enacted. Where only an unofficial copy of an opinion was available, the quotation is cited without a page. Older opinions use language about Native people that is offensive today; quotations are chosen for their legal content and are reproduced as written. Holdings use the courts' own legal categories, such as "non-Indian" and "nonmember," because the rule depends on them.

Statutes and opinions use "Indian," "tribe" and "member." Those words appear in quotations and legal terms (for example [[indian-country]]); elsewhere the text follows the [terminology guide](../../docs/terminology.md). Each nation named here exists today. Events are in the past tense, with dates.

Traditional justice systems are described only from what nations and Native scholars have published. Nothing ceremonial or community-held is described. The standard histories and treatises behind the entries are listed, with what each covers, in *Federal Indian law and Tribal justice: standard works*.

## Eras and Where the Boundaries Come From

The National Congress of American Indians (NCAI) divides federal Indian policy into periods. They are "1492–1828 COLONIAL PERIOD," "1828–1887 REMOVAL, RESERVATION, & TREATY PERIOD," "1887–1934 ALLOTMENT & ASSIMILATION PERIOD," "1934–1945 INDIAN REORGANIZATION PERIOD," "1945–1968 TERMINATION PERIOD," "1968–2000 SELF-DETERMINATION PERIOD" and "2000–PRESENT NATION-TO-NATION PERIOD" [@ncai-tribal-nations-and-the-united-states, pp. 12-13]. The eras here follow those names because they come from an organization of Native nations, but some boundaries move so that related laws sit together:

| Era | Years used here | Difference from NCAI |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before contact, and traditional justice | to 1492, and continuing | NCAI's "pre-contact" block; traditional systems did not end in 1492 and continue today |
| [Treaty era](/foundations/legal-history-timeline-01-treaty-era) | 1492 to 1871 | Runs to the 1871 end of treaty-making rather than 1828 |
| [Removal](/foundations/legal-history-timeline-02-removal) | 1830 to about 1850 | Removal is split out; the end year is editorial |
| [Reservations and allotment](/foundations/legal-history-timeline-03-reservations-and-allotment) | 1871 to 1934 | Starts in 1871 so the 1883 Courts of Indian Offenses and the 1885 Major Crimes Act fall inside it |
| [Reorganization](/foundations/legal-history-timeline-04-reorganization) | 1934 to 1946 | Ends in 1946 to include the Indian Claims Commission Act |
| [Termination](/foundations/legal-history-timeline-05-termination) | 1946 to 1968 | Same as NCAI |
| [Self-determination](/foundations/legal-history-timeline-06-self-determination) | 1968 to 1987 | Ends where the modern statutory framework begins |
| [Modern era](/foundations/legal-history-timeline-07-modern-era) | 1988 to the present | Starts with the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act rather than 2000 |

The boundaries are editorial choices, not legal facts.

## Before Contact, and Traditional Justice

The NCAI guide states: "In the thousands of years preceding the arrival of the Europeans in North America, Native peoples lived in organized societies with their own forms of government—engaging in diplomacy, commerce, and cultural exchange" [@ncai-tribal-nations-and-the-united-states, p. 13].

Those systems are not only history. Writing in *Judicature*, Ada Pecos Melton observes that "In many contemporary tribal communities, dual justice systems exist. One is based on what can be called an American paradigm of justice, and the other is based on what can be called an indigenous paradigm" [@melton-indigenous-justice-systems-and-tribal-society-1995]. Robert Yazzie, then Chief Justice of the Navajo Nation, set out Navajo justice concepts in 1994 [@yazzie-1994-life-comes-from-it]. The Navajo Nation's judiciary describes peacemaking in its own public guide as grounded in Navajo fundamental law [@navajo-nation-judicial-branch-courts-and-peacemaking].

A particular nation's justice system is described from that nation's own published sources, and only with its agreement where the material is community-held.

## Gaps and Open Questions

- The treaty clauses in the treaty era chapter are a sample of nine treaties. A full survey of clauses on crimes and injuries across Kappler's volume 2 has not been done, and each treaty's Kappler page is taken from the treaty database rather than a page image.
- The Hawaiʻi entries rely on one treatise current to about 2014 and on primary records; the treatise's print page numbers are not yet available, so it is cited by chapter and section.
- The U.S. Virgin Islands are not yet covered. For the Northern Mariana Islands, Guam and American Samoa, the Commonwealth Constitution's own text, the Chamorro Land Trust Act's 1975 enactment date, and the official text of the American Samoa and Guam codes (read here from mirrors) still need confirming.
- Several state rules (Arizona, Michigan's adoption date, California's sunset clause) need their official text confirmed.
- The quotations from federal courts of appeals read from unofficial copies need their reporter pages, and later treatment has been confirmed in a citator for only some of the cases.
- For some nineteenth-century Supreme Court cases, the official report names only the author and any dissenter; the other justices are counted in the majority because the report notes no one as not participating. For *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia* the report does not say how Justices McLean and Duvall voted.
- Self-governance compact dispute clauses have not been collected; gaming compact clauses are compared in *Comparing model codes and dispute frameworks*.
- Several subjects of general Indian law are covered briefly or not yet: taking land into trust and *Carcieri v. Salazar*, repatriation under the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act, environmental regulation (including "treatment as a state"), religious freedom, the trust responsibility and *Cobell*, and the Tribal jurisdiction the Violence Against Women Act restored in 2013 and 2022.
- No Indian law merits decision of the Supreme Court after *Becerra* (2024) was found through Nov. 21, 2026.
- The era quotations from the NCAI guide were read from page images and should be checked against the PDF before publication. Several Statutes at Large quotations were matched only against scanned text, which is too noisy to confirm word for word.
