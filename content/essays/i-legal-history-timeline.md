# Federal Indian Law and Native Nations: A Timeline

The statutes, regulations, executive acts and court decisions below shaped the law that applies to [[native-nations|Native nations]], their citizens and their lands today, and how far other governments respect Tribal law and Tribal courts. They run by era, from before contact to the present. The leading court decisions sit in their place in each era, in boxes that give the question, the holding, the court's own words and what later courts made of them. Thematic sections follow the eras. One traces the development of [[tribal-court|Tribal courts]], including [[peacemaking]]. One covers Hawaiʻi, Puerto Rico, American Samoa, the Northern Mariana Islands and Guam. The last sets out the state rules that decide whether a Tribal judgment is enforced off the [[reservation]]. An index of the cases by topic is at the back of the book.

## How to Read the Entries

### Years, Cases and Analysis

Each entry opens with its year. A court decision of lasting importance has a box of its own. The box heading gives the full citation. Beneath it are the nations involved, named as the Bureau of Indian Affairs list or the nation itself writes its name today. For a decision of the Supreme Court of the United States, the box also names the Court as it sat (the Marshall Court, the Warren Court and so on), gives the vote and lists each justice's part. These come from Oyez's case data or, where Oyez has none, from the official report [@oyez-supreme-court-case-data]. The box then states the question and the holding. Under "Quotes" it gives the court's own words, and under "Treatment" it gives later decisions or statutes that applied, limited, distinguished or declined to extend the case.

Boxes labeled "Analysis" give a short reading of an entry's place in the law. They are interpretation, not statements of the sources and not legal advice. A holding stated here is a starting point for research, not a statement of current law.

### Sources and Words

Each entry cites its sources in the notes at the end of the chapter. Quotations were checked word for word against the official reports, the court's own publication or the statute as enacted. Where only an unofficial copy of an opinion was available, the quotation is cited without a page. Older opinions use language about Native people that is offensive today; quotations are chosen for their legal content and are reproduced as written. Holdings use the courts' own legal categories, such as "non-Indian" and "nonmember," because the rule depends on them.

Statutes and opinions use "Indian," "tribe" and "member." Those words appear in quotations and legal terms (for example [[indian-country]]); elsewhere the text follows the [terminology guide](../../docs/terminology.md). Each nation named here exists today. Events are in the past tense, with dates.

Traditional justice systems are described only from what nations and Native scholars have published. Nothing ceremonial or community-held is described. The standard histories and treatises behind the entries are listed, with what each covers, in *Federal Indian law and Tribal justice: standard works*.

## Eras and Where the Boundaries Come From

The National Congress of American Indians (NCAI) divides federal Indian policy into periods. They are "1492–1828 COLONIAL PERIOD," "1828–1887 REMOVAL, RESERVATION, & TREATY PERIOD," "1887–1934 ALLOTMENT & ASSIMILATION PERIOD," "1934–1945 INDIAN REORGANIZATION PERIOD," "1945–1968 TERMINATION PERIOD," "1968–2000 SELF-DETERMINATION PERIOD" and "2000–PRESENT NATION-TO-NATION PERIOD" [@ncai-tribal-nations-and-the-united-states, pp. 12-13]. The eras below follow those names because they come from an organization of Native nations, but some boundaries move so that related laws sit together:

| Era | Years used here | Difference from NCAI |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before contact, and traditional justice | to 1492, and continuing | NCAI's "pre-contact" block; traditional systems did not end in 1492 and continue today |
| Treaty era | 1492 to 1871 | Runs to the 1871 end of treaty-making rather than 1828 |
| Removal | 1830 to about 1850 | Removal is split out; the end year is editorial |
| Reservations and allotment | 1871 to 1934 | Starts in 1871 so the 1883 Courts of Indian Offenses and the 1885 Major Crimes Act fall inside it |
| Reorganization | 1934 to 1946 | Ends in 1946 to include the Indian Claims Commission Act |
| Termination | 1946 to 1968 | Same as NCAI |
| Self-determination | 1968 to 1987 | Ends where the modern statutory framework begins |
| Modern era | 1988 to the present | Starts with the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act rather than 2000 |

The boundaries are editorial choices, not legal facts.

## Before Contact, and Traditional Justice

The NCAI guide states: "In the thousands of years preceding the arrival of the Europeans in North America, Native peoples lived in organized societies with their own forms of government—engaging in diplomacy, commerce, and cultural exchange" [@ncai-tribal-nations-and-the-united-states, p. 13].

Those systems are not only history. Writing in *Judicature*, Ada Pecos Melton observes that "In many contemporary tribal communities, dual justice systems exist. One is based on what can be called an American paradigm of justice, and the other is based on what can be called an indigenous paradigm" [@melton-indigenous-justice-systems-and-tribal-society-1995]. Robert Yazzie, then Chief Justice of the Navajo Nation, set out Navajo justice concepts in 1994 [@yazzie-1994-life-comes-from-it]. The Navajo Nation's judiciary describes peacemaking in its own public guide as grounded in Navajo fundamental law [@navajo-nation-judicial-branch-courts-and-peacemaking].

A particular nation's justice system is described from that nation's own published sources, and only with its agreement where the material is community-held.

### Treaty Clauses on Crimes and Injuries

Many treaties said how the parties would handle a wrong done by one side's people to the other's.

::: analysis How the treaty clauses on crimes and injuries changed
Read together, the clauses show a change in who decides: from joint trials, to surrender of offenders, to complaint between governments, to the federal forum alone.
:::

**1778. Mixed trials.** The first treaty the United States made with a Native nation barred either party from punishing the other's citizens "otherwise than by securing the offender or offenders by imprisonment, or any other competent means, till a fair and impartial trial can be had by judges or juries of both parties, as near as can be to the laws, customs and usages of the contracting parties and natural justice" [@treaty-with-the-delawares-1778, art. IV]. The trial was to be joint, and the law it applied a blend of both peoples' law.

**1785 and 1791. Surrender and no retaliation.** The Hopewell treaty bound the Cherokee Nation to "deliver up" a person who committed a capital crime against a citizen, "to be punished according to the ordinances of the United States," and declared that "the punishment of the innocent under the idea of retaliation, is unjust" [@treaty-with-the-cherokee-1785, arts. VI, VIII]. The Holston treaty repeated both terms and added that neither side would retaliate "until satisfaction shall have been demanded of the party of which the aggressor is, and shall have been refused" [@treaty-with-the-cherokee-1791, arts. X, XII].

**1794 and 1795. Complaint between governments.** The Canandaigua treaty with the Six Nations provided that "for injuries done by individuals on either side, no private revenge or retaliation shall take place; but, instead thereof, complaint shall be made by the party injured, to the other," with the injured side's complaint going to the President or to "the principal chiefs of the Six Nations, or of the nation to which the offender belongs" [@treaty-with-the-six-nations-1794, art. VII]. The Greenville treaty used nearly the same words [@treaty-with-the-wyandot-etc-1795, art. IX].

**1867 and 1868. The "bad men" clauses.** The peace treaties of 1867 and 1868 used one formula. If "bad men among the whites" wronged a Native person, the United States would, "upon proof made to the agent," arrest and punish the offender "and also re-imburse the injured person for the loss sustained." If "bad men among the Indians" did wrong, the nation would "deliver up the wrong-doer to the United States, to be tried and punished according to its laws" [@treaty-with-the-sioux-and-arapaho-1868, art. 1]. The same clause appears in the treaties with the Kiowa and Comanche, the Ute and the Navajo [@treaty-with-the-kiowa-and-comanche-1867, art. 1; @treaty-with-the-ute-1868, art. 6; @treaty-with-the-navaho-1868, art. 1]. By then the forum was the federal one alone.

**The clauses still bind.** The Federal Circuit held in *Tsosie v. United States* that the Navajo clause "has not become obsolete or been abandoned or preempted in any sense that affects its enforceability by suit in the Claims Court under the Tucker Act" [@tsosie-v-united-states-1987]. The Court of Federal Claims held in *Elk v. United States*, under the Fort Laramie clause, that the injured party "is entitled to full compensation for her actual loss" [@elk-v-united-states-2009]. The Federal Circuit has since held in *Richard v. United States* that the clause is not limited to wrongs by federal agents [@richard-v-united-states-2012], and in *Jones v. United States* that the Court of Federal Claims had read the Ute clause too narrowly [@jones-v-united-states-2017]. In an unpublished decision, *Flying Horse v. United States*, the same court affirmed the dismissal of a claim that had not first gone to the agency, because the claimant "has failed to exhaust his administrative remedies under the “Bad Men” clause" [@flying-horse-v-united-states-2017]. The clause's own first step, "proof made to the agent," still comes before any court.

Prucha's history of treaty-making and Williams's study of early treaty diplomacy are the standard secondary works on these terms [@prucha-american-indian-treaties-1994; @williams-linking-arms-together-1997]. The treaty texts are in volume 2 of Kappler's compilation [@kappler-indian-affairs-laws-and-treaties-vol-2-1904].

**1928. Meriam Report.** A government-commissioned survey found that "For the punishment of petty offenses and the settlement of minor civil disputes the only judicial machinery available is the Court of Indian Offenses," that "More than one-half of the reservations exist without them," and that decisions turned on "the current native ideas of property and justice" rather than on code or precedent [@meriam-report-1928, ch. 13].

## Tribal Courts

This thread runs across the eras above.

### Constitutional Courts before Federal Courts

The Cherokee Nation had a Supreme Court from 1823; a 2018 dissertation calls itself "the first legal history of the Cherokee Supreme Court" [@martin-cherokee-supreme-court-1823-1835-dissertation-2018]. The 1827 constitution vested judicial power in that court and lower courts [@cherokee-nation-constitution-1827-article-v, art. V, sec. 1].

::: analysis Constitutional Tribal courts before federal courts
A constitutional judiciary existed decades before any federal court on a reservation, which supports describing Tribal courts as an exercise of inherent sovereignty.
:::

### Imposed Courts, Abolition and Rebuilding

Courts of Indian Offenses followed in 1883 [@rules-governing-court-of-indian-offenses-1883]. William T. Hagan's history of these courts and the Indian police is subtitled *Experiments in Acculturation and Control* [@hagan-indian-police-and-judges]. The Curtis Act abolished the courts of Indian Territory in 1898 [@curtis-act-1898, sec. 28]. The Cherokee Nation created its Judicial Appeals Tribunal under its 1975 Constitution and re-established its District Court "by Legislative Act in 1991" [@cherokee-nation-judicial-branch-history].

The Navajo Nation's judiciary states that "In 1892, the Navajo Courts of Indian Offenses were established" and that the Council "created the Navajo Nation Courts on April 1, 1959" [@navajo-nation-judicial-branch-history-of-the-courts], and that "In 1982, the Peacemaking Court was established by vote of the Judicial Conference" [@navajo-nation-judicial-branch-courts-and-peacemaking]. Former Justice Raymond D. Austin's book describes how Navajo courts apply Navajo common law [@austin-navajo-courts-and-navajo-common-law-2009].

Federal regulations at 25 C.F.R. Part 11 still provide Courts of Indian Offenses "in those areas of Indian country where tribes retain jurisdiction over Indians that is exclusive of State jurisdiction but where tribal courts have not been established," and direct each court to "apply the customs of the tribe" consistent with the regulations [@cfr-25-part-11-courts-of-indian-offenses, secs. 11.102, 11.110].

### National Organizations

The National American Indian Court Judges Association was established in 1969 [@national-american-indian-court-judges-association-history] and published a long-range planning report on Indian courts in 1978 [@national-american-indian-court-judges-association-indian-courts-and-the-future-1978]. The Tribal Law and Policy Institute's Tribal Court Clearinghouse was "established in June 1997" [@tribal-court-clearinghouse-tribal-law-and-policy-institute].

### Peacemaking

The Native American Rights Fund's Indigenous Peacemaking Initiative, founded in 1992, states: "PEACEMAKING IS NOT ALTERNATIVE DISPUTE RESOLUTION" and "THE TERM REFERS TO TRADITIONAL WAYS OF RESOLVING CONFLICT IN INDIGENOUS COMMUNITIES" [@narf-ipi-home]. Writing about outside courts borrowing peacemaking, Carole Goldberg asks: "But has the rhetoric merely shifted from condescension to impractical romanticizing?" [@goldberg-overextended-borrowing-tribal-peacemaking-1997].

### Custom in Tribal Courts

Scholarship on how Tribal courts find and apply custom includes Gloria Valencia-Weber's 1994 article [@valencia-weber-1994-tribal-courts-custom-innovative-law] and Matthew L.M. Fletcher's 2007 article, which argues that "tribal judges have a great deal to learn from H.L.A. Hart's theory of primary and secondary rules" [@fletcher-rethinking-customary-law-tribal-court-jurisprudence-2007]. Christine Zuni Cruz describes the Southwest Intertribal Court of Appeals, a shared appellate court [@zuni-cruz-southwest-intertribal-court-of-appeals-1994].

## Hawaiʻi, Puerto Rico, American Samoa, the Northern Mariana Islands and Guam

Native Hawaiians, the CHamoru people of Guam and the Northern Mariana Islands, the Refaluwasch (Carolinian) people and Samoans in American Samoa are not Tribes, and Tribal status terms are not used for them here ([terminology guide](../../docs/terminology.md)). Federal law draws the same line from its own side. The federal acknowledgment rules define "Indigenous" as "native to the continental United States," and "Continental United States" as "the contiguous 48 states and Alaska" [@cfr-25-83-1]. So the history below runs on a different track from the Tribal one: kingdoms and chiefly orders, cessions, statutes for each island government, and courts that apply custom.

### Hawaiʻi

**1840. A Kingdom with courts.** The Hawaiian Kingdom's 1840 Constitution "created a judicial system, including a supreme court, consisting of the king, the kuhina nui (prime minister), and four others appointed by the house of representatives" [@mackenzie-native-hawaiian-law-treatise-2015, ch. 1, § IV]. Sally Engle Merry's history traces how the Kingdom's courts took up Anglo-American law over the following decades [@merry-colonizing-hawaii-2000], and Jonathan Kay Kamakawiwoʻole Osorio's history follows the Kingdom's constitutional changes to 1887 [@osorio-dismembering-lahui-2002].

**1893 to 1898. Overthrow and annexation.** In 1993 Congress apologized "to Native Hawaiians on behalf of the people of the United States for the overthrow of the Kingdom of Hawaii on January 17, 1893 with the participation of agents and citizens of the United States." The same resolution says that "Nothing in this Joint Resolution is intended to serve as a settlement of any claims against the United States" [@apology-resolution-1993, secs. 1, 3].

**1978. State constitutional amendments.** The 1978 convention created the Office of Hawaiian Affairs and added a protection of "all rights, customarily and traditionally exercised for subsistence, cultural and religious purposes and possessed by ahupua'a tenants who are descendants of native Hawaiians who inhabited the Hawaiian Islands prior to 1778" [@hawaii-constitution, art. XII, secs. 5, 7].

:::: case rice-v-cayetano

::: analysis *Rice v. Cayetano* (2000)
For Native Hawaiian institutions, this limits ancestry-based eligibility in state-run bodies.
:::

**2011 and 2016. Recognition by the State, a path offered by Interior.** Hawaiʻi's Act 195 states that "The Native Hawaiian people are hereby recognized as the only indigenous, aboriginal, maoli people of Hawaii" [@hawaii-revised-statutes-10h-1]. In 2016 the Interior Department adopted procedures for reestablishing a formal government-to-government relationship with a Native Hawaiian government, if the community forms one and asks [@interior-native-hawaiian-final-rule-2016; @cfr-43-part-50]. Neither step makes Native Hawaiians a Tribe, and many Kānaka Maoli reject the federal-recognition model itself [@kauanui-paradoxes-of-hawaiian-sovereignty-2018].

**Hoʻoponopono.** The treatise describes hoʻoponopono as "the Native Hawaiian method of restoring and maintaining good relationships among family members and with the spiritual realm." It sets it beside mediation and draws the difference plainly: "The purpose of mediation is to come to an agreement, while the purpose of hoʻoponopono is to restore a relationship." It also reports the view that "it may not be appropriate to incorporate elements of a process intended for family use into the commercial context of Western-style mediation" [@mackenzie-native-hawaiian-law-treatise-2015, ch. 18, §§ II, II.B]. The treatise names Mary Kawena Pukui as "the main source of contemporary knowledge" of the practice, through *Nānā I Ke Kumu* [@mackenzie-native-hawaiian-law-treatise-2015, ch. 18, § II.B]. It is a family and spiritual practice. It is named here as history; its steps are not described, and it is not offered as a model for anyone else's process.

### American Samoa

**1900 and 1904. Cessions by the chiefs.** The chiefs of Tutuila ceded their islands on terms that "The Chiefs of the towns will be entitled to retain their individual control of the separate towns," subject to federal law and supervision [@instrument-of-cession-tutuila-1900, art. 3]. The Manuʻa instrument provided that "the rights of the Chiefs in each village and of all people concerning their property according to their custom shall be recognized" [@instrument-of-cession-manua-1904]. Congress ratified both in 1929, "as of April 10, 1900, and July 16, 1904," dates that differ from those printed on the instruments [@usc-48-1661, (a)].

**American Samoa today.** American Samoa's law preserves "The customs of the Samoan people not in conflict with the laws of American Samoa or the laws of the United States" and the village, county and district councils of chiefs [@asca-1-0202-preservation-of-samoan-customs]. The High Court's Land and Titles Division has "exclusive jurisdiction" over matai titles and land controversies [@asca-title-3-judiciary-land-titles-village-courts, § 3.0208(b)]. Before such a case may be filed, each party must appear in person, "without an attorney or counsel," at least twice before the Secretary of Samoan Affairs, who certifies that the dispute could not be resolved [@asca-43-0302-certificate-irreconcilable-dispute, (a)].

::: analysis Customary dispute resolution in American Samoa
That is a required, non-binding step run by American Samoa's own customary officials before a court will hear the case.
:::

**2015 and 2021. Citizenship and land.** Two federal courts of appeals have declined to extend birthright citizenship to American Samoa. Both weighed what citizenship imposed by courts could mean for American Samoa's land rules and way of life. The D.C. Circuit, in *Tuaua v. United States*, pointed to "American Samoa’s traditional, racially-based land alienation rules" [@tuaua-v-united-states-2015, p. 310]. The Tenth Circuit, in *Fitisemanu v. United States*, noted that "There are also racial restrictions on land ownership requiring landowners to be at least 50% American Samoan" [@fitisemanu-v-united-states-2021, p. 866].

**Commonwealth law on custom.** Commonwealth law makes the common law the rule of decision "in the absence of written law or local customary law to the contrary" [@cmc-7-3401-applicability-of-common-law]. It also codifies Carolinian family-land custom. When the family cannot agree on a trustee, a court chooses one "in accordance with the customs of the Carolinian people" [@cmc-8-2904-carolinian-custom-family-land, (a)(6)]. No statute setting up a customary dispute body was found.

## How States Recognize Tribal Judgments

Robert Laurence's primer explains the choice. "When a state court is asked to enforce a foreign-nation judgment, as opposed to a sister-state judgment, then comity, not full faith and credit, is the governing principle," and "no consistent rule exists among the jurisdictions" [@laurence-enforcement-of-money-judgments-primer-2002, pt. II].

| State | Rule | Model | Notable conditions |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Washington | Superior Court Civil Rule 82.5 [@washington-superior-court-civil-rule-82-5] | Recognition with exceptions | Refused if the Tribal court lacked jurisdiction, "denied due process as provided by the Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968," or does not reciprocate; 2019 amendments let the two courts talk directly |
| Wisconsin | Wis. Stat. § 806.245 [@wisconsin-statutes-806-245] | Full faith and credit by statute | Five conditions, including that the Tribal court grants Wisconsin judgments full faith and credit; the court may review validity |
| Michigan | Mich. Ct. R. 2.615 [@michigan-court-rule-2-615] | Reciprocity by filing | Applies only if the nation enacts a binding measure to enforce Michigan judgments and sends it to the state's court office |
| South Dakota | S.D. Codified Laws § 1-1-25 [@south-dakota-codified-laws-1-1-25] | Restrictive comity | Proponent proves five conditions by clear and convincing evidence; limited case types |
| Oklahoma | Okla. Stat. tit. 12, § 728 [@oklahoma-statutes-title-12-section-728] | Supreme Court standards, reciprocity | Includes Courts of Indian Offenses |
| North Dakota | N.D. R. Ct. 7.2 [@north-dakota-rule-of-court-7-2] | Recognized unless a party objects | Amended effective Mar. 1, 2025 |

Most of these rules apply only to courts of federally recognized nations, several turn on reciprocity, and several test due process by the Indian Civil Rights Act.

## Gaps and Open Questions

- The treaty clauses above are a sample of nine treaties. A full survey of clauses on crimes and injuries across Kappler's volume 2 has not been done, and each treaty's Kappler page is taken from the treaty database rather than a page image.
- The Hawaiʻi entries rely on one treatise current to about 2014 and on primary records; the treatise's print page numbers are not yet available, so it is cited by chapter and section.
- The U.S. Virgin Islands are not yet covered. For the Northern Mariana Islands, Guam and American Samoa, the Commonwealth Constitution's own text, the Chamorro Land Trust Act's 1975 enactment date, and the official text of the American Samoa and Guam codes (read here from mirrors) still need confirming.
- Several state rules (Arizona, Michigan's adoption date, California's sunset clause) need their official text confirmed.
- The quotations from federal courts of appeals read from unofficial copies need their reporter pages, and later treatment has been confirmed in a citator for only some of the cases.
- For some nineteenth-century Supreme Court cases, the official report names only the author and any dissenter; the other justices are counted in the majority because the report notes no one as not participating. For *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia* the report does not say how Justices McLean and Duvall voted.
- Self-governance compact dispute clauses have not been collected; gaming compact clauses are compared in *Comparing model codes and dispute frameworks*.
- Several subjects of general Indian law are covered briefly or not yet: taking land into trust and *Carcieri v. Salazar*, repatriation under the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act, environmental regulation (including "treatment as a state"), religious freedom, the trust responsibility and *Cobell*, and the Tribal jurisdiction the Violence Against Women Act restored in 2013 and 2022.
- No Indian law merits decision of the Supreme Court after *Becerra* (2024) was found through Nov. 21, 2026.
- The era quotations from the NCAI guide were read from page images and should be checked against the PDF before publication. Several Statutes at Large quotations were matched only against scanned text, which is too noisy to confirm word for word.
