# Using AI with Tribal Law

Artificial intelligence (AI) chat tools can help you learn about Tribal courts, but they make mistakes, and a mistake about your nation's law can cost you your case. This page explains what AI can help with, where it goes wrong with Tribal law, and how to check what it tells you. It is legal information, not legal advice.

It follows the Civil Law Self-Help Center's guide to using AI [@civil-law-self-help-center-using-ai] and the American Association of Law Libraries' guide for people who are not lawyers [@aall-2026-ai-best-practices-for-non-lawyers].

## Why Tribal Law Is Hard for AI

Every Native nation has its own government, laws and justice system. As one legal aid lawyer puts it, "One tribal court is not like another, just as a Nevada state court is not the same as a California state court" [@rawlings-2024-nevada-legal-services-indian-law-project]. An answer that is right for one nation can be wrong for the next, and an AI tool may not notice the difference. It may also answer a question about your nation with a rule from a state or from federal law.

An AI tool can only repeat what it learned or can find. A nation may adopt a written constitution and code, or it may "operate without formal written laws under 'custom and tradition'" [@nevada-lawyer-2024-nevadas-tribal-courts]. Court contact details, rules and forms change too; a 2024 article on Nevada's Tribal courts warns that published court information "is outdated as soon as it is published" [@nevada-lawyer-2024-nevadas-tribal-courts]. So an AI tool may not know your nation's current law at all, even when it sounds sure.

## What AI Can Help With

AI works best as a study helper [@civil-law-self-help-center-using-ai]. It can help you:

1. Explain a legal word in plain English. The [glossary](/glossary) on this site defines many of the words used in Tribal courts and dispute resolution.
2. Summarize a section of law you found yourself, such as part of your nation's code that you copied in.
3. Explain, in general, how a case moves through a court.
4. Turn your own notes into a timeline, after you take out names, numbers and addresses.
5. Make a list of questions to ask the court clerk, a lay advocate or a lawyer.

## Where AI Goes Wrong

1. **It can make things up.** AI tools sometimes invent cases, laws and court rules [@civil-law-self-help-center-using-ai]. A made-up code section looks just like a real one.
2. **It can mix up nations.** It may give you another nation's rule, or a state's, as if it were your nation's.
3. **It may not know which court hears your case.** Whether a Tribal, state or federal court has the case depends on where things happened, who the people are and what the case is about. Check with the court clerk.
4. **It sounds confident when it is wrong.** It "may give you biased information in an effort to please you" [@civil-law-self-help-center-using-ai].
5. **It cannot give legal advice.** It cannot tell you what to file, what to say, or what the judge will do [@civil-law-self-help-center-using-ai].

## Keep Private and Cultural Information Out

AI tools may store what you type [@civil-law-self-help-center-using-ai]. Do not type in enrollment numbers, Social Security numbers, home addresses, children's names, or details of a protection order or a child welfare case. If you are in danger, call 911 or the StrongHearts Native Helpline at 1-844-762-8483.

Think twice before typing in cultural knowledge, such as stories, songs, ceremonies, language teachings or family histories. That knowledge may belong to your community, not to you alone. Indigenous communities feel a tension between "protecting Indigenous rights and interests in Indigenous data (including traditional knowledges)" and "supporting open data, machine learning, broad data sharing, and big data initiatives" [@carroll-2020-care-principles-indigenous-data-governance]. The same authors hold that "Indigenous Peoples must be the ones to determine data governance protocols" [@carroll-2020-care-principles-indigenous-data-governance]. Once you type something into an AI tool, you may not control where it goes. Ask your nation's cultural office or the knowledge keepers first.

## Check Every Answer

1. **Find the law itself.** Your nation's page in [Nations](/nations) links the constitution, codes and courts found so far. Read the section the AI named, word for word.
2. **Ask where the answer came from.** Ask the AI tool for its source, then open that source yourself. If you cannot find it, do not rely on it [@civil-law-self-help-center-using-ai].
3. **Call the court clerk.** The clerk can confirm current rules, forms, fees and hearing days. Clerks can explain steps but cannot give legal advice.
4. **Read every word before you file.** You are responsible for what you give the court, and judges can fine or sanction filings that rely on made-up law [@civil-law-self-help-center-using-ai].
5. **Get advice from a person.** Many Tribal courts let both lawyers and lay advocates practice before them, each under the court's own rules; in Nevada, most do [@rawlings-2024-nevada-legal-services-indian-law-project]. Ask the clerk for a list, or contact a Native legal aid program.

## Better and Worse Questions

Clear, detailed questions get better answers [@civil-law-self-help-center-using-ai]. Name the nation and the court, paste in the text you want explained, and ask for general information.

| Better | Worse |
| --- | --- |
| "Explain in plain words what an answer to a complaint is in a Tribal court." | "What should I file?" |
| "Summarize this section of my nation's code that I pasted below, and list any deadlines it names." | "What is the law on custody for all tribes?" |
| "What questions should I ask the clerk of the Tribal court before I ask for a protection order?" | "What will the judge decide?" |
| "Explain the difference between peacemaking and a court hearing in general terms." | "Write my petition for me." |
