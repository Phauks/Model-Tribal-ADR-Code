# Complaint and summons

**{{court.name}}**, {{nation.name}}

Form `complaint-and-summons`, version 0.1.0. A court may adopt or change this form under its own law. If your court has its own form for this, use the court's form.

## Before you start

- Use this form to start a civil case: for example, to ask for money you are owed, the return of property, or payment for damage. For a claim of {{court.small_claims_limit}} or less, the small claims form may be simpler.
- You are the **plaintiff**. The person or business you are suing is the **defendant**.
- Think about whether the dispute could be settled first, by talking, mediation or peacemaking. Ask the clerk what the court offers.
- Filing fee: {{court.fee_civil}}. If you cannot pay, file the request to waive court fees with this form.
- Do not hand the papers to the defendant yourself. {{court.service_rule}} After service, file the proof of service form.
- Court staff can explain the steps but cannot give legal advice.

## Notes for courts adapting this form

These notes are not part of the form. They explain where each part came from.

- **What the six forms share.** All six give a caption, each party's name and contact details, the facts, a signature, and rules on service. Five state the relief sought, a filing fee, attachments, an attorney line and a response time; four are sworn, warn of default and ask for each party's Tribal status or enrollment (comparison of the forms listed in `based_on`, read Nov. 23, 2026).
- **Numbered facts.** Oneida asks the plaintiff to "list each statement separately," which lets the answer admit or deny each one. The model numbers the facts for the same reason.
- **Jurisdiction.** Only the Southern Plains Court of Indian Offenses form states jurisdiction; three forms ask whether the parties live on the reservation; Oneida asks a non-Indian party to consent to "the jurisdiction of the Oneida Judiciary." The model asks the plaintiff to check the grounds and leaves the rule to the court's code. On the limits federal law places on Tribal civil jurisdiction over nonmembers, see *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544 (1981), and *Strate v. A-1 Contractors*, 520 U.S. 438 (1997).
- **Answer time.** Forms give 20 days (Oneida, Pyramid Lake), 21 or 28 days (Keweenaw Bay), 28 days (Little River Band) and 30 days (Southern Plains). Pyramid Lake's summons says "(20) business days" while its instructions say "twenty (20) days." The model uses `court.answer_days` and says to count every day, to avoid that conflict.
- **Service.** Puyallup says, "You cannot serve the defendant/respondent yourself." Service rules vary (Tribal police, certified mail, process server), so the model uses `court.service_rule`.
- **Settlement first.** None of the six complaint forms mentions mediation; Grand Traverse Band's small claims form asks whether the dispute could be resolved "through mediation or other means." The model points to settlement because this project is about dispute resolution; this is the project's choice.
- **Limits.** Pyramid Lake requires six weeks' residence and filing "within two (2) years after the cause." Those are code rules, not form content.
