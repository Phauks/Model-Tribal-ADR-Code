# Petition to recognize an order from another court

**{{court.name}}**, {{nation.name}}

Form `petition-to-recognize-an-order-from-another-court`, version 0.1.0. A court may adopt or change this form under its own law. If your court has its own form for this, use the court's form.

## Before you start

- Use this form to ask {{court.name}} to recognize a judgment or order made by another court, so it can be enforced here.
- Attach a **certified copy** of the other court's order. Ask that court's clerk for one.
- Leave the case number blank. The clerk gives the case a number.
- A **protection order** from another court can be enforced here without filing anything, if it meets federal law (18 U.S.C. § 2265). You may still file this form so the court has a copy.
- Filing fee: {{court.fee_foreign_order}}. If you cannot pay, ask for the fee waiver form.
- Court staff can explain the steps but cannot give legal advice.

## Notice to the respondent

A petition has been filed asking {{court.name}} to recognize an order from another court against you. If you object, you must file a written objection with the clerk within {{court.objection_days}} days after you receive this notice, and say why. Reasons may include that the other court lacked authority, that you did not get notice, or that the order has been changed or appealed. If you do not object in time, the court may recognize and enforce the order without a hearing.

## Notes for courts adapting this form

These notes are not part of the form. They explain where each part came from.

- **What the eight forms share.** All eight forms read ask for the parties' names and addresses, an oath and a signature, and leave the case number to the clerk. Seven of eight require a certified copy of the other court's order (comparison of forms listed in `based_on`, read Nov. 23, 2026).
- **Statements in section 3.** Four forms ask the petitioner to state the order is final with no appeal pending, three that no later order changed it, and two that the other court had jurisdiction (Grand Traverse Band, Nottawaseppi Huron Band, Mashantucket Pequot, Pueblo of Santa Ana, Southern Ute). The model asks all of them because a court reviewing the petition needs each one.
- **Indian Child Welfare Act check.** Grand Traverse Band lets the court refuse a custody order if "the provisions of the Indian Child Welfare Act ... were not properly followed." The box in section 3 carries that check.
- **Objection period.** Courts give 20 days (Mashantucket Pequot), 21 days (Little River Band) or 28 days (Grand Traverse Band). The model leaves it as `court.objection_days`.
- **Standard of recognition.** Courts name full faith and credit, comity, or reciprocity, and some require the other court's nation to return the favor. That choice belongs in the court's code, not the form, so the form asks only for facts the court needs under any standard.
- **Protection orders.** Federal law makes a valid protection order enforceable without registration (18 U.S.C. § 2265(d)(2)). Ute Mountain Ute's form registers protection orders for a fee, but not orders "involving domestic violence." The model tells people registration is optional.
- **Left out on purpose.** One form asks for the respondent's Social Security number. The model does not, because the clerk can ask for it privately when enforcement needs it.
