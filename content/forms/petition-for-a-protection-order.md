# Petition for a protection order

**{{court.name}}**, {{nation.name}}

Form `petition-for-a-protection-order`, version 0.1.0. A court may adopt or change this form under its own law. If your court has its own form for this, use the court's form.

## Before you start

- **If you are in danger now, call {{nation.emergency_number}} or 911.** For confidential help any time, call the StrongHearts Native Helpline at 1-844-762-8483.
- There is no fee to ask for a protection order.
- You may ask for an **emergency order** today, before the other person is told. A hearing follows, usually within {{court.emergency_hearing_days}} days, where both of you can speak.
- **Keep your address private** if the other person does not know where you live. Check the box in section 1 and leave your address off this form. Give it to the clerk separately.
- Do not hand the papers to the other person yourself. The court or police will serve them.
- A protection order from this court can be enforced in every state, territory and Tribal court in the United States (18 U.S.C. § 2265). Carry a copy with you.
- Court staff can explain the steps but cannot give legal advice. An advocate from {{nation.victim_services}} can help you fill in this form.

## Notes for courts adapting this form

These notes are not part of the form. They explain where each part came from.

- **What the ten forms share.** All ten ask for both parties' names, a narrative of what happened, physical harm or threats as a ground, an emergency or ex parte request, and a signature. Nine offer a stay-away order and an order to leave a shared home; eight ask about the relationship, children, tribal membership and incident dates, and offer a no-contact order (comparison of the forms listed in `based_on`, read Nov. 23, 2026).
- **Safety.** Five forms let the petitioner keep an address off the form (Little River Band, Puyallup, Pyramid Lake, Southern Ute, Tulalip). Southern Ute warns, "STOP: IF YOU CHECK BOX NUMBER 7, DO NOT FILL IN YOUR ADDRESS." The model puts that choice first.
- **Service.** The Southern Plains Court of Indian Offenses packet says, "Do not try and serve the defendant in person with the papers yourself." The model repeats it.
- **No fee.** Pyramid Lake ("NO FILING FEES REQUIRED") and Miami charge nothing; the Southern Plains packet states both an $85 fee and no fee. Federal grant conditions for courts often bar such fees; the model states there is no fee. This is the project's choice; check the court's funding terms.
- **Relationships.** Eight forms use a relationship checklist. Puyallup says, "You do not have to be in a family relationship," while Pyramid Lake covers domestic violence only. The model includes "not related" so the same form covers harassment and stalking; a court that limits orders to family members can delete that box.
- **Firearms.** Five forms offer firearm surrender, and Tulalip cites 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(8).
- **Full faith and credit.** Puyallup and Tulalip cite 18 U.S.C. § 2265. The model tells the petitioner the order travels.
- **Cultural remedies.** Tanana Chiefs Conference's template lists exclusion from the village, elder counseling, circle sentencing and restitution through subsistence. Those belong in the court's code and order forms; a court can add them to section 6.
- **Durations** vary (Kaw: until the next court day; Tulalip: up to 14 days; Tanana: 20 days for an emergency order). The model uses `court.emergency_hearing_days`.
- **Mutual orders.** Pyramid Lake will not grant mutual orders. The model does not offer one.
